# Guided Practice: Your First Sauerkraut

*Stage 3 of 5 — Practice (Guided)*
*Time: 20 minutes hands-on, then 3–14 days waiting*

---

You're about to turn a head of cabbage into sauerkraut using nothing but cabbage, salt, and time. That's it. No vinegar, no starter culture, no special equipment. The bacteria you need are already on the cabbage — you're just creating the conditions for them to do their work.

This is the simplest ferment that exists. If you can chop and squeeze, you can do this.

---

## What You'll Need

### Ingredients
- [ ] **1 medium head of green cabbage** (~900g / 2 lbs) — any green cabbage from the grocery store works
- [ ] **1 tablespoon fine sea salt or kosher salt** (18g if you have a scale)

> **Salt note:** Use non-iodized salt. Iodized table salt can slow fermentation because iodine inhibits bacteria. Sea salt, kosher salt, or pickling salt all work. If all you have is iodized table salt, it'll still work — it's just not ideal.

### Equipment
- [ ] **A large mixing bowl** — the biggest you have
- [ ] **A sharp knife and cutting board**
- [ ] **A clean glass jar** — a wide-mouth quart (1 liter) Mason jar is perfect, but any clean glass jar works
- [ ] **Something to weigh the cabbage down** — a smaller jar filled with water works great (it needs to fit inside the mouth of your main jar)
- [ ] **A clean cloth or loose lid** — to cover the jar while allowing gas to escape

### Nice to have but not required
- [ ] A kitchen scale (makes salt measurement more precise)
- [ ] A wooden spoon or rolling pin (for packing the jar)

---

## Before You Start

**Read this whole guide once before you touch the cabbage.** It'll take three minutes and will save you from the two most common mistakes (not enough salt, not enough packing).

Set out all your equipment. Clean your jar with hot soapy water and rinse well. You do not need to sterilize anything — the salt and the lactic acid bacteria will handle food safety. Overly sterile conditions can actually slow things down by killing the beneficial bacteria you need.

---

## Step-by-Step Instructions

### Step 1: Prepare the cabbage
**Time: 5 minutes**

1. Remove the outer 2–3 leaves of the cabbage. They're often wilted or dirty. **Save one clean outer leaf** — you'll use it later as a cover.

2. Cut the cabbage in half through the core, then cut each half in half again so you have four quarters.

3. Cut out the hard triangular core from each quarter. You can discard the core or snack on it.

4. Slice each quarter into thin ribbons, about the width of a nickel (3–4mm). Don't stress about precision — some thicker, some thinner is fine. Thinner pieces ferment faster and produce a more traditional texture.

> **Common mistake #1:** Cutting too thick. Thick chunks take much longer to release their water and may not ferment evenly. If your pieces are thicker than a pencil, cut them thinner.

5. Put all the sliced cabbage into your large mixing bowl.

---

### Step 2: Add salt and massage
**Time: 10 minutes (this is the most important step)**

1. Sprinkle the tablespoon of salt over the cabbage.

   > **How much salt?** The target is roughly 2% of the cabbage weight. For a 900g cabbage, that's 18g of salt. If you don't have a scale, 1 level tablespoon of fine sea salt is close enough. Too little salt (under 1.5%) and bad bacteria can compete. Too much salt (over 3%) and even the good bacteria will struggle. You have a wide safe range — don't overthink it.

2. **Now massage the cabbage with your hands.** Squeeze it, scrunch it, knead it like dough. You're doing two things at once:
   - Distributing the salt evenly
   - Breaking down cell walls so the cabbage releases its water

3. After 2–3 minutes of squeezing, let it sit for 5 minutes. The salt will start pulling water out of the cells through osmosis. (Remember the Selective Environment from Stage 2? This is it in action — you're creating a salty, soon-to-be-acidic environment.)

4. Squeeze and massage for another 2–3 minutes. By now, you should see a pool of liquid collecting at the bottom of the bowl. This liquid is your **brine** — cabbage juice + dissolved salt. It's critical. It will cover the cabbage in the jar and create the anaerobic (oxygen-free) environment the lactic acid bacteria need.

> **What if there isn't much liquid?** Some cabbages are drier than others, especially in winter. If after 10 minutes of salting and squeezing you don't have much brine, wait 30 minutes and come back. The salt will continue drawing out water. If it's still dry after that, you can dissolve half a teaspoon of salt in a quarter cup of water and add it. This is a last resort — usually patience is all you need.

> **What should it look like?** The cabbage should look wilted, darker, and translucent compared to when you started. It should feel wet and floppy, like coleslaw that's been sitting in dressing. There should be enough liquid that when you press the cabbage down in the bowl, the liquid rises above it.

---

### Step 3: Pack the jar
**Time: 5 minutes**

This step matters more than you think. You're creating an anaerobic environment. Air is the enemy.

1. Grab a handful of the salted cabbage and pack it tightly into your clean jar. Push it down firmly with your fist, a wooden spoon, or the end of a rolling pin.

2. Add another handful. Push it down again. You want to eliminate air pockets. Press hard — you're not going to hurt it.

3. Repeat until all the cabbage is in the jar. **Pour every drop of brine from the bowl into the jar.** That liquid is precious.

4. Press the cabbage down one final time. **The brine should rise above the level of the cabbage.** This is the single most important thing: cabbage below the liquid line ferments. Cabbage above the liquid line molds.

   > **Common mistake #2:** Not packing tightly enough. If brine doesn't cover the cabbage, pack harder. Push down with real force. The cabbage will compress more than you expect.

5. Take that outer leaf you saved. Fold or tear it so it fits inside the mouth of the jar, sitting on top of the shredded cabbage. Press it down. This acts as a "lid" that keeps the shreds submerged under the brine.

6. Place your weight on top — a small jar filled with water, a clean rock, or a ziplock bag filled with water all work. The goal is to keep constant downward pressure so the cabbage stays under the brine.

7. Cover the whole thing loosely — a cloth held on with a rubber band, a coffee filter, or just rest the jar lid on top without screwing it tight. You need to let CO2 escape (remember the Sugar Tax — the bacteria produce gas) while keeping dust and insects out.

> **How full should the jar be?** Leave at least 1 inch (2.5 cm) of headspace at the top. The cabbage will bubble and can overflow in the first few days. If your jar is too full, the brine will bubble over onto your counter. Ask me how I know.

---

### Step 4: Wait (the hard part)
**Time: 3–14 days**

1. Place the jar on a plate or in a shallow bowl (to catch any overflow) and put it somewhere at room temperature, out of direct sunlight. A kitchen counter away from the stove is perfect. The ideal temperature range is **18–24C (65–75F)**.

   - **Too cold** (below 15C / 60F): Fermentation slows dramatically. Not dangerous, just slow.
   - **Too warm** (above 27C / 80F): Fermentation races, potentially creating off-flavors or mushy texture.
   - **Room temperature in most homes is fine.** Don't overthink this.

2. **Check it once a day.** Here's what to do each day:
   - Press the weight down to make sure cabbage is still submerged
   - Look for bubbles — this is CO2 from the bacteria. Bubbles are a good sign. They usually start appearing within 24–48 hours
   - If any brine has overflowed, wipe the outside of the jar

3. **What you'll see over time:**

   | Day | What's Happening | What You'll Notice |
   |-----|-----------------|-------------------|
   | 1 | Salt is drawing out water, Leuconostoc bacteria waking up | Cabbage looks wet, brine is clear |
   | 2–3 | Leuconostoc producing CO2 and lactic acid (the Succession begins) | Tiny bubbles, brine gets cloudy — this is normal and good |
   | 4–7 | Lactobacillus taking over, pH dropping (Succession continuing) | Bubbling slows, tangy smell develops, brine is cloudy |
   | 7–14 | Acid stabilizing, complex flavors developing | Sour taste, crunchy texture, bubbling stops |

---

### Step 5: Taste and decide
**Time: 1 minute**

Starting around day 5, taste it every day or two with a clean fork.

- **Day 3–5:** Mildly tangy, still quite "cabbagey." Some people like it here.
- **Day 7–10:** Noticeably sour, more complex flavor. This is the sweet spot for most people.
- **Day 14+:** Very sour, softer texture. Traditional old-world style.

**There is no single "done" point.** It's done when it tastes good to you. The sauerkraut is safe to eat at any point once fermentation has started (you'll know by the bubbles and the sour smell).

When it tastes the way you like it: remove the weight and the leaf, screw on a proper lid, and put the jar in the fridge. Cold temperature slows fermentation to a near-halt. It will keep in the fridge for months.

## Troubleshooting: What If...

### "There's white stuff on top"
That's **kahm yeast** — a thin, white, sometimes wrinkly film on the surface. It's harmless but can create off-flavors. Skim it off with a spoon, re-submerge the cabbage, and carry on. It happens when the surface is exposed to air. Better packing and weighing prevents it.

### "It smells really bad"
Sauerkraut should smell sour, tangy, and a bit funky — like pickles, not like garbage. A mild sulfur smell in the first day or two is normal (cabbage contains sulfur compounds). If it smells rotten, putrid, or makes you recoil, something went wrong — likely not enough salt or cabbage wasn't submerged. Toss it and try again. Trust your nose.

### "There's mold (fuzzy, colored spots)"
If you see fuzzy mold (usually blue, green, black, or pink), it grew because cabbage was exposed above the brine. If the mold is only on the very top and the submerged cabbage below still smells and tastes fine, you can scrape off the mold plus an inch of cabbage below it and keep the rest. If in doubt, start over — cabbage is cheap.

### "Nothing is happening — no bubbles after 3 days"
Check the temperature. If your kitchen is cold, move the jar somewhere warmer. If it's already warm, give it another day or two. Some batches are slow starters. If nothing happens after 5 days at room temperature, the salt ratio may be too high — but this is rare with the measurements above.

### "The cabbage floats above the brine"
Push it back down. Add a heavier weight. If you've lost brine to overflow or evaporation, dissolve a quarter teaspoon of salt in half a cup of water and add it to top things off.

## What You Just Did (Connecting to the Concepts)

Take a moment to appreciate what happened here:

- **The Invisible Workforce:** You didn't add any bacteria. The Leuconostoc and Lactobacillus were already on the cabbage leaves from the field. You just gave them a reason to multiply.

- **The Selective Environment:** The salt created osmotic pressure that killed most harmful bacteria but let the salt-tolerant lactic acid bacteria thrive. You tilted the playing field.

- **The Sugar Tax:** The bacteria ate the natural sugars in the cabbage and produced lactic acid (the sour taste), CO2 (the bubbles), and trace amounts of other compounds (the complexity).

- **The Succession:** Leuconostoc started the party, dropped the pH, then Lactobacillus took over and finished the job. This is why the flavor changed over time.

- **The Safety Paradox:** Your sauerkraut, with its pH below 4.0, is now *more* resistant to pathogens than the raw cabbage was. You made something safer by letting bacteria colonize it.
