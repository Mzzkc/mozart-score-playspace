# Fermentation Troubleshooting Practice
*Stage 3 of 5 — When Things Go Sideways (and What They're Teaching You)*

---

You're going to encounter problems. Every fermenter does. The good news: most problems are fixable, and every single one teaches you something about the science underneath. This guide walks you through the five most common "oh no" moments, helps you diagnose what happened, and shows you how to fix it — or prevent it next time.

Print this page. Tape it to your fridge. You'll reach for it.

---

## How to Use This Guide

When something looks or smells wrong:

1. **Don't panic.** Most fermentation "problems" are cosmetic or easily fixed.
2. **Find the matching scenario below.**
3. **Read the diagnosis** — understanding *why* it happened matters more than the fix.
4. **Take action** — each scenario has clear steps.

---

## What Healthy Fermentation Looks Like

Before we diagnose problems, you need to know what *normal* looks like — because normal fermentation can look alarming to a beginner.

### Healthy Signs (Don't Worry About These)

- **Bubbling.** Small bubbles rising through your brine or dough. This is CO₂ — the Sugar Tax in action. Your microbes are eating and producing gas. This is exactly what you want.
- **Cloudy brine.** Your crystal-clear saltwater will turn milky and opaque within 2–3 days. This is billions of lactobacillus bacteria thriving. Cloudy = alive = good.
- **Sour smell.** A clean, sharp, vinegar-like or yogurt-like tang. This is lactic acid accumulating. If it smells like something you'd eat (even if strong), you're on track.
- **Color changes.** Vegetables may dull slightly in color — bright green cucumbers become olive-toned, red cabbage may shift toward purple-blue. Totally normal pigment chemistry.
- **White sediment at the bottom.** A thin layer of white residue settling at the bottom of your jar. These are spent yeast and bacteria cells. Harmless.
- **Vegetables floating.** Cabbage, cucumbers, and other veggies will try to float above the brine. This is normal — but you need to deal with it (see Scenario 1).

## Scenario 1: The Fuzzy Intruder (Mold)

### What You See

A fuzzy, raised growth on the surface of your ferment. It may be white, green, blue, black, or pink. The key word is **fuzzy** — it looks like what grows on forgotten bread or old cheese. It's three-dimensional, with visible filaments or a velvety texture.

It almost always appears on the *surface*, on any vegetable or matter exposed to air above the brine line.

### What Happened (The Science)

Remember The Selective Environment from your core concepts? Your brine creates conditions that favor lactobacillus and exclude almost everything else — **but only where the brine reaches.** Anything poking above the surface is exposed to oxygen, and mold is an aerobic organism. It needs air.

The mold didn't come from bad ingredients or a dirty jar. Mold spores are literally everywhere — in your air, on your hands, on your countertop. You can't avoid them. You can only deny them the conditions they need: oxygen and a neutral pH surface to colonize.

### Don't Confuse It With: Kahm Yeast

Kahm yeast is a **flat, thin, white film** on the surface of your brine. It looks like a wrinkled skin or a white, powdery sheet — not fuzzy, not raised, not colorful. Kahm yeast is harmless. It's ugly and can give a slightly yeasty off-flavor if left too long, but it won't hurt you. Skim it off and carry on.

**The test:** Fuzzy and raised = mold. Flat and filmy = kahm yeast.

### The Fix

- **Small spot of mold on the surface (brine still smells sour and clean):** Scoop off the mold and at least 2 cm of food around it. Discard that portion. If the brine below smells good — tangy, clean, sour — the rest is fine. The acidic brine environment below the surface has been protecting everything submerged.
- **Widespread mold, or mold below the brine line:** Discard the batch. If mold has penetrated into the brine, the selective environment has been compromised — likely the salt concentration was too low or the seal failed.
- **Black or pink mold:** Discard the batch regardless of size. These varieties can produce mycotoxins that don't wash away.

### Prevent It Next Time

- **Keep everything submerged.** This is the single most important rule in vegetable fermentation. Use a weight — a small plate, a zip-lock bag filled with brine, a glass fermentation weight, or even a large outer cabbage leaf pressed down.
- **Check daily for floaters.** Push anything down that's crept above the brine line.
- **Top up brine if needed.** If evaporation drops the brine level, mix a fresh batch at the same salt ratio (roughly 2–3% by weight — about 1 tablespoon of salt per 2 cups of water) and top it off.

### The Lesson

Mold teaches you that The Selective Environment has boundaries. Brine is a fortress, but only for what it touches. Above the waterline, you're in mold territory. Submersion isn't a suggestion — it's the mechanism.

## Scenario 2: The Funk (Off Smells)

### What You Smell

Something that makes you recoil. Not the clean sourness of vinegar or yogurt, but something else entirely. Common off-smells include:

- **Sulfurous / rotten eggs** — sharp, gagging, like a hot spring
- **Putrid / rotting garbage** — deep, nauseating decay
- **Nail polish remover** — sharp, chemical, acetone-like
- **Cheesy / sweaty** — like dirty gym socks, not in a good way (some ferments *should* smell a bit funky — but you'll know the difference between "funky food" and "something is wrong")

### What Happened (The Science)

This is The Succession going wrong. In healthy fermentation, beneficial lactic acid bacteria (LAB) colonize quickly and drop the pH, locking out harmful organisms. But if conditions weren't quite right at the start, the wrong microbes may have gotten a head start.

- **Sulfur / rotten egg smell:** Putrefactive bacteria (Clostridium or others) are breaking down proteins. This typically happens when salt is too low, temperature is too high, or vegetables weren't fresh. These bacteria thrive in low-acid, low-salt, warm environments — exactly the conditions The Selective Environment is designed to prevent.
- **Nail polish / acetone:** Certain yeasts can produce ethyl acetate when fermentation is too warm or too oxygen-rich. It's a sign that yeast is outcompeting bacteria.
- **Putrid smell:** General spoilage bacteria won. The ferment never acidified properly.

### The Fix

- **Mild off-smell in the first 24–48 hours:** Sometimes early fermentation smells rough before LAB take over. Wait one more day. If the smell transitions toward sour/tangy, you're fine — The Succession is correcting itself.
- **Strong putrid or sulfur smell after 48+ hours:** The wrong organisms won. Discard the batch. No amount of waiting will fix this — the microbial population has shifted away from what you want.
- **Acetone / nail polish smell:** Move to a cooler location (15–18°C / 60–65°F). If caught early, the LAB may still outcompete the yeast. If the smell persists after 2–3 more days at a cooler temperature, discard.

### Prevent It Next Time

- **Use enough salt.** For most vegetable ferments, 2–3% salt by total weight (vegetables + brine) is the sweet spot. Measure by weight, not volume — a tablespoon of fine salt weighs twice as much as a tablespoon of coarse salt.
- **Use fresh vegetables.** Older produce may already have higher populations of spoilage bacteria, giving them a head start over the LAB.
- **Keep it cool.** Room temperature (18–22°C / 65–72°F) is ideal for most vegetable ferments. Above 25°C (77°F), spoilage organisms and yeasts become more competitive.
- **Ensure anaerobic conditions.** Seal your jar or use an airlock. If you're using an open crock, make sure your weight creates a good seal.

### The Lesson

Off-smells teach you that The Selective Environment is a *balance*, not an on/off switch. Salt concentration, temperature, and oxygen each adjust the competitive landscape. When all three favor LAB, you get clean fermentation. When one or more drifts out of range, other organisms can compete — and win.

## Scenario 3: The Salt Mistake (Too Salty or Not Salty Enough)

### What You Taste or See

**Too salty:** You taste it and wince. It's aggressively, unpleasantly salty — not tangy, just salty. The ferment may also be progressing very slowly (few bubbles, slow souring) because the high salt is suppressing even the beneficial bacteria.

**Not salty enough:** Fermentation rockets forward — bubbles everywhere within hours, rapid souring, maybe soft or mushy texture in your vegetables. Or worse, you get the off-smells from Scenario 2.

### What Happened (The Science)

Salt is the primary lever of The Selective Environment. It does three things:

1. **Draws water out of vegetables** via osmosis (creating your brine).
2. **Slows microbial activity** — all microbial activity, including the good guys. More salt = slower fermentation.
3. **Selectively inhibits** organisms that can't tolerate salt. Most spoilage bacteria and molds are less salt-tolerant than lactobacillus.

The ideal salt concentration is a compromise: enough to inhibit the bad organisms and draw out liquid, but not so much that it stalls the beneficial ones.

### The Fix

**Too salty (caught early, first 1–2 days):**
- Drain off some brine and replace with plain, non-chlorinated water. Taste the brine — aim for "pleasantly salty, like the sea" (roughly 2–3.5%).
- If you used the dry-salt method (massaging salt into shredded vegetables), you can add more unsalted, shredded vegetables to absorb some salt. Pack them down into the existing brine.

**Too salty (caught late, already fermented):**
- Rinse the vegetables briefly under cold water before eating. You'll lose some of the brine's probiotic benefit, but the flavor will improve.
- Use the too-salty ferment as a condiment or seasoning ingredient rather than eating it straight. A tablespoon of overly salty sauerkraut in a soup or stew is a flavor bomb, not a mistake.

**Not salty enough (caught early):**
- Add more salt. Dissolve salt in a small amount of water and stir it into the brine. Aim for 2–3% total.
- If vegetables are already mushy or smell off, it's too late — the damage from Scenario 2 may have already occurred. Assess with your nose.

### Prevent It Next Time

- **Weigh your salt.** This is the single biggest upgrade to your fermentation practice. A kitchen scale that reads in grams costs less than a jar of artisan sauerkraut. For 1 kg of vegetables, use 20–30 grams of salt (2–3%).
- **Use non-iodized salt.** Iodine can inhibit fermentation cultures. Kosher salt, sea salt, or pickling salt all work. Avoid table salt with anti-caking agents.
- **Write down your ratios.** Keep a notebook. "1 head cabbage (850g) + 22g sea salt" is worth more than any recipe.

### The Lesson

Salt teaches you that The Selective Environment is dose-dependent. It's not "salt = good" — it's "this much salt at this temperature for this vegetable." Fermentation is quantitative. When you start weighing your salt, you cross from "hobby" to "practice."

## Scenario 4: The Silent Jar (Nothing Is Happening)

### What You See

It's been 48 hours and... nothing. No bubbles. No clouding of the brine. No sour smell developing. Your ferment looks exactly like it did when you packed the jar. Just raw vegetables sitting in salt water.

### What Happened (The Science)

The Invisible Workforce hasn't shown up for work — or more precisely, they're there but can't get started. The microbes on your vegetables are present (they're always present), but something in the environment is preventing them from multiplying.

Common causes:

1. **Too cold.** Below about 15°C (60°F), LAB activity slows dramatically. Below 10°C (50°F), it's nearly stopped. If your jar is in a cold garage, basement, or next to a drafty window in winter, this is probably the answer.

2. **Chlorinated water.** If you made a brine with tap water, the chlorine may be suppressing microbial growth. Municipal water treatment is specifically designed to kill microorganisms — and it doesn't discriminate between pathogens and your lactobacillus.

3. **Too much salt.** As covered in Scenario 3, excessive salt suppresses even beneficial bacteria. If you went over 5%, you may have created an environment too harsh for LAB to get started.

4. **Irradiated or heavily processed vegetables.** Some commercially packaged vegetables have been irradiated or treated to extend shelf life — which also means fewer viable microbes on the surface. Farm-stand and organic produce tend to carry more diverse microbial populations.

5. **You're not looking closely enough.** Some ferments, especially at cool temperatures, start very slowly. Tiny bubbles along the sides of the jar or a very slight cloudiness may be present but hard to notice.

### The Fix

- **Move it somewhere warmer.** 20–22°C (68–72°F) is the sweet spot for kickstarting fermentation. Put the jar on top of your fridge (heat rises), near (not on) a radiator, or in a warm closet. You may see activity within 12–24 hours of warming up.
- **Wait longer.** Cool-environment fermentation can take 5–7 days to show visible signs. If the brine still smells neutral (not off), give it time. Patience is a fermentation skill.
- **Check your water.** If you suspect chlorine, you have two options for next time: use filtered water, or fill a bowl with tap water and leave it uncovered for 24 hours — chlorine will off-gas. (Note: chloramine, used in some municipal systems, does *not* off-gas. A carbon filter handles it.)
- **If it's a salt issue**, dilute your brine (see Scenario 3).

### When to Worry

If after 5–7 days at room temperature you still have zero activity *and* the brine smells completely neutral — not sour, not off, just like salt water — something is genuinely wrong. Start a new batch with fresh vegetables, filtered water, and measured salt. Consider adding a tablespoon of brine from an active ferment (store-bought raw sauerkraut or kimchi brine works) as a starter to jump-start the population.

### Prevent It Next Time

- **Use fresh, preferably local or organic vegetables.** They carry more diverse natural microbes.
- **Use non-chlorinated water.** Filter or off-gas.
- **Ferment at room temperature initially.** You can move to a cooler spot *after* fermentation is visibly active to slow it down for flavor development.
- **Measure your salt** (this keeps coming up because it solves half of all problems).

### The Lesson

The Silent Jar teaches you that The Invisible Workforce is always there — you just need to create conditions where they can thrive. Temperature, water chemistry, and salt are all environmental levers. Fermentation isn't adding microbes; it's managing an ecosystem that already exists on every surface of every vegetable you've ever touched.

## Scenario 5: The Explosion (Pressure Buildup)

### What You See (or Hear)

- Your jar lid bulges outward, tight as a drum.
- Brine overflows when you crack the lid — a geyser of fizzy liquid and vegetables.
- In extreme cases: the lid pops off, the jar cracks, or you hear a startling *bang* from the kitchen.
- Kimchi in a sealed container in the fridge has oozed red liquid all over the shelf.

### What Happened (The Science)

This is The Sugar Tax making itself very literally felt. When microbes consume sugars, they produce CO₂ as a metabolic byproduct. That gas has to go somewhere.

In an open container or one with an airlock, the gas escapes continuously — you see gentle bubbling. But in a sealed container (a mason jar with the lid screwed tight, a capped bottle, a sealed plastic container), the CO₂ accumulates. Pressure builds. And builds.

Fruits and sweet vegetables (like the cabbage and radish in kimchi, or fruit-based ferments like tepache) produce more CO₂ because there's more sugar for the microbes to process.

This is the same principle behind champagne and beer carbonation — except those bottles are engineered to handle pressure. Your mason jar is not.

### The Fix

- **For a bulging but intact container:** Hold the jar over the sink, point it away from your face, and crack the lid slowly. Let the pressure release gradually. You may get overflow — that's fine. Re-seal loosely, or switch to a container with an airlock.
- **For a fridge mess (kimchi explosion):** This is cosmetic, not a safety issue. The kimchi is fine. Clean the fridge, transfer the kimchi to a larger container (leave headroom!), and burp it daily.
- **For a cracked jar:** Discard the contents if there are glass shards. The ferment itself was fine — it was just too powerful for its container.

### What "Burping" Means

Burping = opening the lid briefly to release gas, then resealing. For any active ferment in a sealed container, burp at least once daily. Over the sink. With a towel nearby.

### Prevent It Next Time

- **Use an airlock.** A fermentation airlock (costs a few dollars) lets CO₂ escape without letting oxygen in. This is the clean solution.
- **Use a loose lid.** Simply not tightening the lid all the way lets gas escape. The ferment is still protected from most airborne contamination because CO₂ is heavier than oxygen and pools above the brine.
- **Leave headroom.** Fill your jar to about 80% capacity. The remaining 20% gives gas a buffer zone before pressure becomes dangerous.
- **Burp sealed containers daily.** If you don't have an airlock and want a tight seal (for transport or fridge storage), open the lid once a day to release gas.
- **Expect more gas from sweeter ferments.** Kimchi, fruit ferments, and anything with added sugar will produce significantly more CO₂ than plain sauerkraut.

### The Lesson

The Explosion teaches you that metabolic byproducts are real, physical forces. The Sugar Tax isn't a metaphor — those microbes are producing enough gas to crack glass. This is the same chemistry that leavens your bread, carbonates your beer, and makes your sourdough rise. Fermentation is not gentle. It's alive, and alive things push back against their containers.

## Quick-Reference Troubleshooting Card

*Cut this out and tape it to your fermentation shelf.*

| Problem | Likely Cause | Quick Fix | Prevention |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Fuzzy mold on surface** | Food exposed to air above brine | Scoop off + 2cm around it; discard if widespread or black/pink | Keep everything submerged; use weights |
| **Flat white film on surface** | Kahm yeast (harmless) | Skim off, carry on | Better seal; cooler temperature |
| **Rotten / sulfur smell** | Spoilage bacteria won; salt too low or temp too high | Discard batch | Measure salt (2–3%); keep at 18–22°C |
| **Acetone / nail polish smell** | Yeast overgrowth; too warm or too much oxygen | Move to cooler spot; wait 2–3 days | Better seal; lower temperature |
| **Way too salty** | Over-salted | Dilute brine with filtered water; or rinse before eating | Weigh salt (20–30g per kg) |
| **Too fast / mushy** | Under-salted; too warm | Add salt if early; eat sooner if late | Weigh salt; cooler spot |
| **Nothing happening** | Too cold, chlorinated water, or over-salted | Move warmer; wait longer; check water source | Room temp start; filtered water |
| **Jar bulging / overflow** | CO₂ pressure buildup (normal!) | Burp over sink; switch to airlock | Use airlock; leave headroom; burp daily |
| **Soft, mushy vegetables** | Too warm, too long, or not enough salt | Eat now (safe if smells sour); shorter ferment next time | Cooler temp; adequate salt; taste daily after day 3 |

## The Three Questions

When something seems wrong, ask yourself these three questions in order:

**1. Does it smell sour (like vinegar or yogurt)?**
→ If yes, The Succession worked. Your ferment acidified. Most surface issues are cosmetic. You're probably fine.

**2. Does it smell putrid (like garbage or sulfur)?**
→ If yes, The Succession failed. Discard and start over. Investigate salt and temperature.

**3. Is the problem above or below the brine line?**
→ Above: probably mold or kahm yeast — a submersion problem, not a ferment problem.
→ Below: if it smells good, it almost certainly *is* good.

## Your Practice Exercise

**Make a small batch of sauerkraut, and keep a daily log.**

You'll need:
- 1 small head of cabbage (about 500–800g)
- Non-iodized salt (2–3% of cabbage weight — weigh it!)
- A clean jar, a weight, and optionally an airlock
- A notebook or your phone

**Each day, record:**
- What you see (bubbles? cloudiness? film? floaters?)
- What you smell (nothing? sour? funky?)
- Any action taken (pushed down floaters, burped lid, skimmed surface)
- Temperature of the room, if you have a thermometer

**After 5–7 days, taste it.** Then decide: ferment longer, or move to the fridge?

This log is your real education. Every scenario in this guide will make more sense after you've watched a jar for a week. You'll see The Succession unfold in real time — the quiet first day, the burst of bubbles on day 2–3, the slow deepening of sourness through the week.

And if something goes wrong? Good. Now you know what it looks like, and you have this guide to tell you what it means.
