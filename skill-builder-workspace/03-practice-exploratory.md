# Fermentation: Design Your Own Ferment

*Stage 3 of 5 — Exploratory Practice*
*For: A curious home cook who's eaten kimchi and sourdough but never made either*

---

## What You're Doing Here

You're not following a recipe. You're **designing a ferment from principles**.

In Stage 2, you learned five ideas that govern every ferment ever made. Now you're going to use those ideas to create something no recipe book contains — a vegetable ferment that you invented, using ingredients you chose, based on reasoning you understand.

This might feel like being tossed into the deep end. It isn't. You already know more than you think. The five concepts *are* the recipe. Everything else is just personal preference.

---

## The Framework: What Every Vegetable Ferment Needs

Before you pick a single ingredient, understand the structure. Every lacto-fermented vegetable follows the same blueprint:

### The Four Non-Negotiables

| Element | What It Does | Your Range |
|---------|-------------|-----------|
| **Vegetables** | Provide sugar (fuel for microbes) and the microbes themselves | Any fresh, raw vegetables — not cooked, not canned |
| **Salt** | Creates the selective environment — suppresses pathogens, favors Lactobacillus | 2-5% by weight of your total vegetable mass |
| **Anaerobic conditions** | Keeps oxygen-loving mold and spoilage bacteria out | Vegetables must stay *below* liquid level |
| **Time** | Allows microbial succession to unfold | Minimum 3 days, up to several weeks |

That's it. No vinegar. No starter culture. No special equipment. Vegetables, salt, no air, patience.

### The Variables You Control

Here's where your design begins. These are the knobs you can turn:

**Salt percentage (2-5%)**
- Lower salt (2%) = faster fermentation, softer texture, milder tang
- Higher salt (3-5%) = slower fermentation, crunchier texture, more gradual flavor development
- Think of salt as the speed dial. Less salt lets more organisms work faster. More salt slows everything down and keeps things crunchy.

**Temperature**
- Cooler (60-68F / 15-20C) = slower, more complex flavors, crunchier
- Warmer (68-75F / 20-24C) = faster, tangier sooner, softer over time
- Above 75F (24C), things move fast — which isn't bad, but you'll want to check more often

**Ingredient selection**
- Dense, crunchy vegetables (cabbage, carrots, radishes, turnips) hold up well over long ferments
- Softer vegetables (zucchini, tomatoes, peppers) break down faster — consider shorter ferment times
- Alliums (garlic, onion) add sulfur compounds that interact beautifully with lactic acid
- Spices (ginger, turmeric, chili, peppercorns, caraway) don't ferment themselves but infuse flavor into the brine

**Technique: dry-salt vs. brine**
- **Dry-salt** — Toss cut vegetables with salt, massage or press until they release their own liquid. Best for high-water vegetables like cabbage. This is how sauerkraut and kimchi are traditionally made.
- **Brine** — Dissolve salt in water and pour over vegetables. Best for whole or large-cut vegetables that won't release enough liquid on their own (like carrot sticks, whole peppers, cauliflower florets).

**Time**
- 3-5 days: bright, lightly tangy, still fresh-tasting
- 1-2 weeks: properly sour, more depth, good backbone
- 3-4 weeks: deep, funky, complex — an acquired taste for some, a revelation for others

## Your Design Process

### Step 1: Choose Your Base Vegetable

Pick one vegetable to be the backbone of your ferment — the thing there's the most of. Good first choices:

- **Cabbage** (any color) — the classic. Releases tons of liquid, very forgiving.
- **Carrots** — sweet, which gives the microbes plenty to eat. Beautiful color.
- **Radishes** — peppery when raw, they mellow into something entirely different when fermented.
- **Cucumbers** — the original fermented vegetable. Use small, firm ones (not waxy grocery store kind if you can help it).

### Step 2: Choose 2+ Supporting Ingredients

This is where your ferment becomes *yours*. Add at least two more elements. Think about:

- **Another vegetable** for texture contrast (shredded carrot in cabbage, sliced radish with cucumber)
- **An allium** for depth (garlic cloves, thinly sliced onion, sliced scallion)
- **A spice or aromatic** for character (fresh ginger, chili flakes, whole peppercorns, cumin seed, dill, caraway, turmeric)

There's no wrong combination. Seriously. The worst that happens is you don't love the flavor — and you'll still learn everything you need to learn.

### Step 3: Decide Your Salt Strategy

Weigh your prepared vegetables (cut, sliced, shredded — however you're using them). Then calculate your salt:

```
Vegetable weight (grams) x salt percentage = salt needed (grams)

Example: 500g vegetables x 0.03 (3%) = 15g salt
```

**Use non-iodized salt.** Iodine can inhibit the bacteria you're trying to cultivate. Sea salt, kosher salt, pickling salt — all fine. Table salt with iodine — avoid it.

If you don't have a kitchen scale: 1 tablespoon of fine sea salt is roughly 18g. For 500g of vegetables at 3%, you need a little less than 1 tablespoon.

### Step 4: Choose Your Vessel

You need a container that can be covered but doesn't need to be airtight (CO2 needs to escape). Options:

- **A mason jar** — the most common choice. Leave the lid loose, or use a jar with a rubber gasket that lets gas burp out.
- **A ceramic crock** — traditional, beautiful, not required.
- **Any glass or food-safe ceramic container** with a plate or lid that sits on top.

Avoid: metal containers (acid reacts with metal), plastic that isn't food-grade.

### Step 5: Assemble and Set the Conditions

**If dry-salting:**
1. Combine your cut vegetables and salt in a large bowl.
2. Massage, squeeze, and press the vegetables for 5-10 minutes. You want them to release liquid — you'll see the bowl getting wet. Taste a piece. It should taste pleasantly salty, like a well-seasoned salad.
3. Pack everything tightly into your jar, pressing down hard. The liquid should rise above the vegetables. If it doesn't, wait 30 minutes and press again — more liquid will come.
4. Place a weight on top to keep everything submerged. A small zip-lock bag filled with brine works well. So does a smaller jar filled with water.

**If brining:**
1. Dissolve your salt in water (use the same percentage — 30g salt per 1 liter of water for 3%).
2. Pack your vegetables into the jar.
3. Pour the brine over until everything is covered by at least 1cm (half an inch) of liquid.
4. Weight it down.

### Step 6: Place It and Start Observing

Put your jar somewhere you'll see it every day. Not hidden in a cupboard — on the counter, where it becomes part of your daily routine. Room temperature (65-75F / 18-24C).

**Cover loosely.** A cloth secured with a rubber band, a lid set on top without tightening, or a jar with an airlock if you have one. The goal: keep debris and flies out, let CO2 escape.

Now the microbes go to work. Your job shifts from maker to observer.

## What to Watch For (Your Daily Check)

This is where the five concepts come alive. Each day, spend two minutes looking at your ferment and noting what you see:

**Bubbles** (The Sugar Tax in action)
- Small bubbles forming along the sides or rising to the surface = fermentation is happening. CO2 is being produced as microbes consume sugar.
- Lots of vigorous bubbling in days 1-3 = *Leuconostoc* bacteria are dominant (remember Concept 4 — The Succession). This is Phase 1.
- Bubbling slowing down after day 3-5 = *Lactobacillus* is taking over. The relay race is progressing.

**Cloudiness** (The Invisible Workforce multiplying)
- Clear brine becoming cloudy = billions of bacteria multiplying. This is a *good* sign. Don't panic. Cloudy brine is the hallmark of a healthy lacto-ferment.

**Smell** (The Safety Paradox as your guide)
- Sour, tangy, briny = good. This is lactic acid. Your ferment is protecting itself.
- Funky, earthy, a bit sulfurous (especially with cabbage or garlic) = normal. Give it a day.
- Foul, putrid, rotten = something went wrong with the selective environment. Not enough salt, or vegetables weren't submerged. Discard and start again. This is rare if you followed the framework.

**Color changes**
- Brine turning from clear to milky/cloudy = normal and expected.
- Vegetables losing some brightness = normal. Pigments shift in acidic environments.
- Pink, red, or purple brine from beets or red cabbage = beautiful and normal.

**Surface activity**
- A thin white film on the surface (kahm yeast) = harmless but unappealing. Skim it off. It means oxygen got to the surface — press your vegetables back down below the brine.
- Fuzzy, colorful mold (green, black, pink) = usually only on surfaces exposed to air. Remove the moldy piece, check if everything below the brine smells fine. If the submerged vegetables smell sour and clean, they're still good.

## When Is It Done?

It's done **when you say it is**. There's no fixed endpoint. Here's how to decide:

**Start tasting on day 3.** Use a clean fork. Pull a piece from below the surface. Taste it.

- Day 3-4: Mildly tangy, still tastes a lot like fresh vegetables. If you like this — it's done.
- Day 7: Noticeably sour, the raw edge is gone. Most people find this a satisfying stage.
- Day 14: Properly fermented. Deep tang, complex flavor, the vegetables taste like something *new*.
- Day 21+: For the adventurous. Funky, sour, deeply flavored. Think of the difference between a young wine and an aged one.

When you reach a flavor you enjoy, move the jar to the refrigerator. Cold temperature dramatically slows fermentation (remember — temperature is a selective pressure). Your ferment will continue to change in the fridge, but very slowly. It'll keep for months.

## Experiments to Try

Once you've made your first ferment, you have a baseline. Now you can start playing scientist. Each of these experiments isolates one variable:

**Experiment 1: The Salt Spectrum**
Make two identical jars — same vegetables, same cut, same temperature. Change only the salt: one at 2%, one at 4%. Taste them at the same intervals. Notice how salt changes speed, texture, and flavor development.

**Experiment 2: The Temperature Split**
Same vegetables, same salt, same jar size. Put one in a cool spot (closet, basement) and one in a warm spot (on top of the fridge, near the stove). Taste both at day 3, 5, 7, and 10.

**Experiment 3: The Time Series**
Make one large batch. On days 3, 7, 14, and 21, move a portion to a separate jar in the fridge. At the end, taste all four side by side. You're tasting the *succession* — different microbial communities at different stages.

**Experiment 4: Whole vs. Shredded**
Take the same vegetable (carrots are great for this). Shred half, cut the other half into sticks. Ferment both with the same salt percentage. The shredded batch will ferment faster (more surface area = more access for microbes), the sticks will stay crunchier. Same principles, different expression.

## Troubleshooting From Principles

When something seems off, don't search the internet. First, ask yourself: **which of the five concepts is involved?**

| What You See | Which Concept | What Likely Happened | What to Do |
|---|---|---|---|
| No bubbles after 48 hours | Sugar Tax (3) + Workforce (1) | Too much salt slowing things down, or too cold | Wait another day. If still nothing at 72 hours, check if salt was iodized. |
| Soft, mushy vegetables | Selective Environment (2) | Not enough salt, or too warm | Taste. If sour and safe-smelling, eat it soon. Next time, increase salt or lower temperature. |
| Mold on surface | Selective Environment (2) | Vegetables exposed to air (oxygen broke the anaerobic condition) | Remove mold and any vegetable it touched. Push everything below brine. Add more brine if needed. |
| Slimy brine | Succession (4) | Certain *Leuconostoc* strains produce a polysaccharide slime early on. Harmless. | Usually resolves as *Lactobacillus* takes over. Taste at day 5-7. Often the texture normalizes. |
| Too salty | Selective Environment (2) | Used more than needed for your taste | Still safe. Let it ferment longer — the sourness will balance the salt. Next time, use a lower percentage. |
| Tastes flat, not tangy | Sugar Tax (3) + Time | Not enough time, or too cold, or vegetables were low in sugar | Give it more time. Move to a slightly warmer spot. |

## A Note on Confidence

You might be staring at your ingredients right now thinking: "But what if I do it wrong?"

Here's the truth about vegetable fermentation — the window of success is *enormous*. Humans have been doing this for thousands of years without kitchen scales, pH meters, or temperature-controlled rooms. They did it in clay pots in the ground. They did it on ships. They did it in apartments without air conditioning.

If you use enough salt (2-5%), keep your vegetables under liquid, and let time do its work, you will get a fermented food. It might not be exactly what you envisioned. It might be better. The point of this first ferment isn't perfection — it's *observation*. You're learning what your microbes do when you give them the right conditions.

The journal matters more than the recipe. Write down what you did. Write down what happened. That's how every great fermenter learned — not from a book, but from a jar on the counter and a notebook beside it.

Go make something.
