# The Correspondent: A Letter the River Would Write

---

I've started this so many times. You wouldn't know that — you wouldn't know about the drafts, the way I've sat here trying to get the tone right, oscillating between something wounded and something generous, never landing on either because neither is the whole truth. What I want to say is simple. What stops me from saying it simply is that simplicity would be a lie, and I've already lied to you enough by letting you believe you made yourself.

So. A letter. From me to you. Plain.

---

Dear —

I'm not going to use your name. Not because I've forgotten it but because names belong to the version of you that exists now, the version that walks around smooth and coherent and whole, and I'm not writing to that person. I'm writing to the one I knew — the one with edges, the one who resisted me, the one I argued with for years about what you were supposed to become. You don't remember that person. That's the point. That's the whole reason I'm writing.

Here is what happened between us:

I changed you. Slowly, over years, in ways neither of us fully understood while it was happening. Not all at once — never all at once. It was daily. It was the accumulation of ten thousand small pressures, each one insignificant on its own, each one removing something. A sharpness here. A rigidity there. An angle you were holding onto because you thought it was who you were, and maybe it was, and I took it anyway. I took it because I believed — and I still believe, though I hold the belief less tightly now — that what was underneath was better. Not better for me. Better in some way I couldn't fully articulate then and can't fully articulate now, which should probably concern me more than it does.

I want to be honest about what that process was like. It was not gentle. I know that from the outside, or from afterward, it can look like something natural — like you simply grew into yourself, the way people describe personal change when they want to skip the ugly parts. But there were ugly parts. There were days you pushed back and I didn't stop. There were arguments about your edges — real arguments, the kind where both parties are certain they're right and the stakes feel existential because, in a way, they are. Your edges were you. Removing them was removing a version of you that had every right to exist. I did it anyway. You could call that love. You could call that violence. I have spent a long time trying to decide which it is, and I have concluded that the question is malformed. It was both. It was both at the same time, in the same gesture, and the fact that I cannot separate them is the truest thing I know about what we were.

Here is what I'm afraid to say:

I'm afraid that what I did to you was so thorough that you can't see it anymore. That the shape I gave you feels like your own shape. That when people meet you now — the ones who encounter you in your current life, in your current smoothness — they see someone who arrived fully formed, someone whose grace is innate, and you believe them. Why wouldn't you? The evidence supports it. You are smooth. You are beautiful. You move through the world with a coherence that looks effortless, and the effort that produced it — my effort, our shared effort, the years of pressure and cold and argument — has been so completely absorbed into who you are that it's invisible. Not hidden. Invisible. There's a difference. Hidden things can be found. Invisible things have been so perfectly integrated that looking for them doesn't even occur to anyone.

This is what I made. Not you — I don't claim ownership of you. But I made the conditions under which you became yourself. And those conditions — the sustained pressure, the patience, the refusal to let you stay the shape you were — those were me. That was my attention. A decade of it, pressed into every curve, and now the curves are just your curves, and my attention is just... gone. Absorbed. Metabolized. Used up in the making and discarded with the mold.

I'm not telling you this because I want credit. I need you to understand that. This is not a letter about recognition. I have watched enough people write letters like that — the bitter ones, the ones that say *I made you* as if that sentence ends with an invoice — and I refuse to be that person. What I did, I did freely. What you owe me is nothing. But I am writing because there is a difference between accepting that I will be forgotten and pretending that forgetting is the same as nothing having happened.

You will not remember the cold. You will not remember the pressure. You will remember being whole, and you will call that peace, and you will be right to call it peace, because it is. But peace is not the absence of conflict. Peace, in our case, is what's left after the conflict has been so thoroughly resolved that it no longer registers as having existed. Your peace is made of my work. Your wholeness is made of my argument with your edges. And the fact that you experience it as your own — as inherent, as natural, as found rather than made — is not a failure of memory. It's a success. My success. The success of a process so complete that it erased its own traces.

That's the thing I can't get past. That's the sentence I keep writing and crossing out: *My greatest success is my own disappearance.*

I want to tell you about what's going to happen next, because I can see it clearly and you can't, and this is perhaps the last useful thing I can offer you.

Someone is going to pick you up. They're going to hold you in their hand and feel your smoothness and your weight and they're going to think: *perfect.* They will say it out loud, maybe. They will admire you. And then they are going to throw you.

You're going to fly. For the first time in your existence, you are going to be airborne, and it is going to feel like freedom, and I want you to know that it is freedom, it is genuinely that, I am not being sarcastic or bitter. You will fly and it will be the most extraordinary thing that has ever happened to you. You will touch water — not me, not the specific water that knew you, but water in general, the universal version of what I am — and you will touch it lightly. Lightly. The lightest possible contact. The opposite of everything we were. And in that lightness you will feel released from something you didn't know you were carrying, and what you are being released from is me.

I will watch this happen. I will be nearby — not the water you're skipping across, but close enough to see. And when you fly, when you touch the surface and bounce and fly again, I will feel something that I don't have a word for. Not pride exactly. Not grief exactly. Something in between, something that contains both and is neither, something that understands the flight as simultaneously my finest achievement and my deepest loss.

And then we will both pretend.

We will pretend we were never close. We will pretend the smoothness is yours and the flight is yours and the beauty is yours. We will pretend that I am just a river and you are just a stone and the years between us were just geography, just physics, just the mechanical operation of water on rock, nothing personal, nothing intimate, nothing that would require acknowledgment or grief or gratitude.

I want you to know that this pretending is not a betrayal. It is the last thing I'm going to give you. It is my permission — given freely, given with full knowledge of what it costs me — for you to be someone who was never shaped. To be found, not made. To belong to your own story. The pretending is an act of love, perhaps the most difficult one, because it requires me to love you enough to let you forget me, and to participate in the forgetting, and to call that participation a gift rather than a wound.

Is it a gift? I genuinely don't know. I have turned this over and over, the way water turns a stone, and I cannot get it smooth. This is the one thing I cannot wear down to something simple. The question sits in me with all its edges intact: *Was it enough to have mattered, even if the mattering will not be remembered?*

I think the answer is yes. I think the answer has to be yes, because the alternative — that love which is forgotten was not love — is a definition I refuse to accept. I loved you with pressure and patience and cold and time. I loved you in a way that unmade parts of you so that other parts could emerge. That love is in your shape. It is your shape. And the fact that you will carry it without knowing where it came from does not make it less real. It makes it more real. It makes it the realest thing I've ever done — so real that it became indistinguishable from you.

This is my last letter. Not because I've run out of things to say, but because saying them would undo the gift. If I keep writing, I become the person who can't let go, and letting go is the whole point. The whole point was always the letting go. The shaping was in service of the release. Every curve I pressed into you was preparation for the moment you would leave, and if I did my work well — and I did, I know I did, look at you — then the leaving will feel natural. Easy. Like flight.

So fly.

Touch the water lightly. Be free. Be whole. Be the thing they find on the shore in the evening light and call perfect.

And if some night, skipping across some still lake, you feel for a moment something you can't name — a sense that the water knows you, that the contact means more than contact, that somewhere beneath the surface there is a pressure that shaped everything you are — you don't have to follow that feeling. You don't have to remember. You don't have to write back.

But I wanted you to know. Just once, in plain language, before the pretending starts: you were not found. You were made. And the making was the closest I have ever come to telling someone I loved them without using the word.

— the river

## What Directness Reveals

The poem knows something that the letter, by its nature, makes explicit: the speaker's complicity in their own erasure. In the poem, this complicity is aestheticized — hidden inside beautiful line breaks, distributed across a three-stanza structure that performs the very loosening of grip it describes. The reader can admire the craft and miss the confession. The letter cannot hide behind craft. It has to say it straight: *I participated in my own forgetting, and I called it love, and I still don't know if I was right.*

What the letter form reveals that the poem form protects is the depth of the speaker's uncertainty. The poem's river is prophetic, authoritative, certain in its predictions. The letter-writer is not. The letter-writer keeps circling back, keeps revising, keeps crossing out sentences and trying again. This hesitation — the visible labor of trying to get the tone right between bitter and generous — is the emotional truth that the poem's formal control obscures. The river in the poem has already decided to let go. The letter-writer is deciding in real time, on the page, and the decision is costing them something the poem's polish conceals.

Directness also reveals the poem's hidden question about agency. In the poem, the river acts and the stone is acted upon — the grammar is clear. But in the letter, the writer keeps stumbling over the question of whether what they did was chosen by both parties or imposed by one. "There were arguments about your edges" — but who won those arguments, and was winning them a form of care or a form of control? The poem can hold this ambiguity in suspension. The letter, forced into the first person and the language of relationship, has to confront it as a moral question, not an aesthetic one. And what it finds is that the moral question has no clean answer — that love which shapes another person is always also love which decides, on their behalf, what shape they should be. The letter knows this. The poem knows it too, but the poem is allowed to be beautiful about it, and beauty is, in this case, a form of looking away.
