# The Painter's Translation: What the River Knows

---

## The Work

**Title:** *What Remains After Holding*

**Medium:** Oil on linen, with areas of exposed ground and selective glazing

**Dimensions:** 152 x 91 cm (60 x 36 inches) — vertical, roughly the proportions of a standing figure or a river stone held in the hand, scaled to the body of the viewer

**Surface preparation:** The linen is primed with a warm ground of raw sienna and lead white, applied unevenly so the weave shows through in places. This ground is the painting's secret: it is always present beneath the image, visible only where the paint thins or where I have deliberately left it exposed. It is the color of a riverbed. It is the color of patience.

---

## Composition

The painting is divided into three zones, not by hard lines but by shifts in density, opacity, and temperature — the way a river changes character as it moves downstream.

**The lower third** is the heaviest. Here, the paint is built up in thick, worked layers — not impasto for drama, but the slow accretion of repeated glazes, each one altering the color beneath it without obliterating it. The palette in this region is a compressed range of umbers, raw and burnt, mixed with Prussian blue and a cold grey made from ivory black and white. The surface has been worked and reworked: painted, partially scraped back, repainted. In places, earlier layers show through the top surface as ghost-shapes — suggestions of harder, more angular forms beneath the final smooth contours. If you look closely — and the painting demands that you look closely — you can see that this lower zone contains the remnants of edges that were there and are no longer. Corners that were softened. Planes that were rounded. The evidence of argument.

The forms in this zone are not quite figurative, not quite abstract. They suggest the weight of stones in water — dense, mineral, slightly luminous from within — but they refuse to resolve into any single recognizable shape. There is a dominant form, roughly ovoid, occupying the left-center of this zone. Its surface appears almost polished: the glazes have been built up to a depth that produces a dark glow, the way a wet stone catches light. This is the stone. But it does not look like a stone. It looks like a body that has been held for so long it has taken the shape of the holding.

Around this central form, the paint thins. The strokes become visible — long, horizontal, repetitive, each one slightly different from the last but following the same trajectory. These are the river's strokes. They do not radiate outward from the stone; they move *past* it, flowing left to right across the canvas. The stone is not the origin of the current. The stone is what the current encounters.

**The middle third** is where the painting opens. The thick, worked surface of the lower zone gives way to thinner paint, broader strokes, more visible ground. The palette shifts: the cold blues and umbers warm into something ambiguous — not warm, exactly, but less compressed. Muted ochres appear. A dusty violet made from the blue-grey of the lower zone mixed with a thin red oxide. The forms here are less defined. The stone's shape is still present as a suggestion — a curvature, a density — but it is diffusing. The edges are no longer contested; they are dissolving.

In this middle zone, I have left a passage of nearly bare linen — a gap in the paint approximately 20 cm wide, running vertically through the center of the composition. The warm ground shows through. This gap is the painting's hinge. It is where the past and the future do not quite touch. It is the present tense that cannot be occupied. On either side of it, the paint continues — but differently. On the left, it carries the weight and memory of the lower zone. On the right, it is lighter, more provisional, as if the paint itself is uncertain whether it has the right to be there.

**The upper third** is the most spare. The paint is thin, almost translucent, applied in quick touches that sit on the surface without sinking into the weave. The palette here is reduced to three colors: a pale grey-blue (titanium white, a breath of cerulean, a trace of raw umber to keep it from going cold), a warmer cream (Naples yellow and white), and — in a single passage near the upper right — a clear, true grey that is neither warm nor cold, neither light nor dark. A grey of absolute neutrality. The grey of forgetting.

In this upper zone, there are marks that could be read as a stone skipping — small, discrete touches of thicker paint, three or four of them, moving rightward and upward across the thinning surface, each one slightly smaller and lighter than the last. They do not describe a stone. They describe a rhythm: contact, lift, contact, lift, contact — and then nothing. The last mark is barely there. After it, the canvas breathes. The linen ground shows through a final haze of diluted white, and then there is only the edge of the stretcher, and the wall behind it, and the room you are standing in.

## Light

The light in this painting does not come from a single source. In the lower third, it is internal — the luminosity of layered glazes, light trapped between translucent films of paint and bounced back through them, the way light behaves inside deep water. It is the light of sustained attention, of years spent looking at something from inches away. It is intimate, enclosed, almost private.

In the middle third, the light shifts. It begins to come from outside the painting — from the left, raking across the surface at a low angle, catching the texture of the linen where the paint thins. This is the light of evening. The light of the banks. The light in which the walkers appear, though the walkers are not shown. This exterior light does not penetrate the lower zone; it slides across its surface without entering. It illuminates the painting's skin without reaching its interior. It sees the beauty. It does not see the making.

In the upper third, the light is ambient and directionless. It could be the light of morning or the light of open sky reflected on still water. It is the light in which things are released. It has no warmth and no coldness. It makes no argument. It simply is — and in its neutrality, it enacts the agreed-upon fiction of distance.

## Texture

The painting's surface is its argument. Where the poem uses time, the painting uses layers. Where the poem uses tense, the painting uses transparency and opacity.

Run your hand across the lower third (you cannot, but imagine): you would feel ridges, valleys, the topography of revision. Scrape-marks where a palette knife removed paint that was wrong. Smooth passages where the glaze has filled in the texture of the weave. In places, the surface is almost glassy — and in those places, you would see your own reflection dimly, as in a dark mirror. The stone the river made is, in part, a mirror. What the walkers see when they say *perfect* is partly themselves.

In the middle third, the texture flattens. The canvas asserts itself. The weave of the linen becomes part of the image, not merely the substrate. This is the moment where the support structure stops being hidden — where what holds the painting up becomes visible through the thinning skin of paint. It is the poem's "the way a word remembers only / its latest meaning" — the surface meaning showing through, the deeper ones submerged but structurally present.

In the upper third, the texture is almost entirely the linen itself, with paint sitting lightly on its peaks, not filling its valleys. The marks here are dry-brushed — the bristle tracks visible, the strokes rapid and sure. They do not labor. They do not press. They touch lightly, lightly.

## What Draws the Eye

The eye enters the painting at the dense, glowing ovoid in the lower left — the stone-shape — because it is the most resolved form, the point of highest contrast and deepest color. It pulls you in and holds you, the way gravity holds a stone in a riverbed. You spend time there. You see the layers. You may begin to notice the ghost-edges beneath the smooth surface.

Then the gap in the middle zone — that bare passage of warm linen — catches you. It is unexpected, almost uncomfortable. A painting is not supposed to show its ground so nakedly. It feels like a wound, or a confession, or simply an absence where you expected continuity. Your eye moves upward through it, drawn by the openness after the density below.

And then the skipping marks in the upper third. Small, joyful, brief. They move your eye rightward and upward toward the edge of the canvas. The last mark is so faint you are not sure it is there. After it: nothing. The painting ends. You are left looking at a wall.

This trajectory — from depth to surface, from held to released, from looking in to looking through — is the painting's narrative. It does not tell a story. It moves you through an experience of letting go.

---

## What Recedes

What recedes is what the poem calls "the slow dark years of my attention." The lower zone of the painting is, technically, the most accomplished passage — the most layered, the most carefully built, the most time-intensive. But it is also the darkest, the least immediately legible, the zone you must work to see. The painting has been constructed so that its most effortful passage is also its most hidden. The labor recedes behind its own results.

The horizontal strokes of the river-current — those patient, repetitive marks that flow past the stone — are painted in colors so close to the ground that they are almost invisible. You might not see them at all on first viewing. You might think the lower zone is simply a dark shape on a dark field. But if you stand with the painting for the time it asks of you, they emerge: the evidence of sustained movement, of years of pressure, of a current that never stopped.

This near-invisibility is the point. The river's work is legible only to those who look long enough to see it — and the painting knows that most viewers will not. Most will see the glowing stone-shape, admire the spare beauty of the upper zone, and move on. They will say: *beautiful painting.* As if it chose this shape.

## Where the Viewer Stands

Close. The painting's vertical format and human-scaled dimensions place the viewer in a relationship of proximity. You are not surveying a landscape; you are standing before something approximately your own size, looking into it the way you look into a face. The lower third is at your waist and hip — the body's center of gravity. The gap is at your sternum. The skipping marks are at your eye level. The painting maps onto your body, and in doing so, it asks you to feel the movement from weight to weightlessness as a physical experience: the heaviness in your gut, the opening at your chest, the release at your eyes.

You are not the river. You are not the stone. You are the one who walks the banks in evening light and picks something up and says *perfect.* The painting has made you the third party — the one who sees the result but not the process. And then, through its layered surface and its deliberate showing of its own ground, it gives you the chance to see deeper. To notice the worked-over passages. To find the ghost-edges. To understand that what you are holding was made, not found.

Whether you take that chance is not the painting's concern. It has made the evidence available. What you do with it is between you and your attention.

---

## What the Painting Knows

Painting knows something about transformation that language cannot articulate, because painting *is* transformation — it is the visible record of material being changed by sustained pressure over time. Every painting is, in this sense, a stone the painter has worn smooth.

What this painting reveals about the poem is the spatiality of forgetting. The poem moves through time — past, future, release — but the painting holds all three simultaneously. In a single glance, you can see the deep-worked lower zone and the spare upper zone and the gap between them. You can see the labor and the forgetting and the absence where the present should be, all at once. Language must sequence these; painting can stack them.

And in that stacking, something becomes visible that the poem can only imply: the forgetting is not a separate event from the making. They occupy the same surface. The thin upper paint sits directly on top of the thick lower paint, separated by nothing but a few millimeters of oil and pigment. The stone's forgetting is *physically continuous* with the river's labor. There is no gap between them — or rather, the gap (that bare linen passage in the middle) is itself a material fact, a place where nothing was put, where the painter chose absence.

The poem asks: *does love survive its own success?* The painting answers — not in words, but in the fact of its own surface: the love is the surface. It does not survive its success or fail to survive it. It *becomes* the success. It becomes the smooth glaze, the luminous depth, the beauty that people call inherent. The love does not disappear. It becomes invisible. And invisible is not the same as gone.

A viewer who looks long enough will find it. Not everyone will look long enough. The river knows this. The painting knows this. Neither of them minds. The evidence is there for anyone who brings enough time to it — which is, after all, what the river brought to the stone. Time, and pressure, and the willingness to be forgotten in exchange for the slow perfection of something that will outlast you, glowing faintly from within, carrying your work in its every curve, calling it nature, calling it peace, calling it its own.
