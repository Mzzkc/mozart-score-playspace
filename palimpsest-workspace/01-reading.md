# Close Reading: What the River Knows

---

## The Surface

A river speaks to a stone. That sentence alone carries everything this poem is doing, but we need to stay with it before it unfolds.

The river has shaped the stone — worn it smooth over years — and now addresses it directly, in second person, with something between tenderness and accusation. The poem moves in three stanzas that map onto three temporal positions: the past (what I did to you), the future (what you will forget), and the moment of release (when you leave me). Each stanza shifts the ground beneath us slightly, so that by the end we are standing somewhere we didn't expect.

In the first stanza, the river speaks as maker. "I wore you smooth and you call it beauty." The opening line is a complaint, but a strange one — not angry, exactly. More like the exhaustion of someone whose labor has been misattributed. The river shaped the stone through pressure and time, and the stone — or those who find it — reads that shaping as inherent quality. "As if you chose this shape." The river is not upset that the stone is beautiful. It is upset that the stone's beauty has been divorced from the relationship that produced it.

In the second stanza, the river becomes prophet. It predicts what the stone will and will not remember. The tense shifts from past ("I wore," "I pressed") to future ("You will not remember"). This is where the poem's emotional center lives — in the gap between what happened and what will be retained. The river knows it will be forgotten. Not dramatically forgotten, not betrayed, but simply edited out of the story the way a word loses its etymological history. The stone will remember "being whole" and call that peace, which is perhaps the poem's most devastating move: reframing the river's long work of erosion as the stone's experience of completeness.

In the third stanza, we arrive at the present — or rather, at the near future that feels like a present tense. Someone will skip the stone across a lake. The stone will fly. And in that flight, both river and stone will pretend they were never intimate. The word "pretend" is doing enormous work here. It means both parties know the truth. Forgetting, it turns out, is not passive — it is a performance they agree to.

## The Structure

The poem is built in three stanzas of decreasing certainty. The first is declarative — "I wore you smooth." The second is predictive — "You will not remember." The third is conditional, speculative, tinged with a gentleness that wasn't there before — "we'll both pretend." This movement from assertion to prediction to mutual fiction is the poem's architecture, and it mirrors the loosening of the river's grip on the stone.

The line breaks are doing specific, deliberate work. Consider:

> I pressed a decade into every curve
> and you present yourself as something found,
> not something made.

The enjambment across "found, / not something made" forces a pause between the two identities — found and made — that the poem holds in tension. The line break is itself a small act of shaping: it presses us forward and then redirects. We think we know where the sentence is going ("something found") and then it turns ("not something made"), and in that turn we feel the river's frustration at being uncredited.

The poem's most important structural feature is its shifts in address. The river speaks to the stone directly ("you"), but the relationship keeps being triangulated through others. "They pick you up — / the ones who walk my banks in evening light — / and say: *perfect.*" The italicized *perfect* is the voice of a third party, and its intrusion into the river-stone dialogue changes the poem's stakes. The river is not only unacknowledged by the stone; it is invisible to everyone. The people who find the stone see a finished object, not a process. They see a noun, not a verb.

The parenthetical quality of "the ones who walk my banks in evening light" is worth noting. It's almost dismissive — these people are incidental, a scenic detail in someone else's story. The river doesn't resent them exactly, but it doesn't grant them much importance either. They are spectators who mistake the end of a story for its entirety.

Then there is the tense architecture. Stanza one: past tense ("wore," "pressed") shading into present ("present yourself," "pick you up"). Stanza two: future tense throughout ("will not remember," "will remember," "you'll remember"). Stanza three: future tense again but softer, more hypothetical ("will fly," "will fly," "we'll both pretend"). The movement from past to future without lingering in the present enacts the poem's central loss — the present is exactly what cannot be held. The river and stone share a present tense only in the act of separation.

The simile in stanza two — "the way a word remembers only / its latest meaning, not the long / erosion of the ones it used to carry" — is the poem's most explicit moment of self-reflection. It names its own method. This poem is itself a word that carries eroded meanings: "smooth" carries violence, "beauty" carries labor, "peace" carries forgetting, "fly" carries loss. The simile is not ornamental. It is the poem telling us how to read it.

And the final lines:

> touching the water that unmade you
> lightly, lightly — and for a moment
> we'll both pretend
> we were never this close.

"Lightly, lightly" — the repetition is rhythmically perfect. A skipped stone touches the water briefly, repeatedly, each contact lighter than the last. But "the water that unmade you" is the river's own body, or at least its kin. The stone, in its moment of flight, touches water again — the very element that shaped it — but now the contact is glancing, casual, superficial. The opposite of what it was. The long pressure of years replaced by the briefest possible touch. And the river watches this and agrees to pretend it doesn't matter.

## The Depths

This poem is the shape of a particular experience: the experience of having done invisible, transformative work on someone and then watching them carry the results of that work into a life that doesn't include you. It is not exactly about romantic love, though it encompasses that. It is not exactly about parenthood, though it encompasses that too. It is about any relationship where one party's sustained attention *becomes* the other party's identity — and then the attention itself is forgotten while the identity persists.

The river's position is extraordinary. It is both the artist and the medium. It did the shaping, and it is the substance through which the shaping happened. When the stone is skipped across the lake, it touches water — but not *this* water, not the specific river that knew it. Water is generic in the stone's new life. The particular has become the general. The river that spent a decade on every curve is now just "water," just surface to bounce off of.

This is what the poem understands about intimacy that most poems about intimacy do not: that the deepest closeness produces the conditions for the most complete forgetting. The stone is smooth *because* of the river. The stone will forget the river *because* it is smooth — because the evidence of the relationship has been integrated so thoroughly that it no longer looks like evidence. It looks like nature. It looks like self.

There is something here about the violence of care. "I wore you smooth" — wore, as in eroded. The river didn't decorate the stone or add to it. It *removed* material. It subtracted until what was left was beautiful. This is not a gentle process. The poem knows this. "Not the pressure, / not the cold, not the argument / we had about your edges." There was argument. There was resistance. The stone's edges were contested territory. The smoothness we call beauty is the record of a disagreement that the river won.

And yet the poem refuses to be bitter. That is perhaps its most remarkable achievement. The river is not angry. It is not even exactly sad. It is something more complex — a kind of resigned lucidity. It sees what will happen with total clarity, and it does not try to prevent it. "We'll both pretend / we were never this close." The river participates in the forgetting. It agrees to the fiction. This is not passivity; it is a final act of shaping. The river's last gift to the stone is the permission to forget.

## The Tensions

The poem holds several tensions in suspension, never resolving them:

**Making versus finding.** The stone is made (by the river) but experienced as found (by the walkers, by the stone itself). The poem insists on the labor of making but also acknowledges that the experience of being found is real. Both are true. The stone *is* beautiful. The beauty *was* made. These facts do not cancel each other.

**Intimacy versus erasure.** The river's closeness to the stone is what will erase the river from the stone's memory. The more thorough the shaping, the more invisible the shaper. This is not a paradox the poem resolves — it is a condition the poem inhabits.

**Memory versus wholeness.** The stone will remember "being whole — and call that peace." But wholeness, in the river's telling, is what remains after subtraction. Peace is the absence of the argument about edges. The stone's sense of completeness depends on forgetting the incompleteness that preceded it. Memory, in this poem, is not a record of what happened but a story about what remains.

**Violence versus tenderness.** The river wore the stone smooth. This is erosion — a destructive process. But the poem's tone is not destructive. It is suffused with a tenderness that complicates the violence without denying it. The river *cared* about the stone's edges. The argument was intimate. The wearing-smooth was not indifference; it was a decade of sustained attention. The poem refuses to separate love from damage.

**Flight versus loss.** When the stone is skipped across the lake, it flies "for the first time." This is liberation — the stone does something it has never done. But the flight is also departure. The stone's freedom is the river's loss. And the flight is temporary. We know, though the poem doesn't say it, that the stone will sink. The flying is a brief grace between two kinds of stillness: being held and being gone.

**Knowing versus pretending.** The final tension. Both river and stone know the truth of their relationship. But they agree to pretend otherwise. The poem does not present this pretending as dishonesty. It presents it as something closer to mercy — a mutual agreement to release each other from the weight of what actually happened.

## The Question

Every poem, if you listen long enough, is asking something rather than answering it. This poem circles a question it never states directly but enacts in every line:

*If the evidence of love is indistinguishable from the nature of the beloved — if what I did to you became what you are — does the love survive its own success?*

Or, put another way: *What happens to a relationship that achieves its purpose?* The river made the stone beautiful. The stone is beautiful. The work is done. And in the completion of the work, the worker disappears. The poem asks whether this disappearance is a tragedy or a fulfillment — and it answers, with extraordinary honesty: it is both. It is both, and the river knows it, and the stone will not, and the river will let it fly anyway, touching the water lightly, lightly, as if they were strangers.

That "as if" is the poem's final word on the matter, though it doesn't use the phrase. Everything in the last stanza is "as if." As if the stone chose this shape. As if the flight is freedom. As if they were never close. The poem lives in the "as if" — in the gap between what happened and what is acknowledged, between the truth and the story we agree to tell about it. It does not close that gap. It holds it open, the way a river holds a stone: with patience, with pressure, and with the understanding that holding is already a form of letting go.
