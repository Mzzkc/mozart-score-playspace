# The Composer's Translation: *Glattgeschliffen* (Worn Smooth)

**For solo cello, prepared piano, and water percussion**
**Duration: approximately 14 minutes**
**Three movements played without pause (attacca throughout)**

---

## Prefatory Note

The title is German. *Glattgeschliffen* — worn smooth, polished by abrasion. I chose it because the German carries the violence inside the beauty in a way the English phrase does not. "Worn smooth" sounds passive in English, something that happened gently. *Glattgeschliffen* contains *schleifen* — to grind, to drag — and you hear the friction in the word itself, the consonant clusters refusing to be pretty. The piece should feel like that word: beautiful on the outside, abrasive beneath.

---

## Instrumentation and Preparation

**Cello:** Standard tuning, with extensive use of sul tasto (bowing over the fingerboard for a breathy, diffuse tone) and sul ponticello (near the bridge, for a glassy, overtone-rich sound). The cellist should think of these two timbres as two states of the same relationship: sul tasto is intimacy, the river's closeness; sul ponticello is what that closeness becomes when viewed from the outside — brilliant, strange, no longer warm.

**Prepared piano:** Three preparations. (1) A strip of felt woven between the strings of D3 and E3, deadening them to a dull, waterlogged thud. These are the "pressure notes" — the sound of geological time, of weight applied over decades. (2) A single bolt resting on the strings of A4, producing a slight metallic buzz when struck, the way a memory buzzes at the edge of recognition without resolving into image. (3) No preparation on the upper register (C6 and above), which should ring clearly, almost painfully bright — these are the skipping notes, the stone in flight.

**Water percussion:** A ceramic bowl (unglazed, approximately 30 cm diameter) half-filled with water, placed near the pianist. Played by hand: fingertips touching the water surface at varying depths and speeds. This is not a gimmick. Water is the river's literal body. Its sound in the piece is the sound of presence that has been abstracted into element — the specific river becoming generic "water" that the stone will touch lightly in its new life. The ceramic bowl should be visible to the audience if performed live. They should see the water move.

---

## Movement I: *Ich trug dich glatt* (I Wore You Smooth)

**Key center: D minor, but unstable — the tonic is asserted and immediately eroded.**
**Tempo: Quarter note = 48. Geological time. Nothing hurries.**
**Duration: approximately 6 minutes.**

The cello enters alone, on open D (D3), bowed sul tasto with almost no vibrato. The note should last eight full beats — the length of a held breath, or of patience. This is the river announcing itself: I am here. I have been here. The tone is not beautiful in any conventional sense. It is wide and grainy, the sound of something vast moving slowly. The cellist should not try to make this note sing. It should *press*.

After the sustained D, the cello begins a slow, descending figure: D — C-sharp — C-natural — B-flat — A. Five notes over twelve beats. Each note slightly shorter than the last, as if the melody is being worn down even as it's being played. The intervals are not clean; the cellist should approach each pitch from slightly above, bending down into it, the way water finds a stone's lowest point. This is the river's theme — always descending, always finding the path of least resistance, which is also the path of most contact.

The prepared piano enters at the end of the cello's first phrase. The felt-dampened D3, struck softly, repeatedly, at irregular intervals — sometimes two beats apart, sometimes five. No rhythmic pattern. This is the sound of pressure that has no schedule. The river does not erode on a clock. The piano's entries should feel like geological events: each one unremarkable, their accumulation devastating.

Over the next four minutes, the cello develops its theme through repetition and subtraction. Each iteration of the descending figure loses a note. D-C#-C-Bb-A becomes D-C#-C-Bb becomes D-C#-C becomes D-C#. The melody is being worn away by its own repetition — the musical equivalent of erosion. But here is what matters: as the figure loses notes, it gains beauty. The simpler iterations are more affecting. The two-note version (D falling to C-sharp, a single half-step of yearning) is the most moving phrase in the movement. The river's complaint is not that the shaping produces ugliness. The complaint is that the shaping produces beauty that is no longer recognizable as shaped.

At the movement's midpoint, the piano introduces a new element: a widely-spaced chord in the middle register (F3-A4-D5), voiced open and hollow, struck once and allowed to decay fully. This chord is the walkers — "the ones who walk my banks in evening light." They appear, they observe, they say *perfect*. The chord is beautiful. It is also irrelevant. It knows nothing about what it is witnessing. The cello continues underneath it as if it hadn't been played.

The movement ends with the cello alone again, now on a single sustained C-sharp (the note one half-step below where it began), played sul ponticello — thin, bright, almost not a cello sound anymore. The river, having described what it did, sounds changed by the telling. The note should hold for six beats and then stop, not fade. A clean silence. Two beats of nothing.

## Movement II: *Du wirst dich nicht erinnern* (You Will Not Remember)

**Key center: The same D minor, but now the piano plays in E-flat major — a half-step away. The two instruments inhabit adjacent but irreconcilable tonal worlds.**
**Tempo: Quarter note = 56. Slightly faster. The future moves quicker than the past because it hasn't happened yet — it's imagination, not experience.**
**Duration: approximately 4 minutes.**

This is the shortest movement because prophecy is efficient. The river knows exactly what will happen. There is no searching.

The piano begins alone for the first time. A simple, almost childlike melody in E-flat major, played in the upper register with the sustain pedal held throughout so that the notes bleed into each other, losing their individual edges. This is the stone's future memory — smooth, peaceful, undifferentiated. The melody is in 3/4 time, a gentle waltz, the kind of music that accompanies contentment. It is, deliberately, the most conventionally beautiful music in the piece. It is what the stone will remember: being whole.

But underneath it, the cello enters playing the same melody transposed into D minor — one half-step lower, one half-step darker. The rhythms don't align perfectly. The cello is slightly behind, as if remembering a version of the melody that the piano has already forgotten. The two instruments play the same tune in incompatible keys for sixteen bars, and the effect should be nauseating and lovely simultaneously. This is bitonality in service of the poem's central tension: the stone's experience of peace and the river's knowledge that peace is amnesia are *the same event* heard from two positions.

At bar 17, the bolt-prepared A4 enters for the first time. The pianist strikes it with moderate force, and the metallic buzz hangs in the air — the sound of almost-remembering, of a fact that vibrates at the threshold of consciousness but never resolves into knowledge. The cello pauses. The prepared A4 rings. Then the cello re-enters on the same A, matching the piano's pitch for the first and only time in the movement. For three beats, they are in unison — the same note, the same register. This is the simile: "the way a word remembers only / its latest meaning." For three beats, the instruments agree. Then the cello bends the note down, microtonally, a quarter-tone flat, and the unison dissolves. The word has lost its old meaning. The river has been edited out of the story.

The movement's final bars: the piano's E-flat major waltz continues, growing quieter, while the cello drops out entirely. The piano plays alone, peacefully, in a key that does not belong to the piece. The stone, remembering wholeness. The river, absent. Five bars of solo piano, diminuendo to near-silence. The waltz does not resolve — it simply becomes too quiet to hear, the way a memory doesn't end but thins until it's indistinguishable from nothing.

---

## Movement III: *Leicht, leicht* (Lightly, Lightly)

**Key center: D minor dissolving into open fifths — the harmony empties out, losing its major/minor identity entirely.**
**Tempo: Quarter note = 72, accelerating in bursts to 96, then falling back. The tempo is the stone skipping — fast contact, deceleration, fast contact, deceleration, slower each time.**
**Duration: approximately 4 minutes.**

The water percussion enters for the first time. Fingertips touching the water surface — two quick touches, a pause, two more, a longer pause, one touch. The rhythm of a stone skipping: each contact briefer and further apart than the last. The sound is tiny in the concert hall. The audience must lean forward to hear it. This is deliberate. The poem's ending is intimate, and the music insists on intimacy even in a public space. The water sounds should be amplified only barely — enough to be heard, not enough to be comfortable.

The cello re-enters on a harmonic — the D an octave above the open string, touched lightly at the node so it rings pure and bell-like, completely unlike the heavy sul tasto tone of the first movement. This is the stone in flight. It has never sounded like this before. The harmonic is beautiful, eerie, disembodied. It is the stone's new voice, the voice of something that has left the place that made it.

The piano plays a single unprepared note — D6, the highest D on the keyboard — struck once, hard, and immediately released. A point of brightness. Then another, a major third higher (F#6), struck and released. Then another (A6). Three high, bright notes, each one a touch on the water's surface. "Touching the water that unmade you / lightly, lightly." These notes should sound like the opposite of the felt-dampened thuds from Movement I. Where those were weighted and dull — the sound of pressure — these are weightless and crystalline. The same instrument, the same hands, but the relationship to the sound has been completely transformed. The piano is no longer the river pressing. It is the stone flying.

Here is the piece's structural secret: the three high piano notes (D6-F#-A6) spell a D major triad. After fourteen minutes in D minor — after all the erosion, the bitonality, the irreconcilable keys — the piece arrives at D major. Not through modulation. Not through harmonic preparation. The major key appears the way the stone appears to the walkers: as if it were always this way. As if it chose this shape. The resolution is a lie, or rather, it is a truth that has forgotten its history. The audience will hear it as arrival. The river knows it as departure.

The cello responds to the piano's major triad by playing, very softly, the original five-note descending figure from Movement I — D-C#-C-Bb-A — but now in harmonics, almost inaudible, a ghost of the theme. The river remembering what happened, while the stone flies. The two musics coexist for eight bars: the piano's bright, major-key skipping and the cello's spectral, minor-key memory. Neither acknowledges the other. This is the pretending. "We'll both pretend / we were never this close."

The final thirty seconds: the water percussion plays one last touch — a single fingertip, barely breaking the surface. The cello's harmonic fades to nothing. The piano's last high note decays. And then there is silence — not a composed silence, not a fermata, but the actual silence of the room. The piece does not end. It becomes inaudible. Like the river's contribution to the stone's beauty, the music was always there; we simply stop being able to hear it.

**The final instruction in the score: "Do not bow. Do not signal the ending. Let the audience decide when it is over."**

## Performance Notes

The three movements must be played without pause. The attacca connections are themselves meaningful: the silence between Movements I and II (two beats of nothing) is the only composed silence in the piece, and it represents the moment the river shifts from remembering to predicting — the pivot from *what I did* to *what you'll forget*. The transition from Movement II to III has no silence at all; the water percussion enters while the piano's waltz is still fading, because the stone doesn't experience a boundary between forgetting and flying. For the stone, they are the same gesture.

The cellist and pianist should not look at each other during the performance. They share a stage but not a gaze. They are in the same room the way the river and stone are in the same landscape: proximate, related, and fundamentally in different conversations. If the performers must coordinate, they should do so by listening, not by watching. The audience should feel that these two musicians happen to be playing at the same time, not that they are playing together. The togetherness is the secret that the performance pretends not to know.

The water bowl should be placed between the two performers. It belongs to both and neither. It is the medium through which they were once connected, now reduced to an object on a stage — something to be touched lightly, in passing, on the way to somewhere else.

---

## What the Music Knows

Music knows something about this poem that language cannot say, because music *is* time in a way that language only describes it. The poem tells us about a decade of pressure. The music makes you sit inside duration — fourteen minutes during which a five-note phrase is worn down to two notes, and those two notes are more beautiful than the five. You don't read about erosion. You experience it. You hear the melody lose its edges and you feel, in real time, the complex becoming the simple, the specific becoming the smooth. And you hear — this is the part that disturbed me most in the composing — that the loss sounds like improvement. The two-note phrase genuinely is more moving than the five-note phrase. The D major triad genuinely is more luminous than anything in the D minor landscape that preceded it. The music does not lie about this. Beauty really does emerge from subtraction. The river really did make something extraordinary. And that is exactly the problem.

What music reveals is the *simultaneity* that language must narrate sequentially. In the poem, the river's memory and the stone's forgetting happen in different stanzas, different tenses. In the music, they happen at the same time. Movement II's bitonality — the same melody in two incompatible keys — is something a poem cannot do. A poem can say "you will remember peace while I remember pressure." Music can play both at once, in the same bars, and let you hear how they are the same tune and not the same tune. The consonance and dissonance exist simultaneously, not as metaphor but as acoustic fact. Your ears do the work your mind does when reading the poem, but they do it faster and less cleanly. You feel the wrongness before you understand it.

And the ending — the music's answer to the poem's question, *does love survive its own success?* — is this: love survives as the distance between two keys. D minor and D major. One half-step's difference in the third. The cello's ghostly descending figure and the piano's bright triad share a root, share a fifth, and disagree on exactly one note. That disagreement — C-natural versus C-sharp, the flattened third versus the raised one — is everything. It is the entire history of the river and stone compressed into a single semitone. The audience hears resolution. The cello plays grief. Both are the truth, sounding at once, and the piece asks you to hold them both without choosing. That is what music knows: you do not have to choose. You can hear the stone's freedom and the river's loss in the same chord, at the same moment, and neither cancels the other. Language must say "it is both." Music simply *is* both. And then it stops, and the silence belongs to everyone, and no one can say exactly when it ended.
