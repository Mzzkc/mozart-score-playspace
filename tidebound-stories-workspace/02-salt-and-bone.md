# Salt and Bone

---

The kem smelled of other people's sleep.

Resa pressed her face into the seam of the wall where two foam-rock panels met, breathing the thin draft that leaked through. Cold, mineral, faintly salt — the air outside, the air of Sovwei, which was at least honest in its bitterness. Inside, the main room held the accumulated warmth of thirty-two bodies: her husband's family, the Dorath kem of Orellen, stacked in their winter arrangement like tools in a vault. Tashren's mother coughing in the alcove. The children on the sleeping shelf, five of them in a row like bread loaves proving. The low percussion of someone — Kellav, probably — tapping patterns on a hearthstone in the next room. Not for anyone's benefit. Just to prove the silence could be broken.

Tashren found her by the wall.

"Your hands are cold," he said. He said it gently, the way he said everything, which was a quality she had loved at first and now found difficult. Gentle men do not mean to be walls, but they are walls.

"They brought me a message," she said.

He waited.

"My mother is dead."

The words were simple, and she had been practicing them for the hour since the Reckoner's boy had come to the kem-door with the slip of vesh, the neat Thursa notation — a name, a date, the glyph for *still*, meaning the body had not been given to the cliff-face but was being held. Held for her. As if anyone held things for her anymore.

Tashren's hand found her shoulder. "Resa."

She waited for the rest.

"We'll observe," he said. "I'll tell Mother."

"No." The word was harder than she'd meant. She softened it, or tried. "No, I need to go to Thursa."

"In Sovwei? The passes are —"

"The coast road. Three days."

"Resa, your mother — she is beneath the water now." He did not say the name. It was Sovwei, and names belonged to the deep, and the Dorath did not break this taboo even for grief. Especially for grief. "Whatever she was keeping, it can wait for the Outbreath. We'll go together in Suulin, and —"

"The vaults," Resa said. "Her salt-vaults. She was the keeper. The *only* keeper."

Tashren's hand stilled on her shoulder. Even a highland man understood salt-vaults. Even a man who had never submerged his hands in that dark, mineral water knew that the vaults held seed-stores and tools and the distillation of a shelf family's wealth across a hundred returns. And he knew — everyone knew — that unclaimed vaults at the Outbreath became common ground. Any family with a diving-hook could crack them.

"Your brother —"

"Hasn't spoken to me in four returns."

"Your sister —"

"Doesn't dive. Never learned. Mother didn't trust her hands." Resa pulled away from the wall, from his touch, from the thin clean air. The kem pressed back in. "I have to go. I have to learn where the vaults are, and I have to recover them before Suulin, or we lose everything my mother carried."

Tashren looked at her the way he looked at storms from the kem's upper window — attentive, respectful, certain the stone would hold. "You haven't dived since you were a girl."

"The body remembers."

"The body also freezes."

She turned from him then, and in the amber light of the rethka-oil lamp — that warm, oily glow that had been the first strange beauty she'd loved about the highlands — she saw what she had not yet said reflected in his face. He understood. Not what she needed to do, but what doing it would mean: that she was going back. Not visiting — returning to the coast, to Thursa, to the shelf-identity she had formally shed when she married into the Orellen kem. That she was, for however many weeks this took, no longer his wife in the highland sense. She was her mother's daughter. She was Voshu.

---

Three days on the coast road in Sovwei was not a walk. It was a negotiation with weather. The path clung to the cliff-face above the winter sea, switchbacking through wind-carved channels in the Copper Stacks, the stone green and amber even under grey sky. Below, the ocean sat heavy and opaque, the color of unpolished tin. Somewhere beneath that surface — thirty meters down, maybe less near the Nearmarsh — the shelf waited. The velu, sealed and dormant. The torath, barnacled and patient. The haara platforms with their empty joint-sockets. And the salt-vaults, stone-lined chambers drilled into bedrock, filled with water so mineral-dense it held the sea at bay.

Her mother's vaults.

Resa walked with a pack of dried fish, a waterskin, and a winter-carving she had taken from Tashren's family shelf without asking. Not stolen. Borrowed. A piece of the kem's history pressed into her palm: a small bone figure of a woman kneeling, which Tashren's grandmother had carved during the winter of Resa's marriage. The figure's hands were open and empty. She had always thought it a gesture of welcome. Now she wondered if it meant something else. The open hands of someone who has let go.

She met no one the first day. The second, a pair of Tanvelu passed her going the other direction — a man and a woman in salt-stiffened clothing, their faces the wind-scored brown of people who lived on the water year-round. They greeted her in the coast dialect, which she answered in the highland. The woman's eyes moved across her — the foam-rock-dust on her coat, the Dorath braids, the soft highland boots unsuited to wet stone — and read her history in three seconds.

"Going down?" the woman asked.

"Going to Thursa."

"The water's rough this week. Southern current." The woman paused. "You're Maera's girl. The one who went up."

Resa felt the name land in her chest like a stone dropped into still water. It was Sovwei. You did not say the names of the dead.

"She's beneath the water," the woman added, not unkindly. "But then, everything worth having is."

They passed. Resa stood on the switchback for a long time after, watching the Tanvelu descend toward the shore where, impossibly, she could see the dark shapes of houseboats riding the winter swell. They did not go up. They did not come down. They drifted, and the drifting was its own kind of permanence.

---

Thursa in Sovwei was a city turned inward. The Copper Stacks — those oxidized sea-cliffs, striated green and amber, rising forty meters above the winter high-water mark — held the settlement like a cupped hand. In summer, the stairway-streets ran down to the Hinge and the shelf beyond, busy with the Raise, with hethka convoys, with the colors and noise of Thalwei. In winter, the lower stairways ended at water. The sea sat in the streets like an uninvited guest who had come to stay, and the city climbed. Rope-bridges connected the upper terraces. Membrane windows glowed with lamplight. The smell was woodsmoke and salt and the particular density of a vertical city in compression — bodies and cooking and stone dust and the sea, always the sea, breathing up from below.

Resa found her brother Venn in the family's winter quarters: two rooms on the third terrace, connected by a narrow passage to the kem they sheltered with — the Sullar family, Dorath by origin but coast-settled for six generations, which made them something neither highland nor shelf. Venn was carving. Not the Dorath tradition, not stone or bone, but a piece of verha wood — steelkelp, dense as iron — working a shape she couldn't identify. He did not look up when she entered.

"You heard," she said.

"I found her." His voice was flat, the vowels clipped. The coast dialect she had once spoken without thinking and now heard as a foreign language inside her own mouth. "Three days ago. She went to check the near-shore vault markers. Slipped on ice. Broken neck."

"Venn."

"Clean death. Fast." He said it as comfort, or as accusation — with Venn, they were the same. "Better than drowning. Better than the cold taking her slow."

"I came for the vaults."

Now he looked up. He had their mother's face — narrow, weather-cut, the skin of someone who had spent thirty returns in wind and salt water. His hands, holding the carving knife and the block of steelkelp, were scarred and competent. Her hands, she knew, looked like a highland wife's: smooth, capable in a domestic way, unscored by salt.

"You left," he said.

"I married."

"Same word."

"Venn, if no one recovers the vaults before Suulin —"

"I know what happens." He set the carving down. "I've been diving every day since she died. I've found two of the six. The near-shore pair, the ones everyone knows about. The four deep vaults — the ones with the real stores, the seed-vaults, the tool-wraps — I can't find them. She never showed me."

"She never showed *me* either."

"Then why are you here?"

"Because she showed someone. And if it wasn't you and it wasn't Hallei —"

"Hallei won't come. She sent word from Kedderath. She'll observe from there." His mouth twisted. "Highland observance. Light a lamp, tap a rhythm, done."

"Venn."

"You want to find the vaults, you'll need to dive. In Sovwei. In winter water. You, who haven't been in the sea since you were what — sixteen? Seventeen?"

"Fifteen."

"Fifteen returns. Half a lifetime ago. You married a highland man and moved to a stone house with a hearth and foam-rock walls and you haven't felt salt water in —"

"I know." She held his gaze. "Will you teach me?"

The silence stretched. Through the wall, she could hear the Sullar children singing — a vetho, the walking-song for the Nearmarsh approach, their small voices tracing a landscape twenty meters underwater. *Past the third torath, where the current splits. Keep the whaleback ridge at your left hand. Count four breaths between the seep-stone and the drop.*

"I won't teach you," Venn said. "But there's someone who might."

---

The Tanvelu woman's name was Oshet, and she lived on a houseboat moored in the lee of the Third Stack, where the cliff's overhang created a pocket of relative calm even in the winter chop. The boat was low-slung, heavy-keeled by Tanvelu standards, its hull sealed with so many layers of tidecreep resin that it smelled permanently of anise. Inside, the single cabin was smaller than Resa's sleeping alcove in the Orellen kem, but it felt larger — the walls curved, the ceiling low, everything designed around the body rather than imposed upon it. Oshet's possessions hung from hooks or nestled in rope-cradles that swayed with the boat's motion. Nothing was fixed. Everything was held.

"Maera's daughter," Oshet said. Not a question. She was older than Resa had expected — sixty returns, maybe more, her face a landscape of salt-exposure and cold, her hands knotted and enormous. She wore her hair in the Tanvelu fashion, close-cropped and oiled against the water. "The one who went up to the stone houses."

"I need to find my mother's deep vaults. The four she kept by touch."

"I know what she kept. I dove with her for twenty returns." Oshet poured something into a clay cup — a tea of dried shelf-herbs, bitter and mineral, tasting of Thalwei in the depths of winter. "Your mother's hands knew those vaults the way your feet know the stairs to your own door. She could find them in the dark, by the current, by the temperature of the water against her wrists. She could tell, by the feel of the stone lip, whether a vault had shifted in the season."

"Can you find them?"

Oshet drank. The boat rocked. Through the hull, Resa could hear the sea — not the dramatic crash of storms but the constant, intimate pressure of deep water against wood. A sound that said: *I am here. I am always here.*

"I can find three of them," Oshet said. "The fourth, the deepest — your mother found that one alone. She dove alone for it. Thirty meters, maybe more. Cold enough to stop the heart if you don't know how to breathe. She never told me where it was."

"Why?"

"Because that vault held something she didn't want anyone to find by accident." Oshet set the cup down. "She told me once — the only time she spoke of it — that the deep vault held what the shelf had given her. Not what she'd stored. What the shelf had *given*."

Resa felt the hair on her arms rise. "What does that mean?"

"It means your mother was a vault-keeper, and vault-keepers keep secrets the way the sea keeps the shelf. Patiently. Completely. For as long as it takes."

"Will you guide me?"

Oshet looked at her for a long time. Through the membrane window, the grey light of Sovwei afternoon fell in soft diffusion, turning the cabin interior the color of old honey. The boat creaked. The sea breathed.

"I will guide you," Oshet said, "for a price."

"Name it."

"When you find what your mother hid in the deep vault, you show it to me before you show it to anyone else. Before your brother, before the Reckoners, before your highland husband."

"I don't know what's in the vault."

"Neither do I. That's why I want to see it."

---

Learning to dive again was not learning. It was remembering.

The first day, Oshet took her to the sheltered water below the Second Stack, where the cliff curved inward and the winter swell broke against rock before reaching them. The water was the grey-green of old glass, and it was cold in a way that Resa's body had forgotten — not the sharp cold of highland winter air, which bit and released, but the slow, total cold of immersion, which entered through the skin and kept going, pressing inward until it found bone.

"Breathe," Oshet said, standing waist-deep, completely untroubled. "You're holding everything. Let the water hold you."

Resa breathed. Her chest was a fist. Her hands, underwater, were already numb.

"Your mother started you at — what — five returns?"

"Six."

"Six. So your body has ten returns of salt water in it. Under all that highland warmth, under the foam-rock and the hearthside and the dry boots, your body knows this. It's waiting for you to stop thinking and let it work."

Resa submerged. The cold was a sound — a high, clean tone that filled her skull and then, as her body adjusted, faded to a hum. She opened her eyes. The water was murky near the surface, stirred by wind and runoff, but below — below, where the cliff-face descended into deeper water — she could see the greenish clarity that she remembered from childhood. The particular light of winter water over shelf-stone: diffuse, directionless, as if the light came from the stone itself.

She surfaced gasping.

"Again," Oshet said.

They dove for three hours. By the end, Resa's hands were white and her jaw ached from clenching, but something had shifted. Not skill — she was clumsy, her kicks inefficient, her buoyancy control nonexistent compared to Oshet's fluid economy. What had shifted was older than skill. A recognition in the muscles of her back, the joints of her fingers, the particular way her body oriented in water: this way is down, this way is the current, this is the temperature gradient that means you are approaching stone.

She had not felt this since she was fifteen. Since the summer she told her mother she was marrying Tashren din Orellen, highland-born, stone-housed, and her mother had looked at her with an expression that Resa now understood was not disappointment but solha. The tenderness of seeing something you are about to lose and hoping you will see it again.

---

They worked in stages. Oshet knew the protocol — vault-keepers had a method, and even the Tanvelu respected it. You did not simply dive to the shelf floor and start groping. You oriented first. The shelf, even submerged, had landmarks: the torath posts, barnacled and current-scarred but upright after hundreds of returns. The haara platforms, too heavy to move, sitting on the seafloor like altars to a god who had gone somewhere and would be back. The Whaleback ridges, still visible as dark striations in the underwater murk.

The first vault — the one Oshet knew best — was in the Nearmarsh zone, eight meters down, tucked into a natural shelf of sandstone that Resa's mother had lined with flat stones to create a chamber the size of a storage chest. The vault-mouth was a square opening, plugged with a fitted stone cap held in place by water pressure and a simple locking-stone that had to be turned, by hand, in a specific direction.

"She could find it by the feel of the current," Oshet said. They were on the houseboat, wrapped in dried-kelp blankets, drinking the bitter shelf-tea. "Water moves differently around an opening than around solid stone. Your mother felt those differences the way you feel the difference between wood and metal."

"I don't have that sensitivity."

"You don't have it yet. But you have her hands." Oshet reached across and took Resa's right hand, turning it over. "Same palm shape. Same finger length. The hands your mother used to read the vaults in the dark — you have the same instruments."

They dove the next morning. Oshet guided her down through the green murk to the sandstone shelf, and Resa felt along the rock-face with fingers already aching from cold. It was nothing like touching stone in air. Underwater, stone had a presence — a thermal signature, a texture made different by the layer of biofilm and sediment that coated everything. She felt along the shelf, centimeter by centimeter, while the cold pressed in and her lungs burned.

Then: an edge. Not natural — too regular, too sharp. A corner of fitted stone.

She surfaced with the memory in her fingers like a word she had forgotten and suddenly heard someone else say. The vault was there. She had found it. Not by knowledge but by the hands that had always known.

---

Three vaults recovered in eight days. Each one was a small triumph and a small devastation. The pouches inside — tide-tempered leather, stiff and gritty with mineral salt, knotted shut with the specific bindings that identified their contents — held what Resa expected: seed-stores, carefully preserved. Tool-wraps, oiled against rust. Folded vesh records. The preserved wealth of a shelf family, sealed against the sea and held in trust by a woman who was now beneath the water and whose name Resa could not say.

Each time she opened a pouch, she felt her mother's hands in the knots. The specific tension, the particular crossing pattern. Her mother had tied these in autumn, in the last days of Karrin, working by memory and habit. The knots were her handwriting, and Resa was reading a letter that said: *I trusted the sea to keep this. I trusted you to come back for it.*

But she hadn't come back. She had gone up. She had married a highland man and lived in a stone house and eaten the cuisine of discipline and forgotten, deliberately, the feel of cold water on her wrists.

The fourth vault — the deep one, the one Oshet couldn't find — remained.

"Your mother dove alone for it," Oshet said. They were sitting on the houseboat's deck in the grey late-afternoon light, the winter sea shifting beneath them in long, muscular swells. Somewhere below, the shelf waited. "Thirty meters. Maybe deeper. The water at that depth is —"

"I know."

"You don't. Not in winter. In summer, thirty meters is cold and dark and the pressure makes your ears sing. In winter, thirty meters is a different country. The water is dense. The cold is not on you — it is in you, in your blood, slowing your heart. Your mother could stay down for four minutes at that depth. Four minutes of work in the dark, by touch, with cold water pressing on her chest." Oshet paused. "Can you do that?"

"I have to."

"You want to. It's not the same. You could leave the deep vault. Let it go. The three we recovered hold enough to rebuild the family's summer holdings. You could walk back to the highlands with the seed-stores and the tool-wraps and tell your highland husband you succeeded. Which you have."

"The deep vault is mine."

"It was your mother's."

"It's mine now."

Oshet studied her. The wind moved between them, salt-sharp and cold, carrying the sound of the winter sea — that constant, low, enormous breathing, the sound of Sovwei itself. The world contracted.

"I can guide you to the depth," Oshet said. "I can show you the approach — the current patterns, the thermal layers. But at thirty meters, in winter, you will be alone. Your mother was alone there, and she chose that. Whatever she kept in the deep vault, she kept alone."

---

Resa sent word to Tashren. A short message, carried by a Reckoner's courier heading inland: *Three vaults recovered. One remains. I am well.* She did not add *I am diving in winter water every day and my hands are learning to read the dark* or *I dreamed of her last night and she was tying knots and I woke with my fingers moving* or *I think I understand now why she never taught me the vaults — it wasn't that she was waiting for me to be ready, it was that she knew I would have to come back for them, and the coming back was the teaching.*

She did not add: *I am not coming home yet.*

Her brother came to Oshet's houseboat on the ninth day. He stood on the mooring-dock, hands in his coat, looking at her the way he had looked at her the day she'd told the family she was marrying up. Not angry. Something worse: resigned.

"You found three," he said.

"Oshet found two. I found the third."

"The deep vault."

"I'm going for it."

Venn stepped onto the boat. It rocked under his weight, and Resa saw him adjust without thinking — the shelf-born balance, weight shifting to the balls of the feet, hands finding holds that a highland person wouldn't see. She had lost that balance. Or no — it was coming back, in pieces, in dreams, in the way her body now moved on Oshet's deck without her mind directing it.

"She dove alone for the deep vault," Venn said. "I asked her, once, to let me come. She said no. She said some things are kept by one pair of hands or not at all."

"She was protecting something."

"She was keeping something. There's a difference."

They looked at each other across the small deck, two children of the same mother, the same salt-vaults, the same shelf-songs learned before reading. Between them, four returns of silence. Resa had gone up. Venn had stayed. Neither had chosen wrong, but neither had chosen free.

"Be careful," Venn said. He stepped off the boat, and the dock swayed, and he walked back toward Thursa without looking back.

---

The dive.

Oshet took her out before dawn, when the winter sea was at its calmest — the brief window between the night swell and the morning wind, when the water lay heavy and dark and still. The houseboat anchored above the Whaleback ridge, and Resa could feel, even through the hull, the shape of the shelf below: the sandstone spine running parallel to the coast, the channels between ridges, and beyond them, the deeper water over the Nearmarsh flats where the vaults were drilled into bedrock.

"The approach is along the ridge," Oshet said. She was checking Resa's belt — weighted stones in leather pouches, distributed for neutral buoyancy at depth, a system Resa's body remembered even as her mind catalogued it as insane. "Follow the ridge south until you feel the temperature drop. Your mother's deep vault is in the transition zone, where the Nearmarsh stone meets the older substrate. Colder water. Different texture."

"How will I know?"

"Your hands will know."

Resa stood at the boat's edge. The sea was black. Not the grey-green of daylight diving but black, the true dark of winter water before dawn, lit only by the faint greenish phosphorescence of disturbed plankton and, far below, the occasional amber pulse of a rethka in its winter phase — slow, dim, dreaming.

She breathed. In, out. In, out. The rhythm her mother had taught her before she could walk: *the sea is breathing and you are breathing and there is no difference.*

She went in.

The cold hit her like a wall that kept hitting — not a single impact but a continuous, crushing presence that tightened every muscle and then went deeper, into the joints, the core, the center of the chest where the lungs wanted to seize. She breathed through it. The weighted belt pulled her down. She kicked, and her body remembered the kick — not the efficient, trained stroke of a Tanvelu diver but the shelf-born kick, shorter, more muscular, the kick of a girl who had learned in shallows and was now applying it to depth.

Ten meters. The pressure on her ears built, and she equalized by swallowing, the way her mother had shown her: *swallow the sea, let it into you, you're not fighting the water, you're joining it.*

Fifteen meters. The cold changed quality. It was no longer on her skin — it was inside her bones, a deep, structural cold that slowed everything. Her heartbeat thudded heavy and slow. Her thoughts narrowed to sensation: the water on her face, the weighted pull of the belt, the direction of the current against her left side meaning she was still on the ridge.

Twenty meters. The light was gone. Not dimmed — gone. She was diving in the dark, in winter, thirty meters of ocean above a shelf she had not seen since she was fifteen years old. Her hands reached forward, and the water offered nothing back. Nothing but cold, and pressure, and the faint mineral taste of shelf-water filtering through her lips.

She found the ridge by touch. Sandstone, rougher than she expected — encrusted with winter growth, tiny barnacles and tube-worms that pricked her fingertips. She followed it south, kicking slowly, conserving air, letting her hands read the stone the way her mother's hands had read it ten thousand times.

The temperature dropped.

Not gradually — a curtain. One stroke she was in cold water and the next she was in colder water, a density change she could feel in her wrists, in the thin skin between her fingers. The transition zone. Where the Nearmarsh sandstone met something older, denser, colder.

She slowed. Her hands moved along the substrate. The stone changed under her fingers — sandstone giving way to a harder material, smoother, with a grain that ran against her palms. Bedrock. The ancient floor beneath the shelf sediment, exposed here where the ridge had been scoured by centuries of current.

She felt for openings. Her lungs were burning now — three minutes, maybe, since her last breath at the surface. The cold had taken her fine motor control; her fingers felt thick, approximate, reading the stone in broad strokes instead of the precise touch her mother must have used. She swept her hands in wide arcs, covering ground, searching for the edge, the corner, the fitted-stone signature of a vault-mouth.

Nothing.

Her chest compressed. Two minutes of air, maybe less. The dark was absolute. The cold was inside her bones.

She stopped moving. Hung in the water. Let the current press against her.

And felt it: a different current. Not the lateral drift of the winter shelf-flow but a tiny, circular eddy — water moving around an obstruction, curling back on itself in a pattern that said *there is an opening here.* The same way air moves differently through a cracked door. Her mother had felt this. Her mother's wrists had read this eddy a thousand times.

Resa followed the eddy with her right hand. The current strengthened, thinned, and her fingers found the edge of fitted stone. A vault-mouth. Square-cut, precise, the work of someone who had shaped stone in darkness by the memory of shape. She found the locking-stone and turned it — a quarter-turn left, the opposite of the shallow vaults, which was either a quirk or a code — and the cap loosened.

Inside: cold water, denser than the sea around it, mineral-heavy, pushing against her hand with the pressure of something that had been still for months. And in the vault, nestled in the stone-lined chamber: pouches. Tide-tempered leather, stiff with salt, knotted shut.

Two pouches. She could feel their shapes, their weights, their positions in the vault. One was the size and heft of a seed-store — the familiar contour, the gentle give of dried biological material inside leather. The other was smaller, harder, heavier for its size.

She took both. Tucked them into her diving belt with fingers that could barely close. The cold had her completely now — her vision, had there been light, would have been narrowing. Her heartbeat was a slow, distant drumming, not panicked but fading, the body's resources drawing inward to protect the core.

She ascended. Kicking for the surface with her mother's pouches against her hip, rising through the layers of cold and pressure and darkness, lungs screaming, the weight of the sea letting go of her in stages — twenty meters, fifteen, ten — and then the faint greenish light of pre-dawn filtering down through the winter water, and then the surface, and then air.

She broke the surface gasping, heaving, coughing salt water, and Oshet's hands were on her shoulders pulling her to the boat's hull and then over the gunwale and onto the deck where she lay on her back with two tide-tempered leather pouches on her chest and the winter sky above her, grey and close and enormous.

"You found it," Oshet said.

Resa's hands would not unclench from the pouches. Her fingers, white and stiff, gripped the leather with a force that had nothing to do with her will. The body held what it had found. The body would not let go.

---

They opened the pouches on Oshet's table, in the cabin's warm lamplight. Resa's hands had thawed enough to unknot the bindings, and she worked them slowly, feeling each crossing, each tension — her mother's fingerprints in cordage, the last physical sentence the dead woman had written.

The first pouch held seeds. Not the common stores of the shallow vaults — shelf-garden stock, salt-rye varieties, the standard inheritance of a vault-keeping family. These were older. Darker. Seeds she did not recognize, in a leather wrapping that was harder and more mineral-encrusted than any tide-tempered material she had seen. Seeds from the deep shelf. Seeds from the Farflat, perhaps. Her mother had kept them separate, kept them deeper, kept them in the coldest water and the darkest vault.

"She was collecting from the far shelf," Oshet said. "I knew she went there in summer. I didn't know she was bringing things back."

The second pouch was smaller. Resa untied it and reached inside and her fingers closed around something that was not seeds, not tools, not anything that belonged in a salt-vault.

It was a piece of stone. But not stone — something that looked like stone the way vesh looked like leather. A fabricated material, the color of pale bone, smooth as water-tumbled glass but with a texture beneath the surface, a grain or structure that her fingertips could feel but her eyes could not see. It was heavy for its size, dense, cool to the touch even after being held. Shaped — not carved but shaped, the way a potter shapes clay, in one continuous motion. A cylinder, narrowing slightly at one end, with the faintest suggestion of channels running through its interior. Tiny channels, like the veins in a leaf, or the tubes through which the Farflat formations sang.

Resa turned it in her hands. In the lamplight, the material had a faint luminescence — not glowing, exactly, but holding light, the way the inside of a shell holds the memory of the sea. She had never seen this material. She had never touched this material. But she recognized it. Every Tidebound child who had walked the far shelf in summer, who had pressed their hands against the singing formations and felt the hum of water through stone, recognized this material.

Calcium-ite aggregate. The stone of the Lost Builders. The stone of the formations that sang at dusk.

"Your mother found this," Oshet said. Her voice was very quiet. "On the far shelf. Or deeper."

"In the vaults?"

"No. This was not stored. This was *found*. By a woman who knew the shelf by touch in the dark." Oshet reached out and took the object gently from Resa's hands. Turned it. Held it to the lamp. The channels inside caught the light and, for a moment, the cylinder seemed to breathe — a pattern of illumination moving through its interior the way water moves through a pipe when you first turn the valve. "There are objects like this in Thursa's deep chambers. The ones that wash up after storms. The ones no one discusses."

"What is it?"

"I don't know. But your mother dove thirty meters in winter to keep it separate from everything else she owned. She kept it in the deepest vault, the one she told no one about. She kept it for — how many returns?"

Resa looked at the pouch. The leather was old. Older than her. Older than her mother's tenure as vault-keeper, maybe. The knots were her mother's, but the pouch itself — the pouch might have been her grandmother's.

"She was keeping it," Resa said. "The way the vault-keepers keep everything. Patiently. Completely. For as long as it takes."

Oshet set the object on the table between them. It sat there, pale and dense and luminous, a piece of a civilization that had built the singing formations and then vanished — or sailed away, or drowned, or become something else entirely. A piece of the mystery that the Tidebound kept in deep chambers and did not discuss. A piece of the question they had never been able to ask: *who built the shelf before we came, and where did they go, and is the singing of the formations an echo or a signal?*

"You owe me nothing," Oshet said. "This is the price, and it is paid. I have seen it." She paused. "What will you do?"

Resa picked the object up again. It fit her hand. It fit her hand exactly, as if shaped for a palm of her dimensions, her mother's dimensions, the particular span and grip of the women in her family who had been vault-keepers for — how many returns? How far back did the keeping go?

The body remembers what the mind has chosen to forget.

"I'll take it home," she said. And then stopped, because she did not know which home she meant.

Outside, the winter sea breathed against the hull. Somewhere below, in the dark, the shelf waited — the velu sealed and dreaming, the torath patient in the current, the empty vault open now, its secret surfaced.

In six weeks, the water would begin to pull back. The Outbreath would come, and the shelf would emerge, and the silence of Velsha morning would hold the coast in its impossible stillness. And Resa would be there — on the shore, in the shallows, watching the stone appear. Not the highland wife returning to visit. Not the shelf daughter come home. Something else. Something her mother had prepared for her without telling her, had summoned her to become by dying at the one time of year when the vaults could only be reached by someone willing to enter the winter water and work in the dark.

Inheritance is not a gift. It is a summons.

Resa closed her fingers around the pale cylinder, and the channels inside caught the lamplight one more time, and for a moment — just a moment — she thought she could feel it humming. A vibration too faint for sound. A resonance in the material, answering the pressure of her hand the way the formations answered the pressure of the sea.

Like something waiting to be asked.
