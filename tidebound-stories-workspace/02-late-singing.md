# The Late Singing

*Late Thalwei, approaching Karrin*

---

The note was in a hand she did not recognize.

Thessa din Rethol had spent fourteen months in the Thursa archives — longer than any apprentice Reckoner in living memory, longer than her instructor Pallav considered reasonable, longer than anyone would have tolerated from a student less meticulous — and she had learned to read the Breathing Ledger the way her mother had taught her to read shelf-ground: not the surface but the pattern beneath. The Ledger's primary columns were measured devotion, seventeen centuries of daily tidal readings at seventeen marker-stones, inscribed in the careful hand of whoever served as Thursa's water-keeper in that generation. These columns were the reason the Ledger existed. They were the heartbeat of Tidebound record-keeping — rock-stable, verified by cross-reference, cited in every kemshara dispute and every Reckoner arbitration and every argument about whether the breath was truly regular or merely appeared so from insufficient distance.

But the margins were where the water-keepers lived.

Between the ruled columns, in the space that remained after the measurements were inscribed, generations of archivists had left their observations. Weather, mostly. Shelf conditions. Notes on crystallization rates in the Whaleback pools, or the density of tolhu emergence, or the health of the velu at specific torath posts. These were not data. They were not indexed, not cross-referenced, not cited. They were the private thoughts of people who had spent years measuring the sea's advance and retreat and could not resist, in the free space between their disciplined columns, saying something about what they saw.

Thessa had read every margin note in the Ledger's last four centuries. Not because anyone asked her to. Because she could not stop.

The note she was looking at now was in an unfamiliar hand — small, tilted left, the ink the brownish-black of iron gall past its prime. It was dated to a year she calculated as roughly six hundred returns ago, and it said:

*The farthest singing came late this year. Three days after the waterline cleared the upper channels. Unusual.*

She turned the page. The next margin, two returns later, different hand — the usual water-keeper's cramped efficiency:

*Farflat formations singing later again. Not yet worrying but noted.*

Thessa pulled the thread. She had been pulling it for four months, working backward and forward through the Ledger, marking every reference to the formations' singing with a slip of vesh tucked between the pages. The slips accumulated. They formed a pattern that made her stomach clench in a way she could not entirely explain, because the pattern was not dramatic. It was not a sudden change. It was the kind of shift that took centuries to notice and minutes to dismiss.

The formations sang later every time someone mentioned them. Never earlier. In six hundred returns of marginal notes — fragments, asides, the offhand remarks of water-keepers who considered the singing peripheral to their work — not a single hand had recorded the formations singing ahead of schedule.

She had counted forty-three separate observations spanning twenty-nine different hands. Forty-three notes, each one a moment when a keeper paused in the middle of measuring the oscillation — the great, stable, seventeen-century oscillation — and wrote, in the margin, something like: *Later this year. Strange.* Or: *The formations' voice came only at the end of Thalwei, when they used to sing by midsummer.* Or, most recently, in a hand from less than two returns ago: *Almost did not hear them. Had to go further out.*

The primary data — the oscillation itself — was impeccable. The rhythm held. Seventeen centuries, seventeen marker-stones, and the sea advanced and retreated within a window of days, as it always had. The margin notes were the mutterings of people who had looked up from the main work and noticed something that the main work was not designed to capture.

Thessa closed the Ledger. Her hands were stiff from the archive's cold — Thursa's deepest chambers held a chill that persisted through Thalwei, the rock remembering Sovwei's cold the way it remembered everything. She sat in the amber glow of a rethka-oil lamp and thought about what to do.

---

Pallav was in the upper terrace, reviewing kemshara precedents for a dispute between two kem-lords in Kedderath that had been simmering for six returns and showed no signs of resolution. He was a lean man of fifty returns with the compressed patience of someone who had spent his life arbitrating between people who did not want to agree. When Thessa found him, he was sitting cross-legged on the sun-warm stone with three vesh scrolls open around him, weighting them with fist-sized rocks against the wind off the Copper Stacks.

"The formations are singing later," she said.

Pallav looked up. He had a way of looking at her that was neither encouraging nor discouraging — the Reckoner's trained neutrality, the art of not reacting until the evidence was complete.

"I've tracked forty-three marginal observations across six centuries," Thessa said. "Twenty-nine different hands. None of them record the formations singing earlier. Every one that comments on timing notes a delay."

Pallav was quiet for a moment. Then he said, "The margins are anecdotal."

"Forty-three anecdotes in one direction are not anecdotal. They're a signal without a dataset."

"That is a lovely phrase, Thessa. Write it down and bring it to a scholar who studies the formations."

"No one studies the formations."

This was the core of the problem, and they both knew it. The Farflat formations were an oddity — beautiful, mysterious, the subject of shelfwalker stories and children's wonder and the occasional footnote in the Ledger. But they were not the oscillation. They were not the shelf-gardens, or the mineral pools, or the foam-rock deposits, or the velu canopy, or the rethka cycles. They were far, they were strange, and they sang at dusk in a way that made you stop walking and feel, for reasons you could not name, profoundly alone. They were, by consensus, the province of the romantic and the unemployed.

"No one needs to study the formations," Pallav said, with the gentleness of a teacher who knows he is about to disappoint. "The oscillation holds. The rhythm is stable. The shelf provides. What the formations do at their own pace, on their own schedule, is their affair."

"And if they stop?"

Pallav set down the kemshara scroll. He looked at her with something she had not seen before — not disagreement but recognition, the look of a man watching someone step onto ground he has decided not to cross.

"If they stop singing," he said, "we will still plant the shelf-gardens. We will still fire the kilns. We will still descend in Suulin and ascend in Karrin and argue about kemshara terms in the winter. The formations are not infrastructure, Thessa. They are — " He paused, searching for the word. "A feature."

"We don't know what they are."

"Precisely. And we have survived seventeen centuries without knowing."

He returned to his scrolls. The wind picked up off the Stacks, carrying the copper tang of the cliffs and, beneath it, the warm salt smell of the shelf in late summer. The shelf was still enormous below them, stretching to the Glint. The air above the Farflat shimmered with heat. Somewhere out there — three days' walk from where she stood — the formations waited in their clusters, their channels threading the ground between them, their worm-tubes producing a sound that fewer people heard each return because fewer people went that far and those who did went later.

"I want to go," Thessa said.

"To the Farflat?"

"To the farthest formations. The ones the Long Campers mapped. The ones at the edge of the shelf."

Pallav did not answer immediately. A Reckoner apprentice was not forbidden from travel — Reckoners were defined by travel, by the obligation to move between communities and belong to none — but an expedition to the deep Farflat was not a walk. It was three days out across featureless clay, carrying water and food and shelter, sleeping on ground that had been seafloor three months prior and would be seafloor again in weeks. Late Thalwei was the wrong season for it. Karrin was approaching. The rethka had not yet gone dark, but they would soon. Every day she spent walking out was a day she would need to walk back, and the water did not wait.

"What do you expect to find?" he asked.

"I don't know."

"That is the first honest thing you've said today." He picked up a kemshara scroll, then set it down again. "Take a hinge-partner. Do not go alone."

"I'll take Rethol."

"Your brother is nineteen and has never been past the Whaleback."

"Then it's time he learned."

Pallav looked at her for a long moment. Then he nodded, once, and returned to the vesh scrolls spread around him. The dismissal was not unkind. It was the Reckoner's way of saying: *I have given you my answer. What you do with it is yours.*

---

They left at dawn. Thursa behind them, the Copper Stacks rising in oxidized green and amber, the rope-bridges strung between the settlement's terraces catching the first light like threads of a web built by something enormous and unhurried. Rethol carried the water — four sealed pouches of freshwater from the glasswort-tested pools near the Nearmarsh — and Thessa carried the instruments: a weighted line for measuring channel depth, a collection of empty vesh pouches for samples, and a bound copy of the margin notes she had transcribed from the Ledger over the past four months.

The shelf stretched ahead of them. They crossed the Nearmarsh in two hours — through the shelf-gardens, the air heavy with chlorophyll and that salt-sweetness that existed nowhere else, the turned-earth smell of soil that had been ocean floor before it was farmland. Tark grazed at the garden edges, their webbed hooves printing the dark earth. Families worked the rows without looking up. The harvest was coming in, the urgency of late Thalwei sharpening every gesture. No one had time for two young people walking the wrong direction.

They crossed the Whaleback by midday. The mineral pools shone in viridian and rust-orange, a few still showing the pale violet of thursa's blush in their thinnest crusts. The air turned dry and sharp, faintly sulfurous. Rethol stopped at the edge of the last ridge and looked out.

"That's the Farflat?"

Thessa nodded.

It did not look like much. From the Whaleback, it was an expanse of grey-blue clay stretching to the Glint, level as water, broken only by the occasional shadow that might be a formation cluster or might be a trick of the heat. The sky pressed down on it. The air above it shimmered. There was no sound except the wind, and the wind itself seemed diminished, as though it had traveled so far across unbroken flatness that it had forgotten what it was doing.

"The shelfwalkers walk this for three days?" Rethol said.

"The shelfwalkers walk further than we're going."

He looked at her with the expression she knew well — the younger brother's mixture of admiration and exasperation, the face of someone who has spent his life watching his sister disappear into books and archives and come back with information no one requested.

"What are we looking for, Thessa?"

"The formations at dusk."

"And what will we hear?"

"That's what I need to find out."

They walked.

---

The Farflat defeated conversation. It was not hostile — it was simply so vast and so level and so uniform that the human voice, which had evolved to bounce off walls and ceilings and the bodies of nearby listeners, seemed to dissipate into the air like water poured on hot stone. Rethol tried, for the first hour, to maintain the walking-chatter that shelf families used to pace long crossings — not a vetho, which was formal and navigational, but the loose, rhythmic commentary of people covering distance. He named what he saw: the clay, the salt crust, the occasional crevice where threadtails swam in standing water. After an hour, he fell silent, and the silence took them.

They walked through the afternoon. The clay was hard underfoot, baked by five months of sun, cracked in polygonal patterns that repeated at every scale — arm-width cracks containing hand-width cracks containing finger-width cracks, all the way down to the hairline fractures you could feel through your shoe-soles if you stood still long enough. The surface was not quite flat. It rose and fell in swells so gradual you only noticed them from the way the Glint appeared and disappeared at the horizon, a razor-line of light winking like an eye.

Late afternoon. The first formation cluster appeared at a distance that was difficult to judge — the Farflat's featurelessness confused depth perception, and Thessa caught herself thinking the formations were small and close when they were large and far. They resolved slowly as she walked: cylindrical shapes, two to three meters tall, their upper surfaces concave, collecting the residue of old rain in shallow basins that had evaporated to mineral crusts. Between them, channels — shallow troughs in the clay, a hand's width wide, connecting formation to formation in a network that branched and merged and branched again.

Rethol reached the first one before she did. He put his hand on it.

"It's warm," he said. "Warmer than the clay."

Thessa touched the surface. He was right. The formation held heat differently from its surroundings — a denser stone, a different mineral composition, a thermal mass that absorbed the day's sun and would release it through the evening. She ran her fingers down the side and found the worm-tubes: narrow openings, each the diameter of her smallest finger, bored into the calcium-ite aggregate in spiraling patterns. The openings were crusted with the shells of the tube-worms that had built them — delicate, calcified structures, some intact, some crumbling.

She leaned close and looked into a tube. It went deeper than the lamplight would have carried, twisting as it descended, its walls smooth with the patient architecture of creatures that built by secretion, adding layer upon layer over years she could not estimate.

"These are the channels the sound comes through," she said. "Water pressure in the tubes — when the temperature drops at dusk, the stone contracts, the channels tighten, and water trapped in the deeper tubes is forced through the narrower openings."

"That's what makes the singing?"

"That's what makes the singing."

Rethol looked at her. "So what would make it later?"

Thessa knelt beside the formation. She had thought about this for months — in the archive, in the cold amber light, turning the pages of the Ledger and reading the margins the way her mother had taught her to read the shelf. Not the surface but the pattern beneath.

"Two things," she said. "Either the stone itself is changing — contracting less, holding heat longer, delaying the pressure differential. Or the channels are changing."

She lowered the weighted line into one of the tubes. It dropped a meter, caught, and stopped. She pulled it back and examined the weight. A chalky white residue coated the lead, and mixed into it, unmistakable, was a smooth streak of calcium-ite — the same aggregate the formations were built from, partially dissolved, redeposited as a paste.

Vaskrel.

Thessa sat back on her heels. The residue on the weight was the calling card of the marrow worm — the enzymatic dissolution of calcium structures, the signature of the one creature on the shelf that felt like an enemy. The vaskrel did not eat the formations in any dramatic sense. They did not topple them, did not crack them, did not leave visible damage. They bored through the base material and dissolved the internal architecture — the worm-tubes, the channels, the intricate networks through which water pressure produced sound. They widened what was narrow. They smoothed what was textured. They ate the throat of the instrument and left the body standing.

"The channels are wider," she said, half to herself. "Wider channels mean lower pressure differentials. Lower pressure means the stone has to cool further before the singing starts. And it was already marginal — the singing was always a product of precise conditions. Precise channels, precise temperatures, precise water levels in the tubes."

Rethol crouched beside her. He did not fully understand what she was saying — she could see that — but he understood the tone. The tone was the one their mother used when she found dimpling in the shelf-ground near their summer settlement, that gentle subsidence that meant something below was eating the substrate. The tone that meant: *this is happening, and it is not going to stop.*

"So the vaskrel are eating the singing," he said.

"They're eating the channels through which the singing happens. The formations will stand for centuries — the calcium-ite is too dense, too massive. The vaskrel can't dissolve a structure this size. But the worm-tubes — the fine architecture, the tubes within tubes, the openings — those are the right scale for them. That's the calcium they can process."

"And no one's noticed because—"

"Because the oscillation holds." She heard Pallav's voice in her own. "The rhythm is stable. The shelf provides. The formations are peripheral, and the singing is subjective, and the marginal notes are the romantic musings of water-keepers who spent too long on the far shelf."

The light was changing. The sun, enormous and horizontal over the Farflat, had dropped to the angle where the clay turned from grey-blue to a deep amber and the shadows of the formations stretched east in long dark fingers. The temperature began to fall. Not quickly — the Farflat held heat like a stone hearth — but perceptibly, the dry air releasing what the clay retained, the sky darkening at the edges.

They waited.

---

The singing began an hour after sunset.

Thessa had expected it. She had timed it against the margin notes, against the latest observation in the Ledger — *Almost did not hear them. Had to go further out* — and she had calculated that at this distance, perhaps four hours' walk from the shelf edge, the formations should still produce enough sound to hear. She had been wrong in one particular: the sound was not quiet. It was simply different from what she had imagined.

She had expected the descriptions in the margin notes and the shelfwalker stories — *like humming through a comb, the loneliest sound in the world.* What she heard was thinner. Less resonant. A sound like breath through a gap in a closing door — not the full-throated hum that the oldest notes described but a remnant of it, a whisper where there had been a voice. The tubes were wider now. The precision was gone. What remained was an approximation, beautiful in the way that a ruin is beautiful, carrying the shape of what it had been without the substance.

Rethol heard it too. He stood motionless beside the formation, his head tilted, his mouth slightly open. The sound came from all around them — not from a single source but from the cluster, the channels connecting formation to formation carrying the pressure wave in a cascade of fading tones. Each tube contributed its note. The notes combined. The combination was no longer a chord but a scatter of frequencies, overlapping without resolving, like the last moments of a song whose singers have forgotten the key.

"That's it?" Rethol whispered.

Thessa did not answer. She was listening with the attention her mother had taught her — not hearing but reading, the way you read shelf-ground, not the surface but the pattern beneath. The singing was telling her something. The formation cluster had nine members, and she could distinguish the contributions of perhaps five — five formations whose tubes were still narrow enough, still intact enough, to produce a pressure-driven tone. The other four were silent. Their channels had widened beyond the point where evening cooling could generate sufficient differential. They stood in the dark, warm and mute, their concave tops holding the mineral crust of evaporated rain, their worm-tubes open and still.

She pulled out the weighted line again and lowered it into a tube on the nearest silent formation. It dropped freely — no resistance, no narrowing, no catch. She drew it out and the weight was coated in smooth, pale residue. The vaskrel had been here. The vaskrel were always here, in the deep shelf, where the calcium deposits were richest and the competition for substrate was nil.

"I need to check the next cluster," she said.

"Now? It's dark."

"The rethka-oil lamp will last. And I need to hear them from a distance."

They walked east, into deeper Farflat, using the fading Glint as a bearing. The clay was cooler underfoot now, the day's heat bleeding into a sky that had gone from amber to indigo to a dark so deep it seemed to have texture. The rethka-oil lamp cast a circle of warm amber around them, and beyond it the Farflat was featureless dark, and beyond that, at the very edge of hearing, the first cluster still sang — faintly, thinly, a thread of sound stretched across half a kilometer of empty clay.

The second cluster resolved in the lamplight twenty minutes later. It was larger — twelve formations, Thessa counted, arranged in a pattern that was not quite circular and not quite linear but something in between, a spiral compressed by some constraint she could not identify. The channels between them were wider than the first cluster's. She could see that without measuring. The troughs were shallow dishes now, not the narrow conduits the margin notes from three centuries ago described. She knelt and pressed her palm to the clay between two formations. It was level. Smooth. The channel had been eaten to the point where it no longer channeled.

This cluster did not sing.

They stood in its center, in the rethka-oil light, and the silence was immense. Not the silence of Velsha morning, which was a human silence, a silence of choice and ritual. Not the silence of the Last Breath, which was a biological silence, the shelf holding still before the sea returned. This was a mechanical silence — the silence of an instrument that had been dismantled from within. The formations stood around them like enormous, patient things, concave tops holding darkness, their worm-tubes open to the night air, and nothing came through them. Nothing would come through them again.

Rethol said, "They're dead."

"They're not alive. They never were. They're stone."

"The worm-tubes — the creatures that built them—"

"Are still building, in the active clusters. The tube-worms add to their structures every return. But here—" She ran her hand along a tube-opening. The rim was smooth, the calcium shell dissolved to a uniform bore. "The vaskrel ate the tube-worms' work. Dissolved the fine structures. And without the fine structures, the tube-worms can't anchor new growth. The old tubes are too wide, the surfaces too smooth. The worms need texture to attach to. When the vaskrel smooth the channels, the tube-worms can't rebuild, and without the tube-worms, the channels stay wide, and without narrow channels—"

"No singing."

"No singing."

She stood in the silent cluster and felt something she did not have a word for. Not solha — this was not loss and expected recovery. This was loss and no recovery. The formations would stand for centuries, perhaps millennia. The calcium-ite aggregate was too dense, too massive for the vaskrel to consume entirely. The shapes would endure. But the singing — the fine, fragile product of narrow tubes and precise pressures and the patient architecture of worm-colonies building inside stone — the singing was a thing that could be erased without erasing the thing that produced it.

The stone would remain. The song would not.

---

They camped between the two clusters, on a patch of clay that was neither silent nor singing but somewhere in between, a place where the first cluster's fading voice reached them as a whisper and the second cluster's silence was a pressure against the other ear. Thessa could not sleep. She lay on her back and watched the stars — the same stars that the calcium-ite objects from beyond the Glint bore on their unknown surfaces, the star charts painted on material no one could identify, found in the cargo holds of deep-keeled ships that the Tidebound had no tradition of building.

She thought about infrastructure.

The formation clusters were spaced across the Farflat at intervals that were not quite regular. Every survey she had read — Oshei din Taravel's cartography, the Long Camp records, the shelfwalker reports — described the same pattern: clusters of five to twelve, separated by distances ranging from half a kilometer to three, connected by channel networks that branched and merged and branched again. The channels were shallow, and by late Thalwei most were dry, but in the weeks after Suulin, when the shelf was still wet and the water table was high, the channels carried water from cluster to cluster in a network that covered — she had calculated this from Oshei's maps — nearly the entire Farflat.

A network. Not random formations singing independently, but a connected system in which water moved between clusters through channels, and sound propagated through the same channels, and the channels were maintained by tube-worms that built inside them, and the tube-worms were being eaten by vaskrel that dissolved the structures the worms built.

If the formations were infrastructure — if they served a function — then the function was being dismantled.

She did not know what the function was. That was the honest center of her investigation, the place where data ended and speculation began. But the margin notes told her something. The singing had been louder, once. Earlier. The shelfwalker reports from Oshei's time described formations that sang at midday, not just dusk — that produced sound whenever the temperature shifted, whenever the pressure in the tubes changed. The singing had been constant, pervasive, a feature of the Farflat that the earliest Long Campers described as overwhelming, almost oppressive, *a sound like the shelf itself was breathing through its teeth.*

That sound was failing. Cluster by cluster, channel by channel, as the vaskrel ate the fine architecture and the tube-worms could not rebuild and the delicate equilibrium that produced the singing tilted toward silence.

And the oscillation held. The rhythm was stable. The seventeen marker-stones recorded the sea's advance and retreat with the precision of seventeen centuries of careful attention, and nothing in those measurements — nothing — suggested that anything was wrong.

Thessa turned on her side and looked at the dark shape of the nearest formation. It stood against the stars like a figure standing in water, patient and still. She thought: *This is what it feels like to be right about something everyone else has decided not to see.* And then, harder: *The data is right too. The rhythm holds. And something is still changing.*

The two truths did not contradict each other. That was the part that made her chest tight. The oscillation was real. Seventeen centuries of data confirmed it. And within that stable rhythm, on a timescale the Ledger was not designed to capture, the shelf was changing in ways the primary data could not measure. The singing was not the oscillation. The formations were not the marker-stones. The vaskrel eating the channels was not the sea advancing and retreating. But they were all part of the same system — the same shelf, the same velaam, the same breathing floor — and if one part could change while the measurements showed stability, then the measurements were measuring the wrong thing.

Or not the wrong thing. The right thing, at the wrong resolution.

She did not sleep until the first cluster's singing faded entirely, sometime deep in the night, when the temperature stabilized and the pressure differentials equalized and the tubes fell silent. The silence woke her more fully than the singing had. She lay in it, listening to nothing, feeling the clay cool against her back, and knew with the certainty of a Reckoner apprentice who has spent fourteen months reading margins that she would go back to Thursa and file her report and the report would be acknowledged and the Ledger would gain one more marginal note in one more hand, and that this was both inadequate and exactly right.

Because the margins were where the truth accumulated. Not the primary data — the primary data was the oscillation, the rhythm, the heartbeat that everyone agreed was stable. The margins were where the water-keepers looked up from the main work and said: *I noticed something.* And the something accumulated, note by note, hand by hand, across centuries, until the margin was fuller than the column and no one had read it because the column was what mattered.

---

They turned back at dawn. The walk was easier in the morning — the clay was cool, the air was clear, and the Glint behind them had shifted from the east to the south as Karrin approached and the sun's angle changed. Rethol was quiet for the first hour, working through something, and then he said:

"If the formations are infrastructure, what are they for?"

Thessa had been waiting for this question. She had been asking it of herself for four months.

"I don't know."

"You have a theory."

She did. She had several. The formation channels connected to each other across the Farflat in a network that, in early Thalwei when the water table was high, carried water from cluster to cluster. The concave tops collected rain. The channels distributed it. The network reached, according to Oshei's surveys, from the inner Farflat all the way to the nehru — the threshold, the outermost shelf edge, where the continental slope dropped into deep water. A water-distribution network spanning thirty kilometers of exposed shelf, built from materials that did not occur naturally, maintained by organisms that had colonized the channels and added their own architecture to the original design.

"I think the formations managed water flow across the Farflat," she said. "I think the channels were irrigation — or drainage, or both. I think the network was designed to move water from the inner shelf to the outer shelf, or from the surface down, or—" She stopped. "I'm speculating."

"You've been speculating for months. You're just saying it out loud now."

She looked at him. Nineteen returns old, a shelf-walker's build — broad across the shoulders, narrow at the hips, the body of a person who had grown up carrying things across uneven ground. He was not a Reckoner. He would never be a Reckoner. He had their mother's gift for the practical, the immediate, the thing-in-front-of-you. But he was listening.

"The Stillwater builders made them," she said. "The first civilization — the one that built before the breathing started. They built on the outer shelf, in dry-stacked stone, flat and confident. They built for permanence. And then the breathing started, and they vanished, and between them and the groove-carvers there's the Silence — the gap where no one built at all."

"And the ships."

"And the ships." The deep-keeled ocean-going vessels in the anoxic sediment, their cargo holds full of sealed containers and star charts and the bones of people who were not Tidebound. "Someone left. And before they left, they built a network across the Farflat — a network of stone and channels and organisms that maintained itself, that sang when conditions were right, that managed water across the shelf on a schedule that matched the oscillation."

"Matched it how?"

"The singing happens at dusk because that's when the temperature drops and the pressure changes. But it also happens seasonally — the channels carry water in early Thalwei when the water table is high, and they dry out in late Thalwei when it drops. The network's activity maps onto the same rhythm as the oscillation. As if it was designed to work with the breathing, not against it."

Rethol was quiet for a while. The Whaleback ridges appeared ahead of them, low on the horizon, their mineral pools catching the sun. The air warmed. The smell of the Nearmarsh reached them — green and iron and the turned-earth richness of the shelf-gardens in their final weeks.

"And the vaskrel are eating it," he said.

"Slowly. Over centuries. The vaskrel have always been on the Farflat — they eat calcium, and the formations are the richest calcium source on the shelf. The difference is time. The tube-worms rebuild what the vaskrel dissolve, but the rebuilding is slower than the dissolution. The margin notes track the result: the singing gets later because the channels get wider because the vaskrel eat faster than the worms build."

"Can anyone stop them?"

Thessa almost laughed. The vaskrel were the shelf's own appetite, the force that dissolved bone and shell and mortar with enzymatic patience. They had emptied the far-shelf graves from below. They were eating the foundations of the Lost Builders' ruins. They were not a problem to be solved. They were a fact of the shelf, as fundamental as the oscillation itself.

"No," she said. "We can't stop the vaskrel. We can only notice what they're doing and understand what it means."

"And what does it mean?"

She did not answer immediately. They crossed the last stretch of Farflat and began the gradual rise into the Whaleback channels. The mineral pools glinted in the sun — viridian, rust-orange, the pale violet of thursa's blush fading as the summer's evaporation completed its work. Families were working the pools, scraping crystal deposits into woven baskets. Someone was singing — a vetho, the gaps deliberate, the melody older than anyone singing it.

What it meant was this: the Tidebound had built their civilization inside a rhythm they considered permanent. Seventeen centuries of data confirmed the rhythm. The oscillation held. And inside that stable rhythm, on a timescale the Ledger measured but the Ledger's keepers ignored, the shelf was losing something that might be essential — something the Lost Builders had put there, something that had survived the end of their civilization and the beginning of the Tidebound's and the long centuries of the breathing, and was now failing because the mechanism of its maintenance was being eaten from within.

The formations would not collapse. The stone would stand. But the singing — the sound that made shelfwalkers stop walking and feel, for reasons they could not name, that they were hearing the shelf itself — the singing was an artifact of precision, and the precision was being consumed.

"It means we're living inside a system we didn't build," Thessa said. "And part of the system is breaking, and we can't hear it breaking because we've defined the system as the part that isn't."

Rethol looked at her with an expression she would remember for a long time — not understanding, exactly, but the willingness to understand. The face of someone standing at the edge of a thought too large to hold all at once.

"You should tell them that," he said.

"I will."

She filed the report on the third day after their return.

The Thursa archive received it with the practiced courtesy of an institution that processes thirty reports a season — field observations, kemshara records, shelf-garden inventories, tidal summaries. Her report was noted as *Reckoner Apprentice Field Observation, Farflat Formation Cluster Survey, Late Thalwei.* The archivist — the same compressed, efficient young woman who had directed her to the Nearmarsh council months ago — recorded the submission date, assigned an index number, and filed it in the section reserved for supplementary observations. Not the primary record. Not the Ledger itself. The margins.

Pallav read it that evening. He said nothing for a long time. Then he said, "This is careful work."

"Thank you."

"It will not change anything."

Thessa looked at the Ledger, closed on its stone shelf in the archive's inner chamber. Seventeen centuries of primary data, measured and verified and cross-referenced, the heartbeat of a civilization that had built its entire existence on the certainty that the rhythm held. The rhythm held. The margins said something was changing. Both were true.

"It doesn't need to change anything now," she said. "It needs to be in the record."

Pallav nodded. He understood this — the Reckoner's understanding, the long view, the patience of a practice that measured its work not in returns but in centuries. The Breathing Ledger had been maintained for seventeen hundred returns not because any single return's data was essential but because the accumulation was. The margins worked the same way. Each individual note — *the singing came late, the singing came late, the singing came late* — was anecdotal. The accumulation was not.

Thessa opened the Ledger to the current return's entry. The tidal columns were filled in — the water-keeper's careful hand recording the day's measurements at the seventeen marker-stones. The oscillation held. The rhythm was stable. The sea would begin its return in weeks, and the shelf families would ascend, and the kemshara negotiations would commence, and the formations on the Farflat would be covered by thirty meters of cold dark water, and the tube-worms would continue their slow construction in the channels, and the vaskrel would continue their slow consumption, and when the shelf emerged next Suulin the formations would stand exactly where they stood now, unchanged to any eye, and the singing would begin —

Later.

She picked up the archivist's pen. In the margin of the current entry, in the space between the ruled columns, she wrote:

*The formations sang later this year. The farthest clusters on the inner Farflat showed significant channel degradation consistent with long-term vaskrel activity. One cluster of twelve has fallen silent entirely — channels widened beyond functional threshold. Tube-worm recolonization absent in degraded channels. Refer to supplementary observation TF-1174-3.*

She set the pen down. The ink dried in the archive's cold air, brown-black against the pale vesh, one more note in one more hand. In the margins of the Breathing Ledger, spanning centuries, different hands had noted the same observation. The formations sang later this year. Always later. Never earlier.

And the oscillation held. And the rhythm was stable. And the shelf provided.

And in the margins, the silence grew.
