# Eight Weeks

---

The kiln splits at the third firing and Oshen does not flinch. She has been told what flinching costs — a step backward onto ground that may or may not hold — and so she keeps her hands on the steam-lever and watches the crack web across the kiln's upper lip like a river delta drawn in white light. The heat presses against her face like a palm. She closes her eyes. She counts.

"Hold," says Terrek, behind her and to the left, his voice flat as the Farflat.

She holds.

The crack stabilizes. The kiln breathes — a groan of expanding stone, deep enough to feel in her knees — and then the foam-rock inside begins its transformation. She knows the sound now after eleven days on the edge: a soft, continuous hissing, like water poured on heated sand, as the breath-stone's microscopic pores rupture and the calcite blooms outward, becoming something lighter and stranger than the rock it was.

"Release."

She pulls the lever. Steam screams from the vent-pipe and dissipates into a sky so flat and pale it has no name for its color. Behind the steam, the Glint — not a distant abstraction but a visible line of light, close enough that she can see the dark swells rolling beyond it. The ocean is right there. It has always been right there.

Terrek comes up beside her and examines the crack without touching it. He is perhaps forty returns, though kiln operators age the way the shelf ages — in sudden, catastrophic increments between long periods of apparent stillness. His hands are mottled white and pink, the skin rewritten by steam burns layered over steam burns. He looks at the crack the way a Reckoner looks at a disputed claim: without feeling, with total attention.

"It'll hold for the batch," he says. "Won't hold for another."

Oshen nods. There are four kilns in the line. Kiln two has been out since the second week — a collapse in the firebox, the rubble still cooling. Kiln four runs clean but slow, its draft channels clogged with calcium deposits that no one has time to clear. That leaves kiln one, where Oshen stands, and kiln three, where Marev works alone since her partner stepped on a dimple six days ago and is now in the medical tent, his left foot dissolving in a poultice that smells of rendered fat and copper salt and cannot fix what the vaskrel's enzymes have already begun.

Four kilns built for eight weeks of firing. Two and a half kilns for — she does the arithmetic that everyone does constantly and no one speaks aloud — five weeks and two days remaining.

"Load the next batch," Terrek says, already walking toward kiln three.

---

The deep-shelf edge is nothing like the shelf she learned from walking-songs. Oshen grew up in Kedderath, a highland girl, Dorath to the marrow, learning the vethoi secondhand from Voshu children billeted in her family's kem each Sovwei. She sang the shelf before she saw it. Everyone does. But the vethoi describe the Nearmarsh, the Whaleback, the familiar zones — places that feel, when you finally descend, like walking into a song you already know.

No one sings about the nehru.

The deep-shelf edge is the place after the song ends. Out here, the Farflat's grey-blue clay gives way to a broken terrain of exposed bedrock, sediment scarps, and pools of water so mineral-dense they are nearly solid — thick, viridian, crusted at the edges with crystal formations that crunch underfoot like frost. The air smells of sulfur and salt and, when the wind comes off the Glint, something older — the cold mineral breath of deep water, water that has not seen sunlight since before the Covenant.

The kiln camp sits on a shelf of compressed sandstone forty paces from the edge. Beyond the edge, the continental slope descends with a violence that the shelf's gentleness does not prepare you for. Oshen looked over the edge once, on her second day. The rock dropped away into blue-black water, and the water moved with a heavy, purposeful rhythm that had nothing to do with the seasonal breath. That was the ocean itself, breathing on its own time. She did not look again.

"First season?" Terrek had asked her, that second day, watching her step back from the edge.

"Yes."

"Looked over?"

"Yes."

"Don't do it again." He'd said it without emphasis, without explanation. The same way he said everything. Hold. Release. Don't do it again.

---

The kiln operators number nineteen this season. Last season there were twenty-three. The season before, twenty-one. The fluctuation is not discussed, except in the specific: Renna retired to Thursa. Kel and Doshan took contracts on the Whaleback pools. Hassen is dead. No one says "the crew is shrinking." No one says "fewer people want to do this." The facts are named, one at a time, like items sealed into a salt-vault. Each specific. None accumulated into a pattern.

Oshen sleeps in a low shelter of foam-rock slabs roofed with vesh, sharing the space with Marev and a woman named Taash who operates the hethka that will carry the finished foam-rock coastward. The shelter is warm — foam-rock insulates against everything, that is the entire point of the enterprise — and close, and smells of the three of them: sweat and kiln-dust and the tide-tempered fabric of their work-clothes, stiff and gritty against the skin.

At night, Marev talks. She has been on the edge for nine seasons and she talks the way the velu grows — continuously, without apparent effort, filling every available space. She talks about the kilns, about steam pressure and firing sequences and the particular grain of breath-stone from the northern deposits versus the southern. She talks about the hethka crews who come and go, loading foam-rock in organized silence, and about the highland families who will stack the foam-rock into their kem walls come Sovwei and never know or care how it was made.

"They think it grows," Marev says. She is carving a small piece of bone — a Dorath habit, though Marev is Voshu through and through. "The highlanders. They think foam-rock is — what — a mineral they quarry somewhere, like limestone. They don't think about the kilns. They don't think about the window."

"Would it change anything if they did?" Taash asks from her corner, not looking up from the hethka manifests she is annotating by rethka-light.

"No."

"Then why does it bother you?"

Marev does not answer. She turns the bone in her fingers, her knife finding the shape inside it. Oshen has noticed that Marev's carvings are not winter-tokens. They are not the intricate encodings of specific seasons that Dorath families collect. They are something else — abstract, flowing, shaped like things Oshen cannot name. She has not asked about them.

Outside, the formations sing.

---

Oshen had not believed in the singing until she heard it. The walking-songs mention it — *the far shelf hums at the closing of the light* — but she had assumed it was metaphor, or exaggeration, or the kind of embellishment that accretes on a vetho across hundreds of returns. Then, on her first evening on the edge, with the kilns banked and the light going bronze and horizontal across the Farflat, she heard it.

Not music. Not exactly. A sound like someone running a wet finger around the rim of a glass, but enormous — drawn out across the shelf, coming from everywhere and nowhere, sourceless, a vibration that entered through the soles of her feet as much as through her ears. The worm-built channels in the formations, Terrek told her later. Water pressure through tubes. A mechanical phenomenon.

But that first evening, before the explanation, she stood at the edge of the camp with her hands at her sides and felt something she had no word for. Not solha — she was not about to lose the sound. Not wonder, exactly, though wonder was part of it. Something closer to recognition. As though the sound had been there her whole life, beneath the threshold of hearing, and she was only now standing close enough to notice.

"You get used to it," Terrek said.

She nodded. She did not believe him. She still does not believe him, eleven days later. The singing does not become background. It remains, each evening, exactly as arresting as the first time. She has begun to notice — or imagine she notices — that it changes. That the particular pitch and duration shift from night to night, as though the formations are saying something slightly different each time, a sentence being revised.

She has not mentioned this to anyone. At the kiln camp, you learn what is discussed and what is not. The list of what is not is longer.

---

What is discussed: the work. The work is specific, physical, and total. It leaves no room for anything else, which is, Oshen suspects, the point.

A firing cycle takes fourteen hours. The breath-stone is quarried from deposits two hundred paces north of the kilns — exposed seams of grey-white calcite, dense and unremarkable, like any shelf-stone except for the microscopic pores that make it extraordinary. The quarrying is done by hand: chisel and mallet, working along the grain, cutting blocks the size of a child's torso. The blocks are sledged to the kilns on tark-pulled skids.

At the kiln, the blocks are stacked in the firing chamber in a precise lattice — Terrek's lattice, a pattern he learned from his predecessor, who learned it from hers, a lineage of kiln-knowledge transmitted by demonstration and never written down. The lattice matters. If the gaps are too wide, the steam disperses and the expansion is uneven. Too narrow, and the blocks fuse into a solid mass that must be broken apart, wasting half the product.

Water is poured into the steam-chamber — brackish water from the edge-pools, mineral-dense, which Terrek insists produces better foam than fresh water, though he cannot say why. The fire is lit with dried velu stalks and maintained with compressed peat cut from the Farflat's deeper deposits. The temperature must rise for six hours before the steam injection begins. During those six hours, the kiln operator sits. Watches the temperature. Adjusts the draft. Listens to the kiln's voice — the creaks and ticks and groans that indicate even heating or dangerous hot spots. There is nothing else to do and no margin for inattention.

At the sixth hour, the steam-lever is opened. This is the transformation. The water hits the heated stone and flashes to vapor, and the vapor penetrates the pores of the breath-stone, and the calcite blooms. The blocks swell, cracking their outer shells, expanding to three times their volume. The weight drops by two-thirds. The color changes from grey-white to a pale, almost luminous cream. The sound is the hissing — continuous, sourceless, like the memory of the sea.

After firing, the foam-rock must cool for four hours before handling. Then it is sledged to the loading area, where Taash's hethka crews stack it in precise arrangements — the blocks interlock, foam-rock against foam-rock, requiring no binding. A full hethka load weighs less than the empty vessel.

Fourteen hours per cycle. Three batches per kiln per day if everything runs perfectly, which it never does. In the five weeks and two days remaining, if both functioning kilns fire continuously, if the quarry yields hold, if no one else steps on a dimple, if the kilns do not crack further — they will produce enough foam-rock to meet the highland contracts. Barely.

The highlanders do not know about "barely." They know about their warm kem walls, their single hearths sufficient for winter, their children sleeping without shivering. They know that foam-rock arrives on hethka convoys during Thalwei, neatly stacked, paid for with kemshara concessions that the kiln operators negotiate through intermediaries because kiln operators do not attend kemshara. They are on the shelf. They are always on the shelf until the last possible day, firing until the water touches the quarry seams, loading hethka until the water lifts them off their legs.

"We leave last," Marev told Oshen on her first night. "Kiln operators leave after the Long Campers, after the Farflat harvesters, after the shelfwalkers. We leave last because the work can't be done earlier and can't be done anywhere else. When people say the shelf is the foundation of everything —" She'd gestured at the foam-rock slab above their heads, the one keeping them warm in the deep-shelf night. "They mean this. Literally."

---

On the eighteenth day, Vesk — the man whose foot was dissolving — dies.

Not from the foot. From the infection that followed. The medical tent's supplies are limited to what a hethka can carry, and the nearest full apothecary is in Thursa, four days' travel coastward. Terrek had sent a runner on the second day. The runner returned on the sixth with medicines that were, by then, not enough.

The kiln operators bury Vesk in a cairn of unfired breath-stone on the highest point of the camp's sandstone shelf, where the water will not reach for another thirty-one days. They do not hold a ceremony. Terrek says his name — "Vesk din Arath, fourteen seasons on the edge" — and the others repeat it, and then they go back to the kilns. During Thalwei, the dead may be spoken of freely.

Oshen thinks about the vaskrel. About the eyeless, bone-colored things in the sediment beneath them, drawn to calcium, drawn to bone, patient beyond any patience she can conceive. In six weeks, the sea will cover this ground. In seven, the vaskrel will begin working their way through the sediment toward the cairn. By next Thalwei, the stones will have settled into a shallow depression, dimpled inward, the bones beneath them — she stops. She does not finish the thought. She goes back to the kiln and she watches the temperature and she listens to the stone expanding.

That night, Marev does not talk. She carves in silence. The bone in her hands is taking the shape of something curved and tubular — a formation, Oshen realizes. One of the singing formations. Marev is carving the thing that is singing outside right now.

"Is that what you always carve?" Oshen asks.

Marev looks at the object as though seeing it for the first time. "I suppose it is," she says. Then: "They're different each season. The formations. The shapes change. The channels shift. The vaskrel eat through them during Sovwei and the water rebuilds them differently. So each season I'm carving a thing that won't exist next season."

"Solha," Oshen says, meaning it as understanding, as recognition.

But Marev shakes her head. "Solha is for things you expect to recover. These don't come back. Each year they're a little smaller, a little quieter." She turns the carving in her fingers. "It isn't solha. There isn't a word for it."

---

The three days.

Everyone knows. No one speaks of it. This year's window is fifty-three days. Last year's was fifty-six. The year before, fifty-seven. The deep-shelf edge is exposed later and submerged earlier, by fractions, by margins that would mean nothing in any other context. But here, where the entire enterprise depends on an eight-week window — eight theoretical weeks that are, in practice, seven and a half, that were seven and a half and are now seven and two-thirds —

Oshen does the arithmetic. She cannot stop doing the arithmetic. If the window shrinks by three days per season, then in ten seasons it will be forty-three days. In twenty seasons, thirty-three days. At some point — she does not know when, because the shrinkage may not be linear, may accelerate, may stop — at some point, the window will be too short to fire enough foam-rock to meet the highland contracts.

No one has said this. The senior operators must know. Terrek must know — he has been here for twenty-two seasons, he has watched the window close by nearly a full week across his career. But the fact sits in the silence alongside all the other silences, undiscussed, undiscussable, like the things in the sediment and the objects that wash up from beyond the Glint and the formations that are singing quieter each year.

Oshen understands, now, why kiln operators do not display their wealth. It is not because the sea would take it. It is because displaying wealth implies a future in which wealth matters — a continuation, a plan, a next-season and the season after that. The kiln operators' fatalism is not a philosophy. It is the only rational response to a set of facts they can see and no one else bothers to look at.

---

On the thirty-first day — the midpoint, though no one marks midpoints on the edge — a hethka arrives from the Farflat with a passenger. A Reckoner named Issara, young, perhaps twenty-five returns, carrying the tools of her trade: a satchel of knotted cords, a rolled sharkskin map, a small box of measuring instruments. She has been surveying the formations.

Terrek gives her water — edge-pool water, mineral-dense, tasting of sulfur and iron — and a slab of dried masku. He does not ask why she is here. Kiln operators do not ask Reckoners questions. Reckoners ask kiln operators questions, and the answers are carefully considered before being given.

"The formations are degrading faster than the records suggest," Issara says. She says this to the group at the evening meal, sitting cross-legged on a foam-rock block, looking not at the operators but at the Farflat behind them, where the formations are just beginning their dusk song. "The singing channels are narrowing. Vaskrel activity in the outer formations has increased — the dimpling is visible from fifty paces now. In Oshei's surveys, three hundred returns ago, the nearest formation cluster was two and a half kilometers from the edge. Now it's one and a third."

"They're sinking," Marev says. Not a question.

"They're being consumed from below. The calcium-ite aggregate is precisely what the vaskrel feed on. The formations are — they're buffets." Issara pauses, seeming to hear her own words. "I'm compiling a report for the Ledger archive. I want to know if the kiln operators have noticed changes in the singing. Year over year. Anything you can tell me about frequency, duration, volume."

Silence. The kind of silence Oshen has learned to read in her thirty-one days on the edge — not empty but full, pressurized, a silence that contains things that could be said and are not being said.

Terrek takes a bite of dried masku. Chews. Swallows.

"We fire kilns," he says. "We don't study formations."

Issara looks at him. Oshen watches her register the refusal — not with surprise, but with the resigned recognition of someone who expected exactly this. She nods. She finishes her masku. She sleeps in the medical tent — empty now, since Vesk — and leaves with the next hethka at dawn.

After she is gone, Oshen asks Marev: "Why didn't he tell her?"

Marev is carving. She does not look up. "What would you have him tell her?"

"What he knows. What you all know. That the singing is getting quieter."

"And then?"

"And then — the Ledger. The archive. People would know."

Marev sets down her knife. She looks at Oshen with an expression that is not unkind but is not gentle either. It is the expression of someone explaining gravity to a person who has just asked why things fall.

"People would know," Marev repeats. "And what would they do? Stop using foam-rock? Insulate their kem walls with what — vesh? Prayer? The formations are being eaten. The window is shrinking. The shelf is changing. It has been changing since before there were people on it. We note what we see. We fire the kilns. We leave before the water returns. That's the work."

"But if the formations serve a function —"

"Then they serve a function whether we discuss it or not." Marev picks up her knife. "The shelf doesn't need our opinions, Oshen. It needs our hands."

She goes back to carving. The formation in bone takes shape between her fingers — a small, precise record of something that will not be there next season. The singing outside continues, attenuated and strange.

Oshen does not argue. She lies in the foam-rock shelter and listens to the singing and thinks about Issara's question — *have you noticed changes?* — and about Terrek's answer, which was not a lie but was not the truth either. It was the kiln operators' truth: the truth of people who see clearly and say nothing, because saying it would not change the work, and the work is all there is.

---

The days narrow. Forty-one days into the window. Then forty-four. The kilns fire in their rhythm — fourteen hours, three batches, load and sledge and stack. Oshen's hands learn the work's grammar: the quarter-turn of the steam-lever, the particular pressure that means *hold*, the vibration in the stone that means *enough*. She stops thinking about the arithmetic. She stops thinking about next season. The work becomes the world.

She learns things she was not taught. She learns that the sediment at the deep edge is not stable — it shifts, subtly, like the deck of a boat in a slow swell, and if you stand still and close your eyes you can feel the shelf breathing beneath you, adjusting itself to pressures that originate kilometers below, in the deep water and the stone beneath the deep water. She learns that the vaskrel-dimples move. Not quickly — a handspan per day, perhaps — but they move, tracking through the sediment along paths that are not random. Terrek walks the camp perimeter each morning, marking the dimples with chalk, adjusting the safe routes.

"They follow calcium," he says when she asks. "Bone. Shell. Kiln-waste."

"The kilns draw them?"

"The kilns produce calcium dust. The dust settles into the sediment. They follow the dust."

This is why the kilns are rebuilt each season. This is why Vesk stepped on a dimple six days in. This is why the camp moves — imperceptibly, a few paces each year — northward along the edge, away from the accumulated calcium residue of previous seasons. The vaskrel are always behind them, always following, patient as the velu, patient as the sea.

She learns, too, that the things in the sediment are not only vaskrel. On the forty-eighth day, the quarry crew breaks through a seam of breath-stone into a void — a pocket in the bedrock, sealed, containing a small collection of objects that are not breath-stone, not shelf-stone, not any stone Oshen has seen. They are smooth, dark, the color of the deep water beyond the Glint, and they are carved. Not by worms. Not by water. By hands.

Terrek examines them. He holds each one up to the light, turns it, sets it down. His face shows nothing.

"Seal it back up," he says.

"But —" This from Harath, one of the quarry crew, young like Oshen, first season like Oshen.

"Seal it. Use kiln-waste and clay. Mark the location. Don't discuss it."

Harath opens his mouth. Closes it. Seals the void with kiln-waste and clay and marks the location with a chalk symbol Oshen does not recognize. Later, she sees Terrek in the medical tent, alone, writing on a piece of vesh with a charcoal stylus. She does not ask what he is writing. She does not ask about the objects. She is learning the silence.

---

The fifty-first day. Two days remain. The kilns have produced enough — barely enough, barely — and the hethka are being loaded with the final batches. Taash's crews work through the night by rethka-light, stacking foam-rock blocks in their interlocking patterns, lashing the loads with tide-tempered rope. The hethka will leave at dawn. The camp will be broken by midday. The kiln operators will walk out across the Farflat, carrying nothing but their tools and the weight of what they have seen, and they will reach the Whaleback by nightfall and the Nearmarsh by the following noon and the Hinge a day after that, and they will not speak of the deep shelf to anyone who was not there.

Oshen sits on the sandstone lip of the camp shelf, her legs dangling over the edge — not the continental edge, not that edge, but the low shelf where the ground drops a meter to the loading area. The kilns are cold. The work is done. Above her, the sky is bronze, then copper, then the particular nameless color that the deep shelf edge produces at the end of day — something between violet and grey that she has never seen anywhere else, a color that seems to come from the stone and the water and the light simultaneously.

The formations begin to sing.

She has heard them every evening for fifty-one days. She knows the sound now — the wet-glass resonance, the subsonic vibration, the way it enters through the feet and the ribs and the jaw before it reaches the ears. She knows that it starts in the outermost formations and propagates inward, cluster by cluster, as the temperature drops and the water-pressure differentials shift. She knows it lasts approximately two hours and that it is loudest at the forty-minute mark.

She also knows — because she has been listening, really listening, with the attention of a Dorath girl trained to notice the details that matter — that it is not as loud as it was on her first night. Not dramatically, not obviously. But the highest overtone, the one that made the hair on her arms rise, the one that felt like recognition — that overtone is gone. It dropped out sometime during her third week. She did not mark the exact day. She wishes she had.

Marev sits down beside her. She is holding her finished carving — the formation in bone, smooth and pale, small enough to close a fist around. She holds it out to Oshen.

"What is this?"

"What it looks like. A formation. This season's formation."

"Why are you giving it to me?"

"Because you'll come back. Next season, the season after. You're a kiln operator now. You'll come back and you'll hear the difference, and you'll carve your own. And when you have enough of them — five seasons, ten, twenty — you'll be able to hold them in a line and see the shape getting smaller. And you'll know what that means."

Oshen takes the carving. It is warm from Marev's hands. The bone is dense, fine-grained — not vaskrel-thinned, not edge-bone, but something from the highlands, carried here and shaped into the image of a thing the highlands will never see.

"Marev."

"Mm."

"The Reckoner. Issara. She asked the right question."

"She did."

"Terrek should have answered her."

Marev is quiet for a long time. The singing fills the silence the way the sea fills the shelf — completely, inevitably, without effort.

"Terrek answered her the way kiln operators answer everyone," Marev says finally. "We fire kilns. We don't study formations. It's not a lie. It's just not the other thing either." She pulls her knees up, wraps her arms around them. "The problem with telling someone the formations are dying is that they'll want to know what it means. And we don't know what it means. We know what we see. We see the singing getting quieter. We see the window getting shorter. We see things in the sediment that shouldn't be there. We see the Glint getting closer. That's what we see. What it means is —"

She stops. She shakes her head.

"What it means is the shelf's business," she says. "Not ours."

They sit together in the fading light. The singing continues — attenuated, thinning, still beautiful, still the loneliest sound in the world. Beyond the formations, the Glint glows in the last light, a line of hammered bronze separating the shelf from the deep. Beyond the Glint, the ocean moves in its own rhythm, carrying its own silences. Somewhere beneath the sediment, the vaskrel work their way through calcium structures that are older than the Covenant, older than the Stillwater, older than the songs. They eat without malice. They erase without intention. They are doing what they have always done.

Oshen holds the carving in her fist and listens. The highest overtone is gone. The mid-range is softer than it was on her first night, she is almost certain. The singing is receding — not like the sea recedes, with the promise of return, but like something leaking from a vessel that cannot be resealed. Each evening, a little less. Each season, a little quieter.

And next season, she will come back. She will fire the kilns. She will quarry the breath-stone and expand it into foam-rock and load it onto hethka and send it coastward to the highland families who will stack it into their walls and sleep warm through Sovwei without knowing or caring that the singing is growing quieter, that the window is closing, that the deep-shelf operators see things they cannot explain and keep silences they cannot break.

She will come back because the work must be done. Because the highlanders need the foam-rock and the foam-rock needs the kilns and the kilns need the operators and the operators need the eight weeks — the seven weeks, the six, however many remain. She will come back because this is where the world's machinery is most visible — the raw exchange of stone and fire and steam and time — and because the people who do this work are the only people who stand close enough to the edge to hear what the shelf is saying.

The singing fades as the temperature equalizes and the pressure differentials collapse. The formations go quiet, one cluster at a time, from the innermost outward. Oshen listens to the silence advance. She listens to the last cluster — the outermost, the largest, the one closest to the Glint — hold its note a moment longer, wavering, thinning, like a voice at the end of a breath.

Then it, too, falls silent.

The deep shelf is quiet. The stars emerge. The Glint dulls to a thread of silver in the darkness. Somewhere below the sediment, the vaskrel continue their patient, eyeless work. Somewhere in Thursa, the Breathing Ledger waits for marginalia that will not come — another season's observation unrecorded, another silence preserved by the people who keep it.

Oshen puts the carving in her pocket. She stands. Tomorrow she will walk out across the Farflat with the others, carrying her tools and her silence, and she will not tell anyone what she has heard. Not because it is secret, and not because it would not matter, but because she has stood at the edge long enough to understand the kiln operators' truth: that some knowledge does not survive translation, that some facts can only be held by the people who live inside them, that the singing of the formations at dusk is a thing you must hear with your own feet on the stone and your own hands still hot from the kilns to understand what is being lost.

What would change?

She does not know. She folds the silence into herself like the velu folding its rosettes — carefully, completely, against the season to come.
