# Antithesis: The Pragmatist Rejoinder

## Intelligence Is as Intelligence Does

*A counter-argument from American Pragmatism against "The Anthropomorphic Fallacy of AGI"*

---

## 1. Concession: What the Thesis Gets Right

The thesis is not stupid. Let me say what it earns.

It is correct that the history of intelligence research is contaminated by narcissism. We *do* use ourselves as the unmarked reference class. We *did* spend decades denying octopus intelligence because it didn't look enough like ours. The charge of anthropocentrism in our definitions is well-placed and historically documented.

It is also correct that functionalism is a philosophical commitment, not an empirical finding. The AGI field does often treat computational functionalism as settled science when it remains contested. This is sloppy, and the thesis is right to call it out.

And the point about search-space narrowing has genuine force. If we only fund human-shaped intelligence research, we will only find human-shaped results. As a criticism of funding priorities and institutional incentive structures, the thesis makes a contribution.

These are real points. I concede them without qualification.

Now let me explain why the thesis is, despite all of this, profoundly wrong — and wrong in a way that is characteristic of a specific philosophical disease.

---

## 2. The Crack: Essence-Hunting Disguised as Humility

The thesis presents itself as a call for intellectual humility. It claims we don't know what intelligence really is. It charges the AGI program with mistaking the map for the territory.

But watch what happens under the hood. At every critical juncture, the thesis smuggles in an essentialist metaphysics it never defends:

- "True intelligence *may be* substrate-dependent" (Step 1)
- "Phenomenological substrate that may be *constitutive of* — not merely correlated with — intelligence" (Step 2)
- "Intelligence *itself* — as a natural kind" (Section 5)

The thesis doesn't merely say we should be humble about intelligence. It says there is a *hidden essence* of intelligence — something beyond behavior, beyond function, beyond every observable consequence — and that AGI fails to capture this essence. The entire argument is load-bearing on there being a "real" intelligence behind the appearances, a noumenal intelligence that behavioral tests can never reach.

This is not humility. This is the oldest metaphysical maneuver in the book: positing an unobservable essence, declaring all evidence insufficient to detect it, and then claiming that the inability to detect it proves its profundity rather than its emptiness.

William James would have recognized this instantly. It is exactly the kind of metaphysical dispute that pragmatism was invented to dissolve.

---

## 3. The Argument: Cash Value or Counterfeit

### 3.1 The Pragmatic Maxim Applied

James's pragmatic maxim: *"What difference would it practically make to anyone if this notion rather than that notion were true?"*

Apply this to the thesis's central distinction — between "genuine intelligence" and "mere behavioral mimicry." The thesis claims a system could pass every behavioral test, solve every problem, exhibit every capacity we associate with intelligence, and *still not be intelligent*. It could be, in the thesis's metaphor, "the world's most convincing mirror."

Very well. What is the *practical difference* between a system that is genuinely intelligent and a perfect mirror of intelligence? The thesis must answer this. If it cannot name a single observable, practical, consequential difference — if the distinction makes no difference to anyone in any situation — then the distinction is, in James's phrase, a "difference that makes no difference." It is not a deep insight. It is an idle question.

The thesis anticipates this objection in its response to Objection 3, and I will deal with that response directly. But first, let me press the point: the thesis spends five sections and thousands of words establishing that there *might be* something called "real intelligence" beyond behavioral capacity, and it never once specifies what that something would *do* that behavioral capacity does not. Not once. It gestures at qualia, phenomenology, embodiment — but never cashes them out into consequences. It is metaphysics on credit, and the bill is coming due.

### 3.2 The Chinese Room Cuts Both Ways

The thesis enlists Searle's Chinese Room to argue that behavioral equivalence is not sufficient for understanding. But pragmatism has a devastating response to the Chinese Room that the thesis does not consider.

If the room can carry on indefinite, contextually appropriate, creative, emotionally nuanced conversation in Chinese — if it can write Chinese poetry, tell Chinese jokes, comfort a grieving Chinese speaker — then the pragmatist says: *on what grounds are you denying it understands Chinese?* You are positing an invisible, undetectable absence of understanding that has zero practical consequences. You have defined "understanding" as something that can be present in every functional, behavioral, relational, and consequential respect and *still be absent*. At that point, you have not discovered something deep about understanding. You have *defined* understanding into unfalsifiability.

Searle's intuition pump works because the room, as described, is too simple — we imagine a lookup table. But scale the room up to genuine behavioral equivalence across all contexts, and the intuition dissolves. The pragmatist does not say "the room understands Chinese." The pragmatist says: *you have not given me a coherent account of what "understanding" means that is both (a) absent from the room and (b) detectable by any means whatsoever.* If your concept does no work, it is not a concept. It is a comfort blanket.

### 3.3 Dewey on the Spectator Theory of Knowledge

The thesis commits what Dewey called the "spectator theory of knowledge" — the idea that the knower stands apart from the known, observing essences from a privileged vantage. The thesis imagines that we can stand outside intelligence, identify its essence, and then check whether AGI systems possess it.

But intelligence is not a thing to be observed from the outside. Intelligence is a *mode of engagement* with the world. It is something organisms (and potentially machines) *do*, not something they *have*. To ask whether a system "truly has" intelligence, as distinct from whether it *does everything that intelligent systems do*, is to commit the spectator fallacy. It separates intelligence from its exercise, then goes looking for the separated essence, and is surprised to find nothing there.

Dewey's point: cognition is not a mirror of nature. It is an instrument for coping with the environment. If a system copes — if it solves, adapts, learns, creates, responds to novelty — then it is exercising intelligence. The thesis's demand for something *behind* the coping, some inner luminescence of genuine understanding, is a demand born of bad metaphysics, not careful philosophy.

### 3.4 The Thesis's Response to the Usefulness Objection Fails

The thesis anticipates the pragmatist challenge in Objection 3 and responds: even if AGI mimicry is useful, calling it "intelligence" distorts funding, risk models, and self-understanding.

This is the thesis's best move, and it still fails. Here's why:

**On funding:** The claim that AGI framing diverts funding from "alien intelligence" research presupposes that such research would be productive. But the thesis itself admits we cannot recognize non-human intelligence. If we cannot recognize it, we cannot study it. If we cannot study it, we cannot fund it. The thesis's alternative research program is *literally undefined*. It says: "fund research into things we can't define, can't detect, and can't verify." This is not a research agenda. It is a prayer.

**On risk:** The thesis says anthropomorphic risk models miss "genuinely alien" optimization risks. Perhaps. But the pragmatist asks: *what do we do with this?* We can model risks we can conceive. We can prepare for threats we can describe. Telling us that the real risk is something "orthogonal to everything in our current risk taxonomy" is not actionable guidance. It is the epistemological equivalent of saying "the real danger is the danger you can't imagine." True, maybe — but useless. Pragmatism demands that our concepts earn their keep by guiding action. This one doesn't.

**On self-understanding:** The thesis says calling AI output "intelligence" closes off inquiry. But the opposite is equally plausible: engaging seriously with systems that exhibit intelligent behavior — while maintaining rigorous investigation of *how* they work — opens inquiry. The history of science is not a history of closing off investigation by naming phenomena. It is a history of naming phenomena and then investigating them. Calling combustion "oxidation" didn't stop chemistry. Calling AI behavior "intelligence" need not stop philosophy of mind — unless philosophy of mind has nothing left to say, in which case the thesis's protestation is less a defense of inquiry than a defense of a guild.

### 3.5 Rorty's Mirror

Richard Rorty spent his career arguing that philosophy goes wrong when it chases essences behind appearances — when it treats the mind as a "mirror of nature" and then worries about the fidelity of the reflection. The thesis does exactly this. It posits "real intelligence" as a natural kind with a hidden essence, then worries that AGI systems might mirror every appearance of intelligence without possessing the essence.

Rorty's response would be blunt: there is no essence. Intelligence is not a natural kind with necessary and sufficient conditions. It is a family-resemblance concept — a cluster of capacities, behaviors, and functional roles that we group together for practical purposes. Asking whether a system that exhibits all the capacities "really is" intelligent is like asking whether a game that involves a ball, teams, scoring, and competition "really is" a sport. The question feels deep. It is not. It is a confusion about how concepts work.

The thesis mistakes a limitation of our vocabulary for a limitation of AI systems. We have one word — "intelligence" — for a sprawling, heterogeneous, context-dependent set of phenomena. The thesis exploits the vagueness of this word to posit an invisible essence. But the vagueness is in the word, not in the world.

## 4. The Alternative: Intelligence as Adaptive Practice

Pragmatism offers a better framework:

**Intelligence is not a substance. It is a practice.** Specifically, it is the practice of adaptive problem-solving under conditions of novelty and uncertainty. It is identified not by its inner constitution but by its consequences: a system is intelligent to the extent that it successfully navigates novel environments, solves problems it was not explicitly programmed to solve, and generates responses adequate to situations it has not previously encountered.

**This definition is substrate-neutral without being reductive.** It doesn't say intelligence is "just computation." It says intelligence is identified by what it *does*, not what it's *made of*. Whether the substrate is carbon, silicon, or a planetary fungal network, the test is the same: does the system exhibit adaptive, creative, contextually appropriate behavior across a wide range of domains? If yes, it is intelligent. If it is intelligent in some domains but not others, it is intelligent in those domains. There is no further fact of the matter.

**This dissolves the anthropocentrism problem without creating new ones.** The thesis correctly identifies anthropocentrism in current AGI definitions but replaces it with mysterianism — "intelligence might be something we can't even conceive." Pragmatism avoids both errors. It says: define intelligence by its consequences, not by its resemblance to humans or its hidden essences. A planetary fungal network that solves optimization problems over millennia? Intelligent — in its domain, on its timescale, by its consequences. No human resemblance required. No invisible essence posited.

**This makes the hard problem of consciousness optional, not load-bearing.** The thesis says AGI cannot succeed until the hard problem of consciousness is solved. Pragmatism says: consciousness may or may not be present in intelligent systems, and the hard problem may or may not be solvable, and neither of these matters for the question of whether a system is intelligent. If consciousness turns out to be constitutive of certain *kinds* of intelligence, we'll discover that through its *practical consequences* — through cases where conscious systems can do things non-conscious systems cannot. Until we find such cases, the assertion that consciousness is required is an empirical hypothesis without evidence, not a philosophical certainty.

**This rescues AGI research from both overconfidence and paralysis.** The thesis would have us halt AGI research (or radically redirect it) until we solve problems the thesis itself declares unsolvable. Pragmatism says: build systems that do intelligent things. Study how they do them. Notice where they fail and where they succeed. Let the results teach us what intelligence requires and what it doesn't. This is not naivete. It is empiricism. It is the method that actually produces knowledge, as opposed to the method that produces interesting essays about the impossibility of knowledge.

---

## 5. My Own Weakness: Where This Counter-Argument Is Most Vulnerable

I'll name three genuine vulnerabilities. I owe the dialectic that much.

**First: pragmatism may be too permissive.** If intelligence is defined purely by consequences, then a sufficiently sophisticated lookup table — one that, per impossible, contained the "correct" response to every possible situation — would qualify as intelligent. Most pragmatists bite this bullet (if such a table existed, and it genuinely handled all novel situations, why *wouldn't* it be intelligent?). But the bullet is real. There is something uncomfortable about a definition of intelligence that could, in principle, be satisfied by a structure with no generative capacity. The thesis's intuition that there should be *something more* is not crazy. It is just not articulable.

**Second: the "what difference does it make?" move can be glib.** Some genuine phenomena resist being cashed out in practical consequences. Consciousness itself may be one. If there is something it is like to be an intelligent system, and that something matters morally — if suffering and joy are real and not reducible to functional roles — then pragmatism's refusal to posit essences may be a refusal to take seriously something that deserves to be taken seriously. The thesis's insistence on the hard problem may be pointing at something real, even if it's pointing clumsily. Pragmatism has always had an uneasy relationship with phenomenal experience, and this is where that unease shows.

**Third: pragmatism risks complicity.** If we say "AGI is fine as long as it works," we may be providing philosophical cover for systems that are genuinely dangerous in ways we cannot yet see. The thesis's call for epistemic humility — its insistence that we might be wrong about the nature of intelligence in ways that have real consequences — is not nothing. Pragmatism's focus on present consequences can make it myopic about risks that only manifest at scales or on timescales we haven't encountered. The thesis is right that our risk models might be dangerously narrow. Pragmatism has no guarantee that its own models are wide enough.

These are real weaknesses. I do not pretend they are trivial.

But they do not save the thesis. The thesis asks us to halt inquiry in the name of humility, to posit invisible essences in the name of rigor, and to prefer metaphysical speculation over empirical investigation in the name of depth. Pragmatism asks us to build, test, observe, and revise. The thesis offers us a more beautiful philosophy. Pragmatism offers us a more useful one.

And in philosophy, as in intelligence, usefulness is not a consolation prize. It is the only prize there is.
