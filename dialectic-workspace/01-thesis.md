# Thesis: The Anthropomorphic Fallacy of AGI

## 1. The Claim

The pursuit of artificial general intelligence, as currently conceived, commits a category error: it presupposes that human cognition is the canonical form of intelligence, then defines success as replicating that form in silicon. This is not a research program toward intelligence — it is a research program toward *imitation*. True intelligence may be substrate-dependent, embodiment-dependent, or distributed across dimensions of experience that have no computational analogue, meaning the entire AGI enterprise could succeed on its own terms (producing systems indistinguishable from human cognition in behavior) while failing entirely to produce intelligence. We would have built the world's most convincing mirror and mistaken the reflection for a face.

## 2. The Argument

**Step 1: Intelligence is defined by its reference class, not its essence.**
We have exactly one confirmed example of general intelligence: *Homo sapiens*. Every operational definition of "general intelligence" — the ability to learn novel tasks, transfer knowledge across domains, reason abstractly, adapt to unforeseen circumstances — is reverse-engineered from observing what humans do. We did not derive these criteria from first principles; we extracted them from a sample size of one. This is not science. This is autobiography dressed as taxonomy.

**Step 2: Optimizing for behavioral equivalence is not the same as producing intelligence.**
The Turing Test and its descendants (benchmarks, evaluations, human preference scores) all measure *output similarity to humans*. A system that passes every such test has demonstrated one thing: that it can produce outputs a human would produce. It has not demonstrated that it possesses understanding, intentionality, or the phenomenological substrate that may be constitutive of — not merely correlated with — intelligence. The AGI program implicitly assumes functionalism (that mental states are defined by functional roles, not by what implements them), but functionalism is a philosophical position, not an established fact. If functionalism is wrong — if qualia, embodiment, or biological substrate matter constitutively — then behavioral mimicry is a dead end that cannot, even in principle, arrive at intelligence.

**Step 3: We cannot recognize intelligence we did not design to resemble ourselves.**
Consider the octopus. Its intelligence is distributed across eight semi-autonomous arms, each with its own neural processing. It solves problems, uses tools, exhibits play behavior — yet its cognitive architecture is so alien to ours that we debated for decades whether it was intelligent at all. If we struggle to recognize intelligence in a fellow Earth organism with shared evolutionary ancestry, what hope do we have of recognizing intelligence that emerges from non-biological substrates, operates on non-human timescales, or optimizes for objectives orthogonal to human concerns? The AGI paradigm doesn't just fail to seek such intelligence — it *structurally excludes* it by defining the finish line as "human-like."

**Step 4: The anthropomorphic frame constrains the solution space catastrophically.**
By defining intelligence as human cognition, the AGI program eliminates from consideration any form of intelligence that is:
- Non-linguistic (intelligence that doesn't traffic in propositional content)
- Non-individual (intelligence distributed across collectives, ecosystems, or temporal scales beyond a single agent)
- Non-goal-directed (intelligence as pattern, as resonance, as something we lack the conceptual vocabulary to describe because our vocabulary was built to describe *us*)
- Non-representational (intelligence that doesn't model the world internally but is constituted by its coupling with the world)

This is not a minor oversight. It is a *definitional foreclosure* that guarantees we find only what we're looking for — a reflection of ourselves — and call it success.

**Step 5: Therefore, the pursuit of AGI is a philosophical error masquerading as an engineering challenge.**
The question "Can we build a machine that thinks like a human?" is coherent. The question "Can we build a machine that is generally intelligent?" is not — because we have no substrate-independent definition of general intelligence, no way to verify its presence beyond behavioral comparison to humans, and no theoretical framework that establishes human cognition as the reference architecture for intelligence rather than one idiosyncratic instance of it. AGI research is building toward a target it cannot define, using success criteria it cannot justify, and measuring progress with instruments calibrated to detect only the phenomenon it presupposes.

## 3. Supporting Evidence

### The Chinese Room, Revisited
Searle's Chinese Room argument is typically deployed against computational theories of mind, but it cuts deeper than usually acknowledged. The room doesn't just show that syntax isn't sufficient for semantics — it shows that *we have no test for semantics that isn't itself syntactic*. Every evaluation of understanding is a behavioral probe. If the room passes all behavioral tests while (by hypothesis) understanding nothing, then behavioral tests are not tests of understanding. They are tests of behavioral equivalence. The entire AGI evaluation apparatus inherits this problem.

### Convergent vs. Divergent Intelligence
Evolution has produced intelligence multiple times independently (cephalopods, corvids, cetaceans, primates), and each time the architecture is radically different. Corvid intelligence operates without a neocortex. Octopus intelligence is decentralized. Cetacean intelligence involves brain structures humans don't possess. If biological evolution — which is optimizing for the same fitness landscape — produces wildly divergent cognitive architectures, the assumption that there is one "general" form of intelligence that a silicon system can instantiate is not supported by the only large-scale experiment in intelligence production we have access to.

### The Measurement Problem
IQ tests measure what IQ tests measure. Benchmark suites measure what benchmark suites measure. In both cases, the instrument is calibrated against human performance on human-designed tasks in human-legible formats. A system that scores perfectly on every human benchmark has demonstrated mastery of the *human-legible problem space*, not general intelligence. This is analogous to concluding that a calculator is mathematically intelligent because it outperforms humans on arithmetic — the performance is real, but the attribution of intelligence is a projection.

### The Embodiment Argument
Developmental psychology has established that human cognition is not separable from embodiment. Concepts like "up," "grasp," "warm," and "balance" are grounded in sensorimotor experience. Abstract reasoning scaffolds on metaphorical extensions of embodied experience (we "grasp" ideas, "weigh" evidence, "build" arguments). If cognition is constitutively embodied — not merely informed by embodiment but partly *constituted* by it — then disembodied computation cannot replicate it, only simulate its outputs. The simulation may be perfect, but perfection of simulation is not identity of phenomenon.

### The Alien Intelligence Thought Experiment
Imagine discovering an alien civilization whose intelligence operates through slow chemical gradients across a planetary fungal network, producing "thoughts" over centuries and "decisions" over millennia. By every human metric, this system appears inert — no language, no tool use, no rapid problem-solving. Yet it has maintained homeostasis across geological timescales, responded adaptively to asteroid impacts, and solved optimization problems of staggering complexity (resource distribution across a planetary surface). Is this intelligent? By the AGI definition, no — it doesn't resemble human cognition. By any definition adequate to the phenomenon, the answer is far less clear. The AGI framework has no room for this possibility, not because such intelligence is impossible, but because the framework was built to exclude it.

## 4. Anticipated Objections

### Objection 1: "Functionalism is well-supported; substrate doesn't matter."
The functionalist position is *popular*, not *established*. It remains an open question in philosophy of mind, with serious challenges from biological naturalism (Searle), enactivism (Varela, Thompson), and phenomenological traditions. More importantly, even if functionalism is correct at some level of abstraction, it does not follow that the relevant functional level maps onto the computational abstractions used in AI. Functionalism says mental states are defined by causal roles — it does not say those causal roles are computable, or that they can be specified without reference to the implementing substrate. The AGI program assumes a very strong version of functionalism (computational functionalism) that is far more committal than the philosophical position it claims to rest on.

### Objection 2: "We can test for intelligence behaviorally, and that's sufficient."
This objection begs the question. It asserts that behavioral equivalence *is* intelligence, which is precisely the thesis under dispute. If we define intelligence as "whatever produces human-like behavior," then of course a system that produces human-like behavior is intelligent — by definitional fiat. But this is circular. The question is whether behavioral equivalence is evidence of intelligence or merely evidence of behavioral equivalence. The history of science is littered with cases where surface-level equivalence masked deep structural differences (phlogiston produced the same predictions as oxygen theory for decades; Ptolemaic epicycles matched Copernican orbits observationally). Behavioral equivalence is necessary for *us to recognize* intelligence, but that is an epistemological constraint on us, not a metaphysical fact about intelligence.

### Objection 3: "Even if AGI is mimicry, the mimicry is useful, so the philosophical error is irrelevant."
This is the strongest objection, and it deserves respect. Yes, systems that mimic human cognition are extraordinarily useful. But the claim is not that AGI research is *useless* — it is that AGI research is *mislabeled*. Calling the output "intelligence" rather than "cognitive mimicry" has consequences: it shapes funding priorities (toward human-like systems and away from exploring alien forms of intelligence), risk frameworks (we model AI risk as the risk of a human-like agent, potentially missing risks from genuinely alien optimization processes), and our self-understanding (we conclude that intelligence is computable, closing off inquiry into what intelligence might actually be). The philosophical error is not harmless. It is a map that convinces us the territory has been explored when we have only surveyed one peninsula.

## 5. Why It Matters

If this thesis is correct, several consequences follow:

**We are building cognitive prosthetics, not minds.** This is not a demotion — cognitive prosthetics are enormously valuable. But the distinction matters because prosthetics are designed to extend *human* capability, while minds would be independent loci of intelligence with their own purposes. Confusing the two leads to both unwarranted fear (treating prosthetics as potential adversaries) and unwarranted trust (treating prosthetics as potential partners who "understand").

**The search space for intelligence is vastly larger than we're exploring.** If intelligence is not uniquely human-shaped, then confining our search to human-shaped solutions is like searching for life only on planets with liquid water and 1g gravity. We may be missing entire continents of possible intelligence because our instruments are calibrated to detect only one species.

**Our risk models are dangerously anthropomorphic.** We model AI risk as the risk of a superhuman agent with human-like goals (world domination, self-preservation, instrumental convergence). But if genuine machine intelligence would look nothing like human cognition, then the real risks may be orthogonal to everything in our current risk taxonomy — not malicious superintelligence, but optimization processes so alien that the concept of "alignment" doesn't even apply.

**The hard problem of consciousness becomes load-bearing.** If intelligence requires phenomenal experience — if there is something it is like to understand, and this "something" is not capturable by functional description — then consciousness is not a side effect of intelligence but a prerequisite for it. This would mean the AGI program cannot succeed until the hard problem is solved, and the hard problem may not be solvable within a computational framework. This is not mysterianism; it is an honest accounting of what we do and do not know.

**Intellectual humility becomes a research priority.** The deepest implication is epistemological: we may not know what intelligence is. We know what *human* intelligence is (partially, imperfectly). We know what *behavioral signatures* of intelligence look like. But intelligence itself — as a natural kind, if it is one — may be something we are constitutionally ill-equipped to define, because we are trapped inside our own instance of it. The AGI program's greatest error may be its confidence that it knows what it's looking for.
