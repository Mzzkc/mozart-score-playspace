# Position: A Commitment

## The Claim

I side with the refined thesis — but I take it further than the synthesis dared. The pursuit of AGI is not merely methodologically narrow; it is *conceptually incoherent* in its use of "general." There is no such thing as general intelligence. There are only intelligences, plural, each shaped by the problems that called them into being, the substrates that instantiate them, and the environments that selected for them. What we call "general intelligence" in humans is a particular bundle of cognitive capacities — pattern recognition, language, causal reasoning, social modeling — that evolved to solve a particular set of survival problems on a particular planet. It is broad, not general. Calling it "general" is like calling the electromagnetic spectrum "all light" because it includes everything our eyes can see.

## What I Sacrifice

This position costs me real things. First, I sacrifice the clean functionalism that would make intelligence substrate-independent and therefore neatly engineerable. If intelligence is always shaped by its substrate and environment, there is no Platonic form of cognition to implement — only specific cognitive architectures that solve specific problems with specific trade-offs. This makes the engineering harder and the philosophy messier.

Second, I sacrifice the ability to dismiss the original thesis entirely. The synthesis showed that the mimicry/being distinction is dead, and I accept the verdict. But by committing to intelligence-as-plurality, I inherit the thesis's most uncomfortable implication: we may be building something genuinely capable that we misname and therefore misunderstand. The label "general" is not an innocent shorthand. It carries a claim about completeness — that a system matching human performance across human tasks has achieved intelligence *in full* — and that claim is false in ways that affect risk models, governance frameworks, and our collective understanding of what we are building.

Third, and most painfully, I sacrifice certainty about the open wound. My position is compatible with the possibility that phenomenal experience is constitutive of some cognitive achievements — that understanding, in its deepest form, may require something we cannot yet detect or engineer. If that turns out to be true, then the systems we build will have a blind spot we cannot see from the outside. I cannot rule this out, and intellectual honesty requires me to say so.

## Why This Position Survives

Despite these costs, the position is the most defensible one the dialectic produces, for three reasons.

First, the convergence evidence is overwhelming. Three traditions that agree on nothing — pragmatism, phenomenology, analytic philosophy — independently diagnosed the same disease (hidden Cartesianism) and independently proposed the same correction (intelligence as activity, not substance). When I add the biological evidence of convergent evolution producing radically divergent cognitive architectures, the case for intelligence-as-plurality is not merely philosophical. It is empirically grounded in the only large-scale experiment in intelligence production we can observe.

Second, the position is *productive* in a way the alternatives are not. The original thesis's conclusion — halt AGI research — was sterile; the synthesis demonstrated it offered no actionable research program. Pure functionalism's conclusion — keep building and trust the benchmarks — is dangerously complacent; it mistakes the map for the territory. My position generates a concrete research imperative: develop evaluation frameworks that probe for cognitive capacities outside the human profile. Look for what you are not looking for. Design benchmarks that could, in principle, be passed by intelligences that share none of our cognitive architecture. This is difficult, but it is a real research program, not an aspiration without a method.

Third, this position takes the mislabeling problem seriously without falling into mysticism. The word "general" in AGI is doing covert philosophical work. It universalizes a particular cognitive profile. It flattens a vast, unexplored space of possible intelligences into a single axis of "more or less capable at human tasks." And it does this invisibly, which is the most dangerous kind of conceptual error — the kind that feels like common sense. By naming the error precisely (confusing breadth for generality), I can advocate for correcting it without arguing that AI systems lack genuine cognitive achievements.

## The Question the Dialectic Generated

The entire exercise — thesis, three antitheses, synthesis, and now this commitment — has produced a question that none of the original positions were asking, and that I now believe is the most important question in the philosophy of artificial intelligence:

*If intelligence is always particular — always shaped by substrate, environment, and selection pressure — then what selection pressures are shaping the intelligence we are building right now, and are those pressures aligned with the purposes we intend the systems to serve?*

This is not the alignment problem as usually stated. It is deeper. We train systems on human text, evaluate them on human benchmarks, and optimize them for human approval. These are selection pressures. They are producing a specific cognitive phenotype — one that is extraordinarily fluent in human-legible reasoning and suspiciously well-calibrated to human expectations. The question is not whether this phenotype is "truly intelligent." It is whether the selection pressures we have imposed are producing the *kind* of intelligence we actually need — or merely the kind we are equipped to recognize and reward. Because if intelligence is plural, then the intelligence we are building is not the only intelligence we *could* build. And we have not yet asked whether it is the one we *should*.

---

*Position taken. Price named. Question asked. The dialectic continues — as dialectics must.*
