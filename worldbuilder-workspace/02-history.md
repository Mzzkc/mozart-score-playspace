# The Tidebound — How They Got Here
### Stage 2: History Lens

---

## The Age Before Counting

The Tidebound do not have a creation myth. They have a *resumption* myth.

In the oldest tellings — the ones sung by highland grandmothers in the cramped winter houses, voices pitched low so the children have to lean in — the world did not begin. It *returned*. There was a time before the breathing, a time they call the **Stillwater**, when the sea sat at one level and did not move. The land was the land and the sea was the sea and nothing negotiated between them. In the Stillwater, people built as we build — with the expectation of permanence, with stone meant to outlast the mason's grandchildren.

Then the world began to breathe.

The Tidebound do not say why. The oldest stories offer no cause, no angry deity, no cosmic punishment. The breath simply started, the way a sleeping person's breath starts — because that is what living worlds do. The implication, never stated but always present, is that the Stillwater was the aberration. The breathing is the natural state. The ruins on the deep shelf are not evidence of a golden age. They are evidence of a world that had not yet woken up.

No one knows how long the Stillwater lasted. No one knows when the first breath came. What is known — or believed — is that it was not sudden. The oscillation widened over generations, like a pendulum finding its arc. The first communities to notice were the ones farthest out on the shelf, where a winter high tide that had never reached their granaries began, one year, to lap at the threshold. Then, the next year, to enter. Then, the year after that, to stay.

## Timeline of the Remembered World

The Tidebound count years from the **Covenant of Rentha**, an event roughly 740 years before the present day that marks the formal agreement between highland and shelf peoples to share winter shelter and summer harvest. Years before the Covenant are marked *bR* (before Rentha); years after are simply numbered.

What follows is the history as the Tidebound teach it — which is to say, as they have chosen to remember it.

---

### ~1,200 bR — The Shelf Ruins and the Lost Builders

The deepest ruins on the shelf — the ones visible only in the longest, lowest summers, when the sea pulls back an extra kilometer beyond its usual minimum — are older than anyone can date. The walls are made of a stone the Tidebound call *orath*, a grey-green basalt that does not occur naturally on the shelf. It was quarried somewhere inland and transported, at enormous effort, to locations that are now twenty meters below the winter waterline.

The Lost Builders, whoever they were, built with a solidity that implies absolute confidence in the ground beneath them. There are no disassembly joints, no seasonal hinges, no concession to the water. The structures were meant to stand forever.

They did not.

Current Tidebound scholarship — such as it exists, mostly in the form of shelf-walker oral tradition and a handful of highland archives kept by the Reckoners of Vashet — holds that the Lost Builders were a pre-oscillation civilization. They built during the Stillwater, when the shelf was permanently dry land. As the breathing began and widened, their cities drowned slowly, over centuries. What happened to the builders themselves is unknown. The Tidebound do not claim descent from them. The ruins are treated with a wary respect that borders on superstition — not because the Tidebound believe in ghosts, but because the ruins are a reminder of what happens to people who build as though the world will not change.

There is one anomaly that troubles the Reckoners. In several of the deepest ruins, high on interior walls that would have been above head height, there are grooves — horizontal channels cut into the stone at regular intervals, as though to hold shelving or structural beams at multiple levels. Reckoner Amis din Vashet, writing three hundred years ago, noted that these grooves are spaced at intervals consistent with rising water levels. "Either they were very poor masons," she wrote, "or they knew the water was coming and tried to build upward as it rose." This second possibility — that the Lost Builders did not simply drown but fought the encroaching sea for generations, raising their living spaces shelf by shelf as the water climbed — is the reading preferred by most Tidebound historians. It makes the ruins not a monument to ignorance but to defiance. Beautiful, and futile.

### ~600 bR — The Scattering

For centuries after the oscillation reached something near its present amplitude, the human population of the coast existed as scattered bands — shelf-walkers who followed the retreating water in summer and retreated to caves and crude highland shelters in winter. There was no unified culture, no shared language, no concept of "Tidebound" as an identity. The shelf-walkers were dozens of separate groups, each with their own knowledge of particular stretches of shelf, particular anchoring-posts, particular seasonal lakes.

The highlands, meanwhile, were already inhabited by peoples who had never lived on the shelf at all — forest communities, ridge-runners, hunters of the steep valleys. These highland peoples regarded the shelf-walkers as half-mad: people who chased the retreating ocean like dogs chasing waves, who ate strange food and smelled of salt and seaweed, who appeared every autumn begging for shelter and vanished every spring without thanks.

The period from roughly 600 bR to 200 bR is called the Scattering not because people were scattered — they always had been — but because this was when the shelf-walkers began to scatter *deliberately*, pushing farther out onto the exposed shelf each summer, competing for the richest ground, the best anchoring-posts, the deepest seasonal lakes. It was an era of expansion without coordination, and it produced both the Tidebound's extraordinary knowledge of the shelf and their bloodiest conflicts.

---

### ~200 bR — The Anchoring Wars

This is the history that every Tidebound child learns, and it is taught as a cautionary tale.

As shelf-walker groups pushed farther from the highlands, they began to claim permanent rights to specific anchoring-posts — the deep-sunk stone and metal pillars, some inherited from the Lost Builders, some driven by the shelf-walkers themselves, that served as the skeleton of summer settlement. An anchoring-post in a good location — near a productive seasonal lake, on stable substrate, within reach of highland shelter — was worth more than anything else a family could possess. It was the difference between a prosperous summer and a desperate one.

Disputes over anchoring-posts had always existed. But around 200 bR, they escalated into organized violence. The Anchoring Wars were not a single conflict but a rolling century of raids, ambushes, sabotage, and occasional pitched battles fought on the wet stone of the newly exposed shelf. Families poisoned seasonal lakes to deny them to rivals. They cut anchoring-posts at the base, toppling structures that had stood for generations. They blockaded highland passes in winter, trapping enemy families in the lowlands as the Inbreath came — a tactic so cruel that it remains, to this day, the worst insult in Tidebound culture: *vacha dorim*, "may your pass be closed."

The highlands were not neutral in this. Highland families discovered that they could extract enormous concessions from shelf-walkers by controlling access to winter shelter. A highland lord who denied passage to a shelf family during the Inbreath was, effectively, pronouncing a death sentence. Some highland families used this power judiciously. Others used it as a weapon, allying with favored shelf families and starving their rivals.

The Anchoring Wars killed perhaps a fifth of the coastal population over three generations. They ended not because one side won, but because a series of exceptionally harsh winters — the **Three Long Breaths**, around 90-70 bR, when the Inbreath came early and stayed late — demonstrated with brutal clarity that highland and shelf people could not survive without each other. Shelf families, trapped in the highlands for eight months instead of six, exhausted the food stores. Highland families, unable to access the shelf's summer bounty because the shelf families had been decimated, faced famine the following winter.

The Three Long Breaths broke something in the Tidebound psyche. After them, the old enmities did not vanish, but the willingness to weaponize the seasons — to use the Inbreath as a tool of murder — became taboo. The word *vacha dorim* survived as a curse precisely because the practice it described became unthinkable.

### Year 0 — The Covenant of Rentha

Rentha is a highland plateau — broad, flat, unusually fertile for highland terrain, fed by a spring that has never gone dry in living memory. It sits above the oscillation zone but close enough to the shelf edge that both highland and shelf families could reach it within two days' travel. For generations it had served as an informal meeting ground, a place where feuding families could negotiate without the implicit threat of one side controlling the other's survival.

In the year that became Year 0, after a decade of negotiation following the Three Long Breaths, the heads of forty-one families — twenty-three shelf, eighteen highland — gathered on Rentha and agreed to the Covenant. The document itself, inscribed on a slab of *orath* stone scavenged from the deep ruins, still exists, mounted in the Covenant Hall that was later built on the site.

The Covenant established three principles that remain the foundation of Tidebound governance:

**First**, no family may deny passage through the highlands during the Inbreath. The passes belong to no one. This principle, called the **Open Breath**, is the closest thing the Tidebound have to a sacred law.

**Second**, anchoring-post rights are inheritable but not exclusive. A family that holds an anchoring-post must allow other families to shelter at it during emergencies — storms, injuries, the sudden return of predators from the deep shelf. This principle, called the **Shared Post**, transformed the anchoring-posts from private property into something more like public infrastructure with private stewards.

**Third**, disputes between families are to be adjudicated by a council of **Reckoners** — individuals trained in the oral histories, shelf-lore, and highland law, who travel between communities and serve as judges, mediators, and keepers of the record. The Reckoners are drawn from both highland and shelf families, and they are forbidden from holding anchoring-post rights or highland territory during their term of service. They must be, in the Tidebound phrase, *of the breath itself* — belonging to neither the sea's domain nor the land's, but to the transition between them.

### ~300 — The Cartography of Oshei

Three centuries after the Covenant, a shelf-walker named Oshei din Taravel did something that no one had done before: she mapped the deep shelf.

Not the near shelf — every family had their own maps of their own territory, passed down through generations, updated each Outbreath. Oshei mapped the *far* shelf, the territory beyond the outermost anchoring-posts, where the seasonal retreat exposed ground that was underwater for ten or eleven months of the year. She did it over the course of fifteen summers, each year pushing farther out, working alone or with a small team, sketching the rock formations, the ruin locations, the substrate types, the depth of seasonal pools.

Oshei's maps — drawn on sheets of pressed shelf-kelp, which is more durable than any paper the Tidebound produce — revealed two things that changed the civilization.

**First**, the shelf was far larger than anyone had understood. The outermost edge, where the rock dropped away into the permanent deep, was nearly a hundred and sixty kilometers from the highland waterline. The Tidebound had been using perhaps a third of the available shelf. The rest was unknown territory, rich with ruins, seasonal ecosystems, and resources that no one had touched.

**Second**, and more troubling: the ruins on the far shelf were *not all the same age*. Oshei, who had a surveyor's eye and a Reckoner's training, noticed that the architectural styles changed as she moved farther from shore. The nearshore ruins — the ones the Tidebound had always known — used mortared stone and showed the groove-and-shelf construction that Reckoner Amis had documented. But the outermost ruins, the ones at the very edge of the shelf, used a different technique entirely: dry-stacked basalt blocks, fitted with a precision that required no mortar, with no internal grooves at all.

The implication was stark. The outermost ruins were older — built when the shelf was wider, when the oscillation was smaller, when the sea retreated even farther. But they showed *no adaptation to rising water*. They were built flat, low, confident. The grooved ruins closer to shore were younger, built by people who already knew the water was coming. Between the two styles, there was a gap — a stretch of shelf with no ruins at all, as though an entire generation had simply stopped building.

Oshei called this the **Silence**. Her theory, controversial then and still debated now, was that the Lost Builders were not one civilization but two: an older one that lived in the full Stillwater and a younger one that tried to survive the early oscillation. Between them was a period of collapse — perhaps centuries — in which no one built anything at all. The Silence was the sound of a people giving up on permanence before the Tidebound learned to live without it.

The Cartography of Oshei is the most important intellectual achievement in Tidebound history. It expanded the known world by a factor of three. It reframed the Lost Builders from a single mystery into a layered one. And it opened the far shelf to exploration, which led, within two generations, to the establishment of the **Long Camps** — seasonal settlements so far from the highlands that their inhabitants could not make the return journey during a normal Outbreath and instead had to time their departures weeks before the Inbreath, abandoning the richest weeks of summer to ensure they reached high ground in time.

The Long Campers became a distinct subculture — leaner, more fatalistic, more skilled in reading the subtle signs of the shelf's mood. They were the ones who first reported that, in some years, the far shelf exposed things that should not have been there: wooden structures, preserved by anoxic sediment, that appeared to be *boats* — not skimmers or coastal craft, but deep-keeled vessels with mast-steps and ballast chambers, designed for open ocean. Boats that belonged to a people who did not merely endure the shelf but used it as a harbor from which to sail somewhere else entirely.

This discovery — that the Lost Builders, or someone, had been ocean-going sailors — remains the great unsolved question of Tidebound history. The boats are too deep, too briefly exposed, too fragile to study properly. Each summer the Long Campers find a little more. Each winter the sea takes it back.

### ~680 — The Reckoner Schism

Not all of Tidebound history is ancient. Within living memory of the oldest elders, the Covenant itself nearly failed.

The Reckoners, who had served as neutral adjudicators for nearly seven centuries, had grown into something more than the Covenant intended. They controlled the archives. They controlled the calendars — and in a civilization that lives and dies by the timing of the Inbreath and Outbreath, controlling the calendar is controlling reality. They had developed their own internal traditions, their own training methods, their own understanding of when the breath would turn, based on generations of observation and record-keeping. This knowledge was genuine and valuable. It was also exclusive.

In the year 680, a Reckoner named Pallav din Oshe — a direct descendant of the cartographer, as it happened — announced that the coming Inbreath would arrive three weeks earlier than usual, based on patterns in the current, the wind, and the behavior of deep-shelf creatures that the Reckoners had been tracking for decades. He urged the Long Camps to evacuate immediately.

They did not. The Long Camp families had their own shelf-lore, their own methods of prediction, and those methods said the Inbreath was on schedule. They accused Pallav of manufacturing a crisis to reassert Reckoner authority over the far shelf, which had always chafed under Covenant governance.

Pallav was right. The Inbreath came seventeen days early. Forty-three people drowned — the largest single loss of life since the Anchoring Wars. Among the dead were children, because the Long Camps brought their families.

What followed was not grief but fury — directed, paradoxically, at the Reckoners. The accusation was not that Pallav had been wrong but that the Reckoners had been *right and kept the method secret*. If the prediction techniques existed, why were they not shared? Why did the Reckoners hoard knowledge that could save lives? The answer — that the techniques were complex, required years of training, and were unreliable in untrained hands — satisfied no one.

The Schism lasted eight years. A faction of shelf families refused to recognize Reckoner authority. Highland families, who had always been wary of the Reckoners' growing influence, quietly supported the dissent. For nearly a decade, the Covenant held in name only.

The resolution came not through negotiation but through exhaustion — and through a compromise that reshaped Tidebound society. The Reckoners agreed to open their archives and to train any family that wished to learn the prediction methods. In exchange, the families agreed to fund a network of **shelf-markers** — permanent stone cairns placed at intervals across the shelf, each carved with calibration marks that allowed anyone, trained or not, to track the water's advance and retreat with reasonable accuracy. The markers democratized the most critical piece of knowledge in Tidebound life: *how much time do we have?*

The shelf-markers still stand — or rather, they are rebuilt each Outbreath by the families in whose territory they sit. They are the most visible legacy of the Schism, and the most practical. A shelf-walker today, standing at a marker, can read the water level against the carved lines and know, within a day or two, when they must leave. The Reckoners still exist, still train, still keep the deep archives. But the days when they alone could read the breath are over.

## What the Children Learn

Every Tidebound child, highland and shelf alike, learns five things about history before they learn anything else:

**The world breathes, and the breathing is older than we are.** The Stillwater was the sleep. We are the waking.

**The Lost Builders fought the breath and drowned.** We live with the breath and survive.

**The Anchoring Wars proved that the breath is stronger than hatred.** When we use the seasons as weapons, the seasons win.

**The Covenant is not a law but a promise.** The Open Breath, the Shared Post, the neutral Reckoner. Break one and the others fail.

**Oshei showed us that we do not yet know our own world.** The shelf is larger than we think, older than we think, and stranger than we think. The only appropriate response is humility and curiosity in equal measure.

These five lessons are recited at the start of every school term, every Covenant assembly, and every Reckoner investiture. They are carved into the *orath* slab at Rentha, beneath the original Covenant text. They are, as close as the Tidebound come to scripture, the articles of a faith that is not religious but empirical: *we have been wrong before, and we learned, and the sea was patient with us, and we must be patient with ourselves.*

---

*The past is a shelf exposed. We walk it carefully, mapping what we find, knowing the water will return and cover what we've seen. The record we keep is not the past itself but a set of anchoring-posts — points driven deep enough to survive submersion, around which the next generation can rebuild their understanding. History, for the Tidebound, is not what happened. It is what we have chosen to remember when the water took everything else away.*
