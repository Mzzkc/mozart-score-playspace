# The Tidebound: How They Live
*Stage 2 — Culture Lens*

---

## I. The Shape of Time

The Tidebound do not count years. They count *returns*.

A person is not thirty years old but "thirty returns," and the word carries a weight that the arithmetic alone does not convey — it means thirty times the shelf was exposed, thirty times the sea reclaimed it, thirty times you climbed the highland paths with your household balanced across your shoulders and thirty times you descended again to find the bones of your summer home barnacled and strange. An old woman of sixty returns has, in a sense that is not metaphorical, lived sixty lives in the same place.

Their calendar is quartered, but unequally. The two great seasons — *Thalwei* (the Dry, when the shelf is exposed) and *Sovwei* (the Deep, when the sea holds it) — are each roughly five months long. Between them lie the transition periods: *Suulin* (the Outbreath, when the water withdraws) and *Karrin* (the Inbreath, when it advances). These transitions last roughly six weeks each, and they are the most important time in Tidebound life. Not the seasons themselves — the *changes*.

The first day of Suulin, when the retreating waterline exposes the highest anchoring-posts, is called *Velsha* — the Uncovering. It is the closest thing the Tidebound have to a universal holy day. On Velsha, no one works. The highland settlements empty as people walk down to the emerging shore and stand in the cold shallows, watching the water pull away from stone that has been submerged for five months. Children are held up to see it. Old people who cannot walk are carried. There is no ceremony, no priest, no invocation. They simply watch. The silence is the ritual.

The last day before the shelf is fully submerged — *Kashren*, the Surrendering — is different. Kashren is loud. It is the final night of summer life, and it is spent on the shelf itself, on the highest *haara* platforms, with fires and music and the particular reckless joy of people who know that the ground beneath their feet will be five meters underwater by the following week. Couples who intend to marry announce it on Kashren. Debts are forgiven or called in. Old quarrels are either settled or formally carried into winter, which is a public declaration of seriousness — to carry a grudge through Sovwei means you consider it worth feeding through the lean months.

## II. Two Peoples, One Rhythm

The seed document describes the highland-shelf tension. What it does not describe — because the seed could not yet know — is how deeply this division has shaped identity.

### The Voshu: Shelf Families

The Voshu (literally "those who go down") are the families whose identity is rooted in the exposed shelf. They are engineers, farmers of the seasonal soil, readers of sediment and tide-pool. A Voshu family's wealth is not measured in land — how could it be, when the land vanishes? — but in *knowledge*. They know where the best anchoring-posts stand. They know which stretches of shelf-soil will produce grain and which will produce only salt-grass. They know the migratory paths of the shelf creatures, the safe routes through the unstable sediment of early Outbreath, the location of freshwater seeps that emerge only when the sea retreats.

This knowledge is passed in *vethoi* — literally "walking-songs," though the translation flattens them. A vetho is a navigational chant, rhythmically structured so it can be sung while walking, that encodes the features of a particular stretch of shelf. A Voshu child learns her family's vethoi before she can read. The songs describe the contours of land that she will not see until the next Outbreath, and when she finally walks the shelf for the first time, at seven or eight returns, the landscape matches the song. The experience is reportedly uncanny — a recognition so deep it feels like memory. Voshu say that a person who knows the vetho *remembers* the shelf even if they have never seen it. The shelf, they believe, is always there. The sea is the interruption.

Voshu culture is gregarious, improvisational, and profoundly visual. Their summer settlements — rebuilt each return on the same anchoring-post lattices — are painted in pigments made from shelf-minerals: ochres, vivid greens from copper deposits, a startling blue derived from a crusite that grows only on submerged stone. The settlements are beautiful and impermanent, and the Voshu take an almost competitive pride in making each return's version more striking than the last. To rebuild exactly as before is considered unimaginative. To improve is expected.

They eat communally, in open-air kitchens built on the *haara* platforms, and their cooking is based on the extraordinary marine-terrestrial ingredients that the shelf provides: salt-garden vegetables with flavors that do not exist on permanent land, fish smoked over driftwood fires, a fermented paste made from tidal-pool organisms that is, by all accounts, an acquired taste of considerable power.

### The Dorath: Highland Families

The Dorath ("those who remain") are the highland families, and they are a different civilization sharing the same language.

Where the Voshu are expansive, the Dorath are compressed. Highland life during Sovwei is a study in managed scarcity. The settlements are permanent — stone buildings, some of them very old, with thick walls and small windows and root cellars that go deep into the mountain rock. The Dorath do not paint their buildings. Stone is stone. A Dorath house announces its age by its silence: no ornament, no color, just the grain of the rock and the depth of the wear on the threshold.

Dorath social life is organized around *kem* — a word that translates poorly as "household" but means something closer to "the people who survive winter together." A kem is typically three or four related families sharing a compound of buildings around a central hearth. The kem is the unit of survival: it pools food, fuel, labor, and — crucially — space. In winter, when the highland population triples as the shelf people ascend, a kem that was comfortably inhabited by twenty people may hold sixty. This is not hospitality. It is obligation, governed by intricate agreements that are renegotiated every year during the Inbreath.

The negotiations are called *kemshara* — "household-weighing" — and they are the most politically important events in Tidebound society. Each highland kem must determine how many shelf families it will shelter, what the terms will be (labor, food contribution, behavioral expectations), and — most delicately — what happens if the winter is harder than expected and the kem's resources cannot support everyone. These negotiations happen in public, on the last days of Karrin, and they are presided over by figures called *Voicekeepers*: elderly men and women, typically from families that have deep roots in both highland and shelf communities, whose role is to remember the outcomes of past kemshara and hold the negotiating parties to their precedents.

The Dorath value restraint, precision, and silence. Their art form is not painting but carving — small, intricate objects made from highland stone or bone, with a level of detail that seems almost punitive. A Dorath carver may spend an entire Sovwei on a piece that fits in the palm of a hand. The objects are not decorative. They are records: each one encodes a specific winter, a specific kem, a specific set of negotiations and tensions and small kindnesses. A Dorath family's collection of winter-carvings is their history, held in stone that does not wash away.

They eat privately, within the kem, and their food is preserved, smoked, dried, salted — the cuisine of people who have learned, over centuries, that the distance between autumn's last harvest and spring's first growth is a distance measured in discipline.

## III. The Art of Losing

The most distinctive feature of Tidebound culture is not what they build but how they let go of it.

There is no word in their language for "ruin." The closest term — *velsha-kho* — means "that which the uncovering will show," and it carries no connotation of decay or failure. A collapsed wall on the shelf is not a ruin. It is a stage in a cycle. The Tidebound response to finding their summer structures damaged by submersion is not grief but assessment: *what remains, what must be replaced, what can be improved?*

This attitude extends beyond architecture. Tidebound children are taught a practice called *solha-making*: the deliberate cultivation of tenderness toward things that are about to be lost to the sea. In the final weeks before Karrin, families walk their summer holdings together, naming what they see — *this is the garden where the amber-root grew tall, this is the stone where we cleaned the morning's catch, this is the path the children wore between the houses* — and then they leave. Not with sorrow. With *solha*. With the specific, luminous attention that comes from knowing you are seeing something for the last time *this time*.

The practice produces a people who are, by the standards of most civilizations, startlingly present. A Tidebound artisan building a wall knows the sea will take it. She builds it well. Not because it will endure — it won't — but because the quality of the making is the only permanence available. The care is the thing that survives. The Voshu have a proverb: *"The sea remembers your hands."* It means: what you make with attention, the shelf preserves in some form — a foundation that lasts, a drainage channel that guides the next year's water, a cleared stone that is easier to build on next return. Carelessness, on the other hand, leaves nothing.

## IV. What Is Taboo

The Tidebound have few universal prohibitions, but the ones they hold are absolute.

**Anchoring-post theft.** An anchoring-post — the deep-driven stone or metal pylon that marks a family's shelf claim — is the closest thing the Tidebound have to sacred property. To damage, remove, or build on another family's post is an offense so serious that the language has a specific grammatical construction for it: a verb tense used only for acts of violation that cannot be undone. The punishment is not death or exile but something the Tidebound consider worse: *kemlessness*. The offender's kem is dissolved. They belong to no household. In winter, no one is obligated to shelter them. They survive, if they survive, on the sufferance of strangers. It is the only punishment that is truly permanent in a culture built on cyclical renewal.

**Blocking the Inbreath.** Any structure, barrier, or action that impedes the natural advance of the water is forbidden. This is partly practical — the force of the seasonal sea is irresistible, and attempts to resist it have historically ended in structural failure and death — but it is also deeply cultural. The Inbreath is not an enemy. It is a partner. To block it is to misunderstand the fundamental contract of Tidebound life: *the sea takes, the sea returns, and you do not fight either movement.* The handful of historical attempts to build permanent seawalls are remembered as cautionary stories, told with the same appalled fascination that other cultures reserve for tales of cannibalism or sacrilege.

**Speaking the dead during Sovwei.** The Tidebound believe that the dead descend with the water. Not literally — they are not superstitious about it — but poetically, metaphorically, in the way that shapes behavior even when belief has faded. The dead are under the sea, with the shelf, with the submerged world. To speak their names during Sovwei — to call them back during the season when they are meant to be at rest beneath the water — is considered a cruelty. The names of the dead may be spoken freely during Thalwei, when the shelf is exposed and the dead, in some sense, have also surfaced. But in winter, they sleep. You let them sleep.

## V. The Surprise: The Drift-Chosen

There is a third group that neither the Voshu nor the Dorath fully claim.

They are called the *Tanvelu* — the Drift-Chosen — and they are the people who refuse to leave the shelf.

Not in the way that a fool refuses to leave a burning building. The Tanvelu are expert navigators, boat-builders, and divers who spend Sovwei *on the water above the submerged shelf*. They live in houseboats — flat-bottomed, lashed together in floating compounds of eight to twelve vessels, anchored above their families' shelf holdings. They fish the winter sea. They dive to tend the submerged gardens, harvesting the organisms that grow only in the dark months. They maintain the anchoring-posts underwater, scraping them, checking their integrity, replacing the guide-ropes that shelf families will use to locate their holdings when the water retreats.

The Tanvelu are a small population — perhaps one in twenty Tidebound — but they are culturally enormous. They are the only people who experience both seasons in the same place. They watch the shelf submerge from above. They see, through the winter water, the shapes of the summer world wavering beneath them like a memory that has not yet solidified. They report what they see — which anchoring-posts have shifted, which shelf formations have changed, where new freshwater seeps have opened — and this information is desperately valuable to the Voshu families preparing for the Outbreath.

But the Tanvelu are also unsettling. They live *between*. They belong to neither the rhythm of descent and return that defines the Voshu nor the rhythm of endurance that defines the Dorath. They are people who have, in a sense, refused the fundamental bargain of Tidebound life — the acceptance of loss and recovery — by choosing never to leave. The Voshu admire them and find them strange. The Dorath distrust them and depend on them. The Tanvelu themselves tend toward a philosophical quietness, a quality of observation that comes from spending half the year watching the world through water.

Their songs are different from the Voshu's *vethoi*. Where the walking-songs describe the shelf as fixed landscape, the Tanvelu sing *veshoi* — "seeing-songs" — that describe the shelf as it appears through winter water: shifting, blue-green, refracted, alive with creatures that the shelf families never see. The *veshoi* are considered the most beautiful music in Tidebound culture and the most difficult to learn, because they describe a world that is always in motion, seen through a medium that distorts everything, and the singer must convey both the distortion and the truth beneath it simultaneously.

At the kemshara negotiations, the Tanvelu hold a peculiar status. They need no winter shelter — they remain on the water — and so they owe no kem-obligations. But they possess information that every kem needs. This gives them a quiet, lateral power that neither the highland Dorath nor the shelf Voshu fully control. The Tanvelu trade in knowledge: the state of the submerged shelf, the health of winter fisheries, the structural integrity of anchoring-posts. They trade it carefully, selectively, and with a patience that the other groups find either admirable or maddening, depending on how urgently they need what the Tanvelu know.

## VI. How They Sing

Music is the art form the Tidebound have perfected beyond all others, and the reason is structural: they cannot preserve visual art across the oscillation, but they can preserve sound.

Tidebound music is polyphonic, layered, and — most distinctively — *responsorial across time*. A Voshu singer performing a vetho at the start of Thalwei will leave deliberate gaps in the melody. These gaps are filled by the *echo-singers* — usually children or apprentices — who sing the phrases that were left incomplete at the end of the previous Thalwei. The song is never whole within a single season. It accumulates across returns, each generation adding phrases that respond to phrases laid down by the previous one. The oldest vethoi are palimpsests of hundreds of returns' worth of addition, and to hear one performed in full — with all its historical layers sung simultaneously by different voices — is, by every account, overwhelming. The sound has been described as "a chord made of centuries."

The instruments are spare. A double-reeded pipe carved from the hollow stems of a shelf-plant. A frame drum with a head made from the membrane of a tidal creature, which produces a tone so low it is felt more than heard. A stringed instrument, small enough to carry during the ascent, with gut strings tuned to intervals that sound dissonant to outside ears but which the Tidebound hear as the natural harmonics of the oscillation itself — the sound of water advancing and retreating, translated into pitch.

The Dorath, characteristically, prefer silence. Their music is percussive, minimal, performed in the close spaces of the kem during the long winter nights. The rhythms are complex — polyrhythmic patterns that interlock like the joints of their disassemblable structures — but the dynamic range is narrow. Dorath winter music is not meant to fill a room. It is meant to be heard through a wall, from the next chamber, where someone is trying to sleep and finding, in the distant tapping, a reason not to feel alone.

---

*The culture of the Tidebound is not a philosophy of impermanence. It is a practice of return — the daily, seasonal, generational discipline of letting go and coming back and finding, in the coming back, that you are both the same person and not. The shelf teaches this. The sea teaches this. The silence on Velsha morning, when an entire civilization stands in the shallows and watches the water pull away from stone, teaches this. What remains is what you carried. What you carried is what mattered. Everything else, the sea will show you again next year.*
