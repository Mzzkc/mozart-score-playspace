# The Tidebound — What Lives Here
## Stage 2: Ecology Lens

---

## I. The Biome Between

There is no word in any language of the permanently-landed peoples for what the exposed shelf is. It is not a beach. It is not a plain. It is not a marsh, though it has elements of all three. The Tidebound call it the *velaam* — the "breathing floor" — and they speak of it the way a farmer speaks of soil: with intimate, proprietary knowledge, with love that is inseparable from wariness.

The velaam is not one ecosystem but a gradient of dozens, layered by elevation, distance from the shelf edge, substrate type, and the particular history of each stretch of ground. A limestone platform four kilometers from the winter waterline, submerged under only two meters of water at peak, develops a different ecology than a silt-filled canyon twenty kilometers out, where the winter depth reaches ten meters and the water stays cold and dark for months. The Tidebound navigators who lead the first Outbreath expeditions do not simply know the shelf's geography — they know its *life*, season by season, meter by meter, the way a midwife knows the stages of labor.

What follows is not a catalog. It is an introduction to the creatures and plants that have solved the most extraordinary problem in biology: how to live in a place that ceases to exist, reliably, every year.

---

## II. Three Who Endure

### The Velu — The Shelf Garden

The most important organism on the velaam is not an animal but a plant — or rather, a colonial organism that blurs the line between plant, fungal mat, and something else entirely. The Tidebound call it *velu*, which translates roughly as "the patient green," and it is the foundation upon which the entire exposed-shelf ecosystem rests.

Velu resembles, at first glance, a dense carpet of succulent rosettes, each the size of a child's palm, growing in interlocking clusters that can cover hectares of exposed rock. The color is not quite green — it is a grey-green with an iridescent quality, like the inside of an abalone shell, that comes from a waxy cuticle evolved to shed salt while transmitting light. When the shelf first emerges during the Outbreath, the velu is already there: dormant, compressed, its rosettes folded tight as fists, crusted with a season's accumulation of barnacles and silt. Within three days of exposure to air, it begins to unfold. Within a week, it is photosynthesizing. Within two weeks, the first shoots appear — not flowers, but hollow-stemmed stalks that grow at a rate visible to the naked eye, rising a handspan per day, each one tipped with a cluster of seed-capsules that will mature in eight weeks.

The speed is the miracle. Velu has the fastest verified growth rate of any macroscopic organism in the known world, and the Tidebound's agricultural civilization is built upon it. They do not plant velu — it is always there, waiting beneath the water, patient as its name implies. What they plant *in* the velu is everything else: grain-analogues, tuber vegetables, the fibre-plants from which they make their celebrated textiles. The velu mat stabilizes the substrate, retains moisture, fixes nitrogen through symbiotic nodules in its root-mesh, and — crucially — creates a microclimate at ground level that is several degrees warmer than the ambient air, which extends the effective growing season by weeks on either end.

When the Inbreath comes, the velu does not die. It retracts. The stalks wither and fall, but the rosettes seal themselves — each one secreting a resinous coating that hardens into a waterproof capsule. Inside, the organism enters a metabolic state so reduced that it is, by most definitions, not alive. It persists through six months of submersion in cold, dark seawater, drawing just enough energy from chemosynthetic bacteria in its root nodules to maintain cellular integrity. The Tidebound have a saying: *"The velu sleeps in salt and dreams in sunlight."* It is the closest they come to a creation myth about the shelf itself — the idea that the velaam remembers its own summers, and that the velu is the medium of that memory.

### The Rethka — The Lantern Crab

If the velu is the shelf's patience, the rethka is its cleverness.

The rethka is a decapod crustacean roughly the size of a human head, with a flattened carapace the color of wet slate and, in its terrestrial phase, a bioluminescent organ on its abdomen that produces a steady amber glow. The Tidebound harvest this light — carefully, without killing the animal — by placing ceramic cups beneath rethka that have been coaxed onto wooden platforms. The secretion that produces the glow is a thick, oily liquid that the rethka exudes as a territorial marker, and it continues to luminesce for six to eight hours after collection. Rethka-oil lamps are the primary light source during Outbreath expeditions, and the particular warm amber of their light is so deeply associated with the shelf-summer that highland children who have never seen the velaam still call any golden light "rethka-colored."

But the rethka's most remarkable adaptation is its dual respiratory system. It breathes water in winter through feathery gills concealed beneath its carapace. In summer, when the shelf is exposed, a secondary set of structures — not true lungs, but highly vascularized chambers lined with a moist membrane — inflates within the gill cavity, and the rethka becomes an obligate air-breather. The transition takes approximately four days, during which the rethka is sluggish, vulnerable, and irritable. The Tidebound call this period the *rethka's confusion*, and it is a minor hazard of early Outbreath: a disoriented rethka, mid-transition, will pinch anything that comes near it with chelae strong enough to sever a finger.

In its summer form, the rethka is a scavenger and opportunistic predator, feeding on the smaller invertebrates that emerge from the substrate, on velu shoots, on carrion. It is social in a rudimentary way — rethka aggregate in groups of ten to thirty around the deeper tidal pools, which they defend collectively against larger predators. Their bioluminescence serves both as territorial marking and, the Tidebound believe, as a form of communication: the intensity and rhythm of the glow changes in ways that correlate with the proximity of threats, the availability of food, and — most curiously — the phase of the oscillation. Rethka begin to dim two full weeks before the first measurable rise of the Inbreath, which makes them one of the most reliable biological indicators the Tidebound possess. When the rethka go dark, it is time to begin packing.

In winter, submerged, the rethka is a different animal entirely. The bioluminescence ceases. The carapace softens slightly, allowing greater flexibility for swimming. It becomes a benthic scavenger, slow-moving and solitary, creeping along the drowned shelf in the lightless cold. There is some evidence — contested among Tidebound naturalists — that the rethka does not retain memory across the transition. That the summer rethka and the winter rethka are, in some meaningful neurological sense, different creatures inhabiting the same body. If true, it would make the rethka's seasonal aggregation even more remarkable: not a reunion of individuals, but a convergence of strangers who happen to know the same dances.

### The Vaskrel — The Marrow Worm

This is the unsettling one.

The vaskrel is a vermiform organism — a worm, broadly speaking, though its anatomy is sufficiently alien that the Tidebound classify it in its own category, separate from the earthworms, polychaetes, and ribbon-worms they know well. It lives in the deep shelf, the outermost reaches of the velaam, where the winter submersion is deepest and longest and the summer exposure is briefest. In those far zones, the velu grows thin and the substrate turns from consolidated rock to deep, silty sediment — the accumulated detritus of centuries of marine snowfall.

A mature vaskrel is between one and two meters long, as thick as a human wrist, and the color of old bone — a yellowish-white that is deeply wrong against the grey-green of the shelf. It has no eyes. It has no discernible head. Both ends taper to blunt points, and either end can lead. Its movement is peristaltic, a slow rhythmic clenching that drives it through the sediment the way a tongue pushes food to the back of the throat.

The vaskrel feeds on the dead. Not exclusively — it consumes organic matter of all kinds in the sediment — but it is drawn, with an efficiency that defies its apparent sensory impoverishment, to the remains of large organisms. It finds bones. The Tidebound discovered this when they began burying their dead on the far shelf, in the old custom, and found that within a single summer's exposure the graves had been thoroughly excavated from below — not ransacked, not scattered, but *emptied*, the bones drawn down into the silt with a precision that suggested intent. What remained in the graves was a fine powder with a calcium-phosphate signature, and a faint smell — sweet, cloying, unmistakable once known — that the Tidebound call *vaskrel-breath*.

The organism dissolves calcium structures enzymatically. It exudes a compound — still uncharacterized by Tidebound naturalists, though several have burned their hands learning about it — that breaks calcium carbonate and calcium phosphate into soluble components, which the vaskrel absorbs through its body wall. It eats bone. It eats shell. It eats the calcified husks of old coral formations and the petrified remnants of deep-shelf organisms. And it does so with a specificity that is, to the Tidebound, profoundly disturbing.

The vaskrel does not surface. It exists entirely within the sediment, sensing — through vibration, through chemical gradients, through mechanisms the Tidebound have not determined — the presence of calcium-rich deposits above it. It then migrates upward through the silt, sometimes from depths of three or four meters, over the course of days or weeks, until it reaches the source. The first sign of a vaskrel's approach is a faint subsidence of the ground — a settling, a dimpling, as though the sediment is being gently inhaled from below. Then the smell. Then, if you dig — and the Tidebound advise against digging — the pale, eyeless, slowly clenching body, moving through the earth like a finger through wet clay.

The vaskrel is not dangerous to living humans in any direct sense. It does not bite. It does not attack. Its enzymatic secretion causes chemical burns on skin contact, but it has no mechanism for pursuing prey. The danger is psychological and, in one specific case, structural. The vaskrel's appetite for calcium has, over centuries, weakened the foundations of the deep-shelf ruins. The massive stone walls that predate the Tidebound civilization are riddled, at their bases, with vaskrel channels — smooth-bored tunnels where the worms have dissolved the lime morite binding the stone. There is a theory, held by a minority of Tidebound scholars, that the deep ruins are not sinking because of geological subsidence but because the vaskrel are eating them from below. Eating the mortar. Eating the foundations. Eating, in the most literal sense, the bones of the old world.

The Tidebound do not bury their dead on the far shelf anymore. They have not done so for six generations. But the vaskrel are still there, patient, blind, slowly dissolving whatever the deep shelf offers them, as though the earth itself were digesting its own history.

## III. The Smaller Lives

Between the three keystone species, the velaam teems with smaller organisms that each occupy precise niches in the oscillation.

**Shelf-skippers** (*tolhu*) are thumb-sized amphipods with muscular tails that allow them to leap a full meter when disturbed. They emerge by the billions during early Outbreath, feeding on decaying velu matter, and their collective motion across the exposed shelf produces a soft, continuous crackling sound — like distant applause, the Tidebound say — that is one of the first signs of summer. They are eaten by everything, including humans. Dried tolhu, ground into a salty paste, is a dietary staple.

**Glasswort** (*sheru*) is a translucent, branching organism — technically an animal, a colonial hydrozoan — that grows in the tidal pools left behind as the water retreats. Each colony resembles a miniature, leafless tree made of clouded glass, standing ten to thirty centimeters tall. Glasswort filters microorganisms from the pool water, and its presence is a reliable indicator of water quality: pools with healthy glasswort colonies are safe to drink from after boiling. When the Inbreath returns, the glasswort releases free-swimming larvae that disperse into the rising sea. The glass-like skeletons remain, and the Tidebound collect them — they are beautiful, fragile, and valued as children's toys and, in their larger forms, as objects of contemplation. There is a shelf proverb: *"Clear as glasswort"* — meaning honest to the point of vulnerability.

**Threadtails** (*masku*) are eel-like fish, eyeless and pale, that live in the flooded crevices of the shelf year-round — in the deep cracks and caverns where water persists even at summer's lowest ebb. They are the permanent residents, the creatures that never leave, and the Tidebound regard them with a mixture of familiarity and superstition. Threadtails are harvested for food from the accessible crevice-pools, but the deeper populations — those living in caves that have not been exposed to air in living memory — are left alone. There are stories, old and persistent, of threadtails growing to enormous size in the deep crevices, and of sounds heard from sealed cave openings during quiet summer nights: a rhythmic tapping, as of something very large and very patient, testing the walls.

## IV. The Rhythm of the Living Shelf

What is most remarkable about the velaam's ecology is not any single organism but the choreography of the whole. The ecosystem does not simply endure the oscillation — it is *composed* by it, the way a piece of music is composed by silence as much as by sound.

The sequence is precise and, to the Tidebound, as legible as a calendar. First the water drops below the velu line and the grey-green rosettes begin to unfurl — Day Zero of summer. Three days later, the rethka begin their transition, and the tolhu emerge crackling from the substrate. By the end of the first week, the velu stalks are rising and the first glasswort polyps are budding in the retreating pools. The Tidebound do not need almanacs. They read the shelf the way a musician reads a score: each organism's entrance is a measure mark, each behavior a dynamic notation.

And the crescendo, the passage that the Tidebound navigate with the greatest care, is the Inbreath's arrival. Two weeks before the water reaches any given elevation, the rethka there go dark. One week before, the tolhu begin migrating upslope in vast, crackling rivers. Three days before, the velu starts to seal. The day before the water arrives, the shelf goes quiet — a silence so thorough, so complete, that the Tidebound have a name for it: the *last breath*. It is the only moment in the entire cycle when the velaam holds nothing living on its surface. Everything has retreated — downward into the sediment, upward to higher ground, inward into sealed capsules. The shelf is, for a few hours, a landscape of pure geology: stone and silt and the smell of salt, waiting.

Then the water comes, and the breathing floor sleeps, and the velu dreams in salt, and the rethka forget themselves, and the vaskrel go on eating the bones of whatever came before.

## V. A Note on the Deep-Shelf Edge

At the outermost margin of the velaam, where the continental shelf begins its long descent into true ocean, there is a zone the Tidebound call the *nehru* — "the threshold." This is the place where the exposed shelf, at summer's lowest ebb, drops away into water that never fully retreats. The nehru is always wet, always liminal, always dangerous.

Here the ecology shifts again. The organisms of the nehru are not oscillation-adapted — they are permanent marine creatures, denizens of the shelf edge that venture onto the briefly exposed ground the way a fox ventures into a clearing: cautiously, briefly, ready to flee. The Tidebound encounter species here that they see nowhere else: large cephalopods that crawl into the shallows at dawn, their chromatophores pulsing with colors that have no apparent communicative function, as though they are dreaming while awake. Eel-colonies — masses of intertwined threadtail relatives, some as thick as a human thigh — that drape themselves over the shelf edge rocks during low exposure, basking in air they cannot breathe, their skin glistening with a mucus that prevents desiccation for hours.

The nehru is where the Tidebound go to be humbled. It is a place that reminds them that the velaam, for all its familiarity, for all their generations of intimate knowledge, is a border. On one side is the world they know — the oscillation, the rhythm, the breathing floor. On the other side is the deep water, where things live that have never surfaced and never will, and where the rules that govern the shelf do not apply. The Tidebound are not a seafaring people in the deep-ocean sense. They are a shelf people, and the nehru is where the shelf ends and the unknown begins.

There are stories — always stories, with the Tidebound, who build their history from narrative as the velu builds from light — of expeditions that went past the nehru, out along the slope, into the deep. Some came back with specimens: luminous things in jars, creatures with too many limbs, fragments of shell that were harder than stone and resonated when struck, like bells. Most came back with nothing but silence and a reluctance to discuss what they had seen.

One expedition, the most famous, did not come back at all. But that is a story for another lens.

---

*The velaam is not a place. It is an event — a six-month performance staged on stone and sediment, with a cast of millions, choreographed by the oldest director there is. The Tidebound do not own it. They attend it. And every year, when the breathing floor opens its grey-green eyes to the sun, they take their seats and watch the same ancient show, knowing every cue, mouthing every line, still somehow surprised when the lights come up.*
