# The Tidebound: World Bible

*A Complete Reference for Creators*

*Tone: Literary science fiction. Ursula K. Le Guin meets Hayao Miyazaki.*

---

## 1. Overview

The ocean breathes with the year. On a world whose severe axial tilt conspires with a broad continental shelf and a pulsing polar ice mass, the sea advances thirty kilometers inland every winter and retreats every summer, exposing a vast, fertile expanse of rock and sediment and ruin. The people who live along this coast — the Tidebound — do not build to last. They build to return to. Their civilization is a masterwork of cyclical engineering: modular architecture raised on ancient anchoring-posts, walking ships that transition between land and sea, farms planted in soil that was seafloor three weeks prior, and an insulating stone produced on the most dangerous edge of the shelf that the highlands cannot survive winter without. Three groups navigate this rhythm — shelf families who descend each summer, highland families who endure each winter, and the Drift-Chosen who refuse to leave the water at all — bound together by a seven-century-old covenant, a shared calendar of loss and recovery, and the unspoken question of who built the singing ruins on the far shelf, and where they went. This is a world where permanence is a fantasy of the inland mind, where the most profound question is not *how do I keep what I have?* but *what will I find when I return?* — and where, in the margins of a seventeen-century tide-ledger, someone notes, every few decades, that the far shelf sang later this year.

## 2. Geography

### The Mechanism

The planet's axial tilt — roughly 35 degrees — cants the northern hemisphere toward its sun for half the year and away for the other half. The Tidebound coast sits at the confluence of two additional geological accidents: a continental shelf so broad and shallow that it extends nearly forty kilometers from the cliff-base before dropping into true ocean, and a polar ice mass that swells and contracts like a breathing lung, pulling water toward it as it grows and releasing it as it shrinks.

The result is not a tide. It is the sea deciding, over the course of weeks, that it would rather be somewhere else. At midsummer, the exposed shelf stretches to the horizon. At midwinter, thirty meters of water sit where summer children played.

The transition takes six weeks in each direction. The summer retreat is the **Outbreath**; the winter advance is the **Inbreath**.

### Barantha — The Exposed Shelf

The shelf — *Barantha*, "the floor that was hidden" — is not flat. It is a landscape as complex as any above permanent sea level, organized into three distinct zones:

**The Nearmarsh** — A band roughly three kilometers wide where freshwater streams meet the receding salt. Black, impossibly rich soil — centuries of marine sediment layered with highland runoff. This is where the shelf-gardens grow: dense tangles of vegetation that sprint from mud to harvest in fourteen weeks. The Nearmarsh squelches underfoot and smells of iron and green.

**The Whaleback** — Ancient sandstone ridges running parallel to the coast, rising from the shelf like the spines of buried animals. The channels between them hold standing seawater pools that evaporate through summer, growing saltier and stranger — crusted with mineral deposits in viridian, rust-orange, and a pale violet called *thursa's blush*. The pools breed brine-flies that feed shelf-birds that feed everything else. The Whaleback is the spine of the summer economy.

**The Farflat** — Beyond the Whaleback, the shelf descends into an eerily level plain of compacted grey-blue clay stretching to the Glint. Too saline for most gardens, too exposed for permanent structures. The Farflat is where the ruins are.

### The Ruins of the Farflat

Geometric formations made of a calcium-ite aggregate that matches no natural deposit. Arranged in clusters of five to nine, repeating at intervals that are not quite regular but not quite random. Each formation is roughly cylindrical, two to four meters tall, with a concave upper surface that collects rainwater. Channels connect formations within a cluster, creating networks of shallow pools at different heights, temperatures, and mineral compositions.

The formations are encrusted with unique life within days of exposure. Transparent crustaceans, slow-moving stone-stars, and soft-bodied worms that build tubes inside the channels and *sing* — water pressure through the tubes producing a sound like someone humming through a comb. At dusk, you can hear the formations singing from half a kilometer away. It is the loneliest sound in the world.

The Tidebound do not build on the Farflat. They harvest from it carefully, ritually. The shelfwalkers who make the three-day journey there are respected and slightly feared.

### The Deep Edge and the Glint

Past the Glint — that razor-line of light where the retreated ocean begins — the continental slope descends with shocking steepness. The Tidebound have no deep-water tradition. Their keelless skimmers cannot survive open ocean swells. The deep is not forbidden; it is simply unreachable.

And yet. Things wash up after storms: fragments of unfamiliar wood, cordage braided in unknown patterns, and objects of worked stone made of the same calcium-ite aggregate as the formations. The Tidebound collect these objects and store them in Thursa's deep chambers, and do not discuss them.

### Three Key Locations

**Thursa** — The largest settlement, built *into* the Copper Stacks: sea-cliffs of oxidized sandstone, striated green and amber, rising forty meters above the winter high-water mark. The city is vertical — streets are stairways, markets are terraced ledges connected by rope-bridges. The deepest chambers house the archives, including the *Breathing Ledger*: a continuously updated document tracking the precise advance and retreat of the sea, measured daily at seventeen marker-stones, spanning seventeen centuries. Thursa smells of copper and cooking-smoke and the ozone tang of sea-cave air.

**Kedderath** — An inland highland town on a granite bluff above the river Sullow's floodplain. Its population triples in winter as shelf families migrate inland. Every building has a **summer face** (modest, for the permanent population) and a **winter face** (opening onto wider interior spaces for three families where one lived before, with shared hearths and long dormitory-halls). There is a saying: *"The house breathes out in summer and in in winter."* It is not a warm saying.

**The Hinge** — Not a settlement but a zone: the strip where the Nearmarsh meets permanent land during the six-week transitions. During the Outbreath, the Hinge is where the sea is *right now* — the most productive and most dangerous place on the coast. Hinge-workers wear broad, flat *vesh*-woven shoes, carry hooked poles, and work in pairs connected by braided rope.

## 3. Ecology

### The Velaam — The Breathing Floor

The Tidebound call the exposed shelf the *velaam* — "breathing floor." It is not one ecosystem but a gradient of dozens, layered by elevation, distance from the shelf edge, substrate type, and particular history. What follows are the organisms that have solved the most extraordinary problem in biology: how to live in a place that ceases to exist, reliably, every year.

### Three Keystone Species

**Velu** (*VEH-loo*) — "The Patient Green"

A colonial organism blurring the line between plant, fungal mat, and something else entirely. Dense carpets of succulent rosettes, each the size of a child's palm, growing in interlocking clusters covering hectares. Grey-green with an iridescent quality, like the inside of an abalone shell. When the shelf emerges, velu is already there: dormant, compressed, crusted with barnacles. Within three days of air exposure it unfolds. Within two weeks, hollow-stemmed stalks rise at a handspan per day — the fastest verified growth rate of any macroscopic organism in the world. The Tidebound do not plant velu. It is always there, waiting. They plant everything else *in* it. The velu mat stabilizes substrate, retains moisture, fixes nitrogen, and creates a microclimate several degrees warmer than ambient air. When the Inbreath comes, the rosettes seal themselves with a resinous coating and enter a metabolic state so reduced they are, by most definitions, not alive — persisting through six months of cold, dark submersion. *"The velu sleeps in salt and dreams in sunlight."*

**Rethka** (*RETH-kah*) — "The Lantern Crab"

A decapod crustacean the size of a human head, with a flattened slate-grey carapace and, in its terrestrial phase, a bioluminescent abdominal organ producing a steady amber glow. The Tidebound harvest the luminous oil — a thick, territorial-marking secretion — without killing the animal. Rethka-oil lamps are the primary light source during shelf expeditions. The rethka possesses a dual respiratory system: feathery gills for winter submersion and vascularized air-chambers for summer. The four-day transition between modes leaves them sluggish and dangerously irritable — the *rethka's confusion*.

Most crucially: rethka begin to dim two full weeks before the first measurable rise of the Inbreath. When the rethka go dark, it is time to begin packing. There is contested evidence that rethka do not retain memory across the respiratory transition — that the summer and winter rethka are, in some neurological sense, different creatures inhabiting the same body.

**Vaskrel** (*VASK-rel*) — "The Marrow Worm"

The unsettling one. A vermiform organism one to two meters long, thick as a human wrist, the color of old bone. Eyeless. No discernible head. Either end can lead. It lives in deep-shelf sediment and feeds on calcium structures — bone, shell, mortar, foundation-stone — dissolving them enzymatically. The first sign of approach is a faint subsidence of the ground, a dimpling, as though the sediment is being gently inhaled from below. Then the smell: sweet, cloying, unmistakable. The vaskrel's calcium appetite has, over centuries, riddled the bases of the deep-shelf ruins with smooth-bored tunnels, slowly dissolving the mortar and stone of the Lost Builders' civilization. The Tidebound stopped burying their dead on the far shelf six generations ago, after discovering that graves were emptied from below with terrible precision.

### The Smaller Lives

**Shelf-skippers** (*tolhu*) — Thumb-sized amphipods emerging by the billions in early Outbreath, producing a soft continuous crackling — "like distant applause." Eaten by everything, including humans. Dried tolhu paste is a dietary staple.

**Glasswort** (*sheru*) — Translucent colonial hydrozoans resembling miniature glass trees, growing in tidal pools. Reliable water-quality indicators: pools with healthy glasswort are safe to drink after boiling. Their fragile glass-like skeletons are collected as children's toys and objects of contemplation. Shelf proverb: *"Clear as glasswort"* — honest to the point of vulnerability.

**Threadtails** (*masku*) — Eyeless, pale, eel-like fish living in the flooded crevices year-round, the permanent residents that never leave. The accessible ones are harvested; the deeper populations are left alone. Stories persist of enormous threadtails in deep caves, and rhythmic tapping heard from sealed openings on quiet summer nights.

### The Rhythm of the Living Shelf

The ecosystem is choreographed by the oscillation like a musical score:

- **Day Zero:** Water drops below the velu line; grey-green rosettes unfurl
- **Day 3:** Rethka begin respiratory transition; tolhu emerge crackling
- **Week 1:** Velu stalks rising; first glasswort polyps budding
- **Weeks 2–14:** Full terrestrial ecosystem operational
- **Inbreath minus 14 days:** Rethka go dark
- **Inbreath minus 7 days:** Tolhu migrate upslope in vast crackling rivers
- **Inbreath minus 3 days:** Velu begins to seal
- **Inbreath minus 1 day:** *The Last Breath* — the shelf goes completely silent. Nothing living remains on the surface. Stone, silt, salt, waiting.

### The Nehru — The Threshold

At the outermost margin, where the shelf drops into permanent deep water, lies the *nehru*. Always wet, always liminal, always dangerous. Here, permanent marine creatures venture briefly onto exposed ground: large cephalopods with chromatophores pulsing in purposeless colors, eel-colonies basking in air they cannot breathe. The nehru is where the Tidebound go to be humbled — where the shelf ends and the unknown begins.

## 4. People & Culture

### The Shape of Time

The Tidebound do not count years. They count *returns*. A person of thirty returns has lived thirty summers on the shelf, thirty winters in the highlands, thirty descents and thirty ascents. Their calendar is quartered but unequal:

| Season | Name | Duration | Character |
|--------|------|----------|-----------|
| The Dry | **Thalwei** | ~5 months | Shelf exposed; building, farming, living |
| The Outbreath | **Suulin** | ~6 weeks | Water retreating; most productive, most dangerous |
| The Deep | **Sovwei** | ~5 months | Shelf submerged; highland survival, confinement |
| The Inbreath | **Karrin** | ~6 weeks | Water advancing; departure, negotiation, urgency |

The transitions — not the seasons themselves — are the most important times. The *changes* are what define Tidebound life.

### Three Peoples, One Rhythm

**The Voshu** (*VOH-shoo*) — "Those Who Go Down" — Shelf families whose identity is rooted in the exposed shelf. Engineers, seasonal farmers, readers of sediment and tide-pool. Wealth measured in *knowledge*, not land: the location of anchoring-posts, productive stretches of shelf-soil, migratory paths, freshwater seeps. Knowledge encoded in *vethoi* — "walking-songs," navigational chants that describe shelf-features rhythmically, learned before reading. A child who knows the vetho *remembers* the shelf before seeing it.

Voshu culture is gregarious, improvisational, profoundly visual. Summer settlements are painted in shelf-mineral pigments — ochres, vivid greens, startling blues. Each return's version must be more striking than the last. They eat communally in open-air kitchens on *haara* platforms.

**The Dorath** (*DOR-ath*) — "Those Who Remain" — Highland families. A different civilization sharing the same language. Where the Voshu are expansive, the Dorath are compressed. Permanent stone buildings — no paint, no ornament, just the grain of the rock and the depth of the wear. Social life organized around the *kem*: a compound of three or four related families sharing buildings around a central hearth, the unit of winter survival. A kem of twenty may hold sixty when the shelf families ascend.

Dorath art is carving — small, intricate objects in stone or bone encoding specific winters, specific negotiations, specific tensions and kindnesses. A family's collection of winter-carvings is their history in a medium that does not wash away. They eat privately, within the kem; cuisine of preservation — smoked, dried, salted, the food of discipline.

**The Tanvelu** (*tan-VEH-loo*) — "The Drift-Chosen" — Perhaps one in twenty Tidebound. Expert navigators, boat-builders, and divers who spend Sovwei on houseboats above the submerged shelf. They dive to tend submerged gardens, maintain anchoring-posts underwater, and report on changes below. They are the only people who experience both seasons in the same place.

The Tanvelu are culturally enormous but unsettling — they live *between*, refusing the fundamental bargain of loss and recovery. Their songs are *veshoi* — "seeing-songs" — describing the shelf as it appears through winter water: shifting, blue-green, refracted. The *veshoi* are considered the most beautiful and most difficult music in Tidebound culture. At the annual negotiations, the Tanvelu hold quiet, lateral power: they need no winter shelter, but they possess information every kem needs.

### The Art of Losing

The most distinctive cultural feature is not what the Tidebound build but how they let go. There is no word for "ruin." The closest — *velsha-kho*, "that which the uncovering will show" — carries no connotation of decay. Tidebound children practice *solha-making*: the deliberate cultivation of tenderness toward things about to be lost. In the final weeks before Karrin, families walk their summer holdings together, naming what they see, then leave — not with sorrow, but with *solha*, the luminous attention of seeing something for the last time *this time*.

Voshu proverb: *"The sea remembers your hands."* What you make with attention, the shelf preserves in some form. Carelessness leaves nothing.

### Music — The Art That Survives

Tidebound music is polyphonic, layered, and *responsorial across time*. A singer performing a vetho at Thalwei's start leaves deliberate gaps. Echo-singers fill them with phrases left incomplete at the previous Thalwei's end. The song is never whole within a single season. It accumulates across returns — the oldest vethoi are palimpsests of hundreds of returns' worth of addition. Performed in full, with all historical layers sung simultaneously, the sound has been described as "a chord made of centuries."

Instruments: a double-reeded pipe from hollow shelf-plant stems; a frame drum with tidal-creature membrane producing sub-audible bass; a small stringed instrument tuned to intervals the Tidebound hear as the natural harmonics of the oscillation — advancing and retreating water translated into pitch.

Dorath music is percussive, minimal — complex polyrhythmic patterns performed in winter kem-chambers, meant to be heard through a wall by someone trying to sleep, finding in the distant tapping a reason not to feel alone.

### Taboos

**Anchoring-post theft** — The offense so serious the language has a specific verb tense for it. Punishment: *kemlessness* — dissolution of the offender's household. No one is obligated to shelter them. The only punishment that is truly permanent in a culture built on cyclical renewal.

**Blocking the Inbreath** — Any structure or action impeding the water's natural advance. The Inbreath is not an enemy but a partner. Historical attempts to build seawalls are remembered with the appalled fascination other cultures reserve for tales of cannibalism.

**Speaking the dead during Sovwei** — The dead descend with the water. To call their names in winter is a cruelty. Names may be spoken freely during Thalwei, when the shelf is exposed and the dead have, in some sense, surfaced. In winter, they sleep. You let them sleep.

### Social Institutions

**Kemshara** (*kem-SHAR-ah*) — "Household-weighing." Annual negotiations on the last days of Karrin determining how many shelf families each highland kem will shelter, on what terms. The most politically important events in Tidebound society.

**Voicekeepers** — Elderly men and women from families with roots in both highland and shelf communities, who remember past kemshara outcomes and hold parties to precedent.

**Reckoners** — Individuals trained in oral histories, shelf-lore, and highland law, traveling between communities as judges, mediators, and record-keepers. Drawn from both highland and shelf families, forbidden from holding property during service. They must be *of the breath itself* — belonging to neither sea's domain nor land's.

## 5. Technology & Architecture

### Philosophy: Recoverability Over Permanence

The Tidebound did not pour their genius into permanence. They poured it into *recoverability*. The question was never "how do we make this endure?" but "how do we make this return?" An enduring structure resists change. A recoverable one collaborates with it.

### The Architecture of Return

**Anchoring-Posts (Torath)** — Columns of *verha* (steelkelp), an extraordinarily dense highland hardwood, driven into shelf bedrock with weighted drop-hammers. The oldest known posts have survived submersion for over four hundred years. Each community maintains a *lattice-map* — painted on cured sharkskin in salt-resistant pigments — showing every post's location. These maps are inherited matrilineally and are among the most valuable objects in Tidebound culture.

**Wishbone Joints** — Interlocking wooden brackets slotting into carved grooves on the posts, locking with a quarter-turn. No nails. No mortar. Six people raise a dwelling frame in three hours; disassembly takes forty minutes.

**Tide-Tempered Panels** — Woven reed-and-seagrass walls sealed with rendered fish oil and ground mussel shell. These panels become stronger with each saltwater cycle — salt crystals forming mineral reinforcement within the fiber matrix. A panel through thirty cycles is harder than one through five. The principle of *tide-tempering* extends to ropes, sails, structural fibers, and clothing.

**The Raise** — The annual reconstruction, beginning days after the Outbreath exposes the posts and continuing two to three weeks, following the retreating waterline outward. Not construction but *tuning* — the skeleton is already there, and the work is in refitting, tightening, adapting to the shelf's changes.

### The Hethka — Ships That Walk

Broad, flat-hulled vessels (~8m × 3m) made of steam-bent steelkelp planks sealed with tidecreep resin. Their defining feature: six articulated outrigger legs mounted in rotating sockets along each side. On dry rock, they walk — crewed by teams operating the legs via internal levers and counterweights. On shallow water, the legs become push-poles. In deeper water, they fold flush and the hethka becomes a flat-bottomed barge moved by oar or sail. The transition between modes is continuous. During the Inbreath, convoys of hethka pick their way across the flooding plain — their legs splashing and folding in rhythm — looking, from a distance, like a migration of vast, purposeful insects.

Children learn hethka-crewing as a dance before they learn to swim. *"Hekka-thol, hekka-thol, three legs down and the water's cold."*

### The Breathing Farms

Agriculture operates under an extreme constraint: five months of growing season on soil that was seafloor three weeks ago. The Tidebound solution is ecological staging:

1. **Tark-grazing** — Hardy, webbed-hoofed domesticated animals are released first to eat the toxic *first-skin* (dead marine mat), trample the anoxic surface, and deposit alkaline waste that neutralizes acidity
2. **Salt-rye** — A fast-growing grain (harvest in 40 days) tolerating residual salinity, pulling salt through its roots, preparing the soil
3. **Shelf-gardens** — Polyculture plots of 20–30 species in concentric rings around seasonal pools, using moisture gradients. Spacing measured in hand-widths with knotted cords specific to each family

Beneath the gardens: **breath-root** networks — clay pipe systems connecting gardens to seasonal pools. As pool levels drop (evaporation), air is drawn through, aerating soil from below. When storms raise pool levels, water pushes through, irrigating from beneath. No moving parts. The system breathes with the weather.

### Salt-Vaults

Stone-lined chambers built into shelf bedrock below the summer water table, filled year-round with still, mineral-saturated water denser than incoming seawater. Tools, seeds, ceremonial objects, and machine parts are sealed in tide-tempered leather pouches and sunk before the Inbreath. A hereditary vault-keeper retrieves and inventories by touch in cold, dark water. Failure rate: ~3% per year. The great fear is not the sea taking objects but losing the knowledge of vault locations.

### Foam-Rock and the Compression Kilns

The technology without parallel. Certain deep-shelf deposits contain *breath-stone* — a calcite riddled with microscopic pores that, when kiln-fired with steam, expands permanently to three times its volume while becoming lighter and stronger. The product — **foam-rock** — floats in freshwater, can be carved with hand tools, is fireproof and rot-proof, and insulates better than any material available to a comparable civilization.

Foam-rock transformed highland winter architecture. Where three hearths were needed, one suffices. Highland deforestation reversed within a generation. But the material is produced on the shelf, from shelf-stone, in shelf-kilns — creating an economic dependency that reversed the ancient highland-shelf power dynamic. The foam-rock kilns operate in the most dangerous zone: the deep-shelf edge, exposed for only eight weeks, worked by a fatalistic subculture of specialists who are, per capita, the wealthiest people in the civilization. They do not display this wealth. The sea would only take it.

### What They Never Built

**Walls** — Fortification is pointless when the sea is the only invader that matters. Conflicts are resolved through economic pressure, social ostracism, and the targeted destruction of lattice-maps (its own legal category of violence).

**Wheels** — The shelf terrain defeats them. Everything moves on hethka, human backs, or tark-pulled sledges.

**Glass** — A transparent, rigid, breakable material has no place in a civilization of disassembly. Windows are oiled panels of tide-tempered membrane, admitting soft, warm light. Visitors describe Tidebound interiors as feeling like the inside of a lantern.

## 6. History & Timeline

### The Resumption Myth

The Tidebound do not have a creation myth. They have a resumption myth. In the oldest tellings, the world did not begin — it *returned*. There was a time called the **Stillwater**, when the sea sat at one level and did not move. Then the world began to breathe. No cause is given. The implication: the Stillwater was the aberration. The breathing is the natural state.

### Timeline

Years are counted from the **Covenant of Rentha** (Year 0). Earlier dates are marked *bR* (before Rentha).

| Period | Event | Significance |
|--------|-------|-------------|
| **~1,200 bR** | **The Shelf Ruins & the Lost Builders** | Two distinct pre-oscillation civilizations revealed: confident Stillwater builders (outer shelf, dry-stacked, flat) and adaptive groove-carvers (inner shelf, mortared, with water-level grooves). Between them, a gap — the *Silence* — where no one built at all. |
| **~600 bR** | **The Scattering** | Shelf-walker bands push farther out each summer, competing for anchoring-posts and rich ground. Expansion without coordination. |
| **~200 bR** | **The Anchoring Wars** | A rolling century of raids, sabotage, and battles over anchoring-post rights. Highland families weaponize winter shelter access. The worst insult in the language is born: *vacha dorim* — "may your pass be closed." |
| **~90–70 bR** | **The Three Long Breaths** | Exceptionally harsh winters — early Inbreath, late Outbreath. Demonstrated with lethal clarity that highland and shelf peoples cannot survive without each other. Broke the willingness to weaponize the seasons. |
| **Year 0** | **The Covenant of Rentha** | Forty-one family heads inscribed three principles on a slab of scavenged *orath* stone: the **Open Breath** (no family may deny highland passage during Inbreath), the **Shared Post** (anchoring-posts must shelter others in emergencies), and the **Neutral Reckoner** (disputes adjudicated by trained, property-less mediators). |
| **~300** | **The Cartography of Oshei** | Shelf-walker Oshei din Taravel maps the far shelf over fifteen summers, discovering it extends 160km — three times the known range. Identifies two distinct ruin-types and the Silence between them. Establishes the **Long Camps**: seasonal settlements so far from the highlands that residents must depart weeks before the Inbreath. Long Campers later discover **deep-keeled ocean-going vessels** preserved in anoxic sediment — suggesting the Lost Builders were sailors who used the shelf as a harbor to somewhere else. |
| **~680** | **The Reckoner Schism** | Reckoner Pallav din Oshe correctly predicts an early Inbreath; Long Camps refuse to evacuate; 43 die. Fury directed at Reckoners for hoarding prediction methods. Eight-year schism resolved by opening archives and installing a democratic network of **shelf-markers** — carved stone cairns letting anyone read the water level. |

### The Five Lessons

Recited at every school term, every Covenant assembly, every Reckoner investiture:

1. *The world breathes, and the breathing is older than we are.* The Stillwater was the sleep. We are the waking.
2. *The Lost Builders fought the breath and drowned.* We live with the breath and survive.
3. *The Anchoring Wars proved that the breath is stronger than hatred.* When we use the seasons as weapons, the seasons win.
4. *The Covenant is not a law but a promise.* The Open Breath, the Shared Post, the neutral Reckoner. Break one and the others fail.
5. *Oshei showed us that we do not yet know our own world.* The shelf is larger, older, and stranger than we think. The only appropriate response is humility and curiosity in equal measure.

## 7. The Calendar

### The Four Quarters of a Return

**SUULIN — The Outbreath** (~6 weeks, late spring)

The sea retreats. The most dangerous and most beautiful time. The waterline drops a hand's breadth per day, then faster. The Hinge-workers are the first out — broad-shoed specialists reading a landscape that changes beneath their feet. Rethka begin their confused, irritable respiratory transition. Tolhu crackle from the substrate by the billions. The velu unfolds. The shelf gardens germinate beneath ankle-deep water. Families descend from the highlands in organized expeditions.

*First day of Suulin:* **Velsha** — The Uncovering. The closest thing to a holy day. No one works. The highland settlements empty as people walk to the emerging shore and stand in the cold shallows, watching the water pull away from stone. Children are held up to see it. Old people are carried. There is no ceremony, no priest. They simply watch. The silence is the ritual.

**THALWEI — The Dry** (~5 months, summer)

The shelf is fully exposed. The Raise reconstructs the summer settlements. Tark graze the first-skin. Salt-rye grows. Shelf-gardens are planted. The deep-shelf kiln operators fire compression kilns on the most exposed edge. Hethka convoys transport foam-rock, salt, mineral pigments, and shell-goods coastward. The Whaleback pools crystallize in impossible colors. The Long Camps push to the far shelf, mapping, harvesting, watching the formations. The Farflat sings at dusk. Music fills the open-air kitchens. Debts are settled. Marriages are arranged. Children walk the shelf for the first time, recognizing it from walking-songs they learned in winter.

The dead may be spoken of freely. They have surfaced with the shelf.

**KARRIN — The Inbreath** (~6 weeks, autumn)

The sea returns. Two weeks before the water reaches any elevation, the rethka there go dark. One week before, the tolhu migrate upslope in vast crackling rivers. Three days before, the velu begins to seal. The day before: *the last breath* — total silence on the shelf surface.

Families load hethka and begin the ascent. Convoys pick their way across the flooding plain. The kemshara negotiations commence — the political heart of the year, determining who shelters whom and on what terms. Everything that cannot be carried is sealed in salt-vaults or abandoned.

*Final night before full submersion:* **Kashren** — The Surrendering. Loud, reckless joy on the highest *haara* platforms, with fires and music. Couples announce intentions to marry. Debts are forgiven or formally carried into winter — a public declaration of their seriousness. The ground beneath the revelers will be five meters underwater by the following week.

**SOVWEI — The Deep** (~5 months, winter)

The shelf is submerged. The population compresses into the highlands. Kem life dominates: managed scarcity, careful rationing, social friction in rooms too small and too warm. The Dorath carvers work in stone and bone, encoding each winter. The Tanvelu remain on the water above, tending the submerged shelf, diving to maintain anchoring-posts, reporting on changes.

The dead are beneath the water. You do not speak their names.

The Reckoners travel between communities. The Voicekeepers preside over disputes. Children learn the walking-songs for a shelf they cannot yet see. The highland-shelf tension is at its sharpest: three families where one lived before, highland discipline meeting shelf improvisation, everyone waiting for the same thing.

Then the water begins, imperceptibly, to recede.

## 8. Story Seeds

### 1. The Late Singing

A young Reckoner notices that the Farflat formations have been singing later each year — the pattern in the Breathing Ledger's margins that no one has formally investigated. She mounts an expedition to the farthest formations during a long, low summer, and discovers that the vaskrel are not merely eating the ruins — they are eating the *channels* through which the formations sing. The singing is failing. And if the formations are infrastructure, if they serve a function no one currently understands, their silence may herald something the Breathing Ledger's seventeen centuries of stable data cannot predict.

### 2. The Kiln-Workers' Strike

The deep-shelf kiln operators — the fatalistic subculture that produces foam-rock in an eight-week window on the most dangerous edge of the shelf — refuse to fire the kilns unless the highland families renegotiate kemshara terms. The highlands face winter without insulation. The shelf families face political fracture. The Tanvelu, who owe no kem-obligations, hold the swing vote — and their price is access to the deep-shelf ruins, which the kiln operators have always guarded as their territory. A story about labor, leverage, and the terrifying fragility of systems everyone assumed were permanent.

### 3. The Vault-Keeper's Daughter

A vault-keeper dies mid-Sovwei — the only person who knew, by touch, the precise location and contents of her family's salt-vaults. Her daughter, who rejected the shelf life for a highland marriage, must learn to dive in winter, work by feel in freezing dark water, and recover the family's irreplaceable seed stocks before the Outbreath, when other families will claim unrecovered vaults as abandoned. A story about inheritance, about what we carry and what we leave behind, about the difference between choosing to lose something and having it taken.

### 4. The Ship in the Sediment

A Long Camper discovers, during an exceptionally low summer, a nearly intact ocean-going vessel in the anoxic sediment of the outermost shelf — its cargo hold containing sealed containers of worked calcium-ite objects, star charts painted on an unknown material, and the skeletal remains of crew members whose bone structure does not match any known Tidebound population. The Reckoners want to study it. The shelf families want to claim it. The Tanvelu insist they have seen similar shapes beneath their houseboats for generations and never spoke of them. The discovery forces the question the Tidebound have always refused to ask: who left, and why, and did they mean to come back?

### 5. The Closed Pass

Two highland kem-lords, in defiance of the Covenant's Open Breath, blockade a mountain pass during the Inbreath to pressure a shelf family into surrendering their foam-rock kiln rights. The shelf family is trapped on the lowlands as the water rises. A Tanvelu flotilla offers shelter on the water — but accepting would mean the shelf family owes the Drift-Chosen a debt that has no kemshara precedent. A Reckoner races to invoke Covenant law while the sea does not wait for politics. A story about the worst thing you can do in Tidebound culture, and why the worst thing is still, after seven centuries, possible.

## 9. Sensory Palette

### Sight

**Summer/Thalwei:** Vast horizontal expanse — the shelf stretching to the Glint, that razor-line of light at the ocean's edge. Mineral pigments on settlement walls: ochre, copper-green, crusite-blue. Whaleback pools in impossible colors — viridian, rust-orange, thursa's-blush violet. Velu mats shimmering grey-green with abalone iridescence. Rethka glowing amber at dusk. Hethka convoys silhouetted against the Glint, legs moving in rhythm. The Farflat formations like clusters of stone thimbles at the horizon.

**Winter/Sovwei:** Vertical world — the Copper Stacks striated green and amber above grey-green winter sea. Rope-bridges swaying in salt wind. Lamplight through tide-tempered membrane windows: the interior of a lantern, soft and warm. The sea appears to grow trees where the lowland forest canopy breaks the surface. The Tanvelu houseboats in floating compounds, lanterns reflected in dark water.

**Transition:** The Hinge — water pulling away from stone in slow motion, everything glistening. Or advancing: hethka convoys threading across a flooding plain, legs splashing, the waterline a living boundary creeping upslope.

### Sound

The crackling of tolhu at Outbreath — soft, continuous, distant applause. The singing of the Farflat formations at dusk — water pressure through worm-tubes, like humming through a comb, the loneliest sound in the world. Vethoi sung in open air, with deliberate gaps that will not be filled until next return. Dorath percussion heard through kem walls, complex rhythms meant to keep a sleeper company. Hethka legs splashing and creaking. The frame drum felt more than heard. The silence on Velsha morning when an entire civilization watches the water retreat. The silence of the last breath before the Inbreath arrives.

### Smell

The Nearmarsh: iron and green, turned earth and salt-rot and bloom. Thursa: copper, cooking-smoke, and ozone channeled through sea-caves. Tidecreep resin: faintly of anise. Tide-tempered fabric: salt and grit. Rethka oil: warm, oily, amber. Drying vesh-kelp: leather and brine. Hydrogen sulfide from anoxic first-skin: a sharp warning. Vaskrel-breath: sweet, cloying, unmistakable once known. Highland winter: woodsmoke, stone dust, dried fish, the close scent of too many people in too small a space.

### Taste

Shelf-garden vegetables with flavors that do not exist on permanent land — salt-sweetness, mineral undertones. The fermented tidal-pool paste: an acquired taste of considerable power. Salt-rye flatbread: tough, bitter, honest. Dried tolhu paste: salty, umami, ground. Highland preserved food: smoked, dried, salted, the cuisine of discipline. Fresh water from glasswort-tested pools: clean with a mineral edge.

### Touch

Tide-tempered fabric: stiff, slightly gritty, faintly iridescent. The wishbone joints locking with a quarter-turn, clicking into carved grooves — the feel of things fitting. Vesh-woven hinge-shoes: broad, flat, the sensation of distributed weight on uncertain ground. Cold vault-water on hands, groping by touch for knotted leather pouches. The shelf underfoot: rock, then sediment, then clay, then the soft dimpling over a vaskrel below. Velu rosettes unfurling under fingertips — a fist becoming a palm.

*This document is a living reference. The world breathes, and so should its record. What is written here is the shelf as we know it — the data of seventeen centuries, the songs of a hundred generations, the anchoring-posts driven deep enough to survive submersion. What is not written is what the Outbreath will show next. The formations sing later each year. The vaskrel eat deeper. The ocean-going ships in the anoxic sediment wait for someone to ask where they were going.*

*The Tidebound would say: the mystery is the best part. It is the singing of the formations at dusk, the loneliest sound in the world. And the world needs it to stay lonely a little longer.*
