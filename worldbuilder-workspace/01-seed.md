# The Tidebound — Foundational Document

## I. The Shape of the World

The ocean breathes with the year.

It is not a tide in any sense the word usually carries — not the pull of a moon, not a twice-daily rhythm one learns in childhood and forgets. It is slower than that, and vaster. Beginning in late autumn, the sea rises. Not catastrophically, not as a wall of water, but with the patience of something that has done this forever. A hand's breadth per day, then a forearm's width, then faster — a slow, inexorable inhalation that swallows beaches, then meadows, then the lower forests. By the depth of winter, the waterline has advanced as much as thirty kilometers inland from its summer minimum. The continental shelf, which slopes at a gentle gradient along this coast, becomes a shallow sea — warm at the surface where the current flows, cold and silted beneath.

Then, beginning in late spring, the exhalation. The water retreats. It pulls back like a sheet drawn from a bed, slowly at first, then with gathering momentum through the long summer months. By midsummer the shelf is exposed: a vast, glistening expanse of rock and sediment and ruin, stretching to the horizon, threaded with tidal pools that are really seasonal lakes, and everywhere the smell of salt and wet stone and the particular green scent of things that grow only when the light returns to ground that was, for half the year, seafloor.

**Why does this happen?** The mechanism is atmospheric and oceanic, not magical. This world's axial tilt is more severe than Earth's — roughly 35 degrees — and the continent on which the Tidebound live sits at a latitude where this tilt conspires with a dominant warm current to produce an extraordinary seasonal oscillation. In winter, prevailing winds and the thermal expansion of a vast equatorial current system push water toward the coast. The continental shelf here is unusually broad and shallow — hundreds of kilometers of gently sloping seabed — which means that even modest changes in sea level translate into enormous horizontal shifts in the waterline. In summer, the current retreats, the winds reverse, and the water draws back, exposing the shelf like a theater curtain opening on an act that lasts only half the year.

The transition periods — which the Tidebound call the Inbreath and the Outbreath — last roughly six weeks each. During the Inbreath, the pace of advance is relentless but navigable. Communities have weeks of warning. During the Outbreath, the retreat is slightly faster, and the newly exposed ground is dangerous: unstable sediment, stranded creatures, pockets of anoxic water that can kill a person who wades into them carelessly. The Outbreath is a time of both euphoria and caution. The land returns, but it returns wild.

## II. The Emotional Core

What does it feel like to live this way?

Imagine knowing, with absolute certainty, that the place where you are standing will not exist in four months. Not destroyed — submerged. The stones of the foundation will remain. The old anchoring-posts, driven deep into bedrock generations ago, will stand like the masts of sunken ships beneath twelve meters of green water. And then, half a year later, they will emerge again, draped in kelp and barnacles, and you will return to the same stones, scrape them clean, and rebuild.

The Tidebound do not experience this as tragedy. They experience it as rhythm — as fundamental and unremarkable as breathing, as the alternation of sleep and waking. But it has shaped them profoundly. Their relationship to permanence is different from ours. They do not build to last. They build to return to. A house is not a possession but a partnership with a place — something you assemble in the Outbreath and relinquish in the Inbreath, knowing the sea will scour it, knowing you will come back and find the bones of it and raise it again. The oldest families have been rebuilding on the same anchoring-posts for centuries. The posts endure. Everything else is negotiation.

This gives their culture an emotional texture that is difficult to translate. There is a word in their language — *solha* — that means something like "the tenderness you feel toward a thing you are about to lose and will certainly recover." It is not grief. It is not nostalgia. It is closer to the feeling of watching a child fall asleep: the knowledge that this particular version of consciousness is ending, and that another will begin, and that both are precious, and that the transition between them is holy.

They are not a melancholy people. They are, if anything, intensely present. When you know the meadow will be ocean by winter, you look at the meadow. You learn its grasses. You harvest what it offers. You do not defer joy because you understand — in a way that most human cultures only pretend to understand — that deferral is the only true loss.

But there is a shadow. The rhythm teaches patience and acceptance, but it also teaches a certain fatalism. The Tidebound are not great innovators. They perfect rather than invent. They have built exquisite, lightweight structures that can be disassembled in days and reassembled in hours, using joint systems of extraordinary sophistication. Their rope-making and textile work is unmatched. Their understanding of marine biology is centuries ahead of any comparable civilization. But they have not developed the restless, accumulative drive that on other continents produced industrialization, imperial expansion, or systematic science. Why would they? The sea takes everything back. The lesson the world teaches, every year, is: *do not accumulate. Adapt.*

## III. The Central Tension

The tension is between **the shelf and the highlands.**

When the sea retreats, the exposed shelf is astonishingly fertile. Centuries of marine sediment have created soil of extraordinary richness. The seasonal lakes teem with fish that have adapted to the oscillation — species that breed in the shallows during summer and migrate to deeper water as the Inbreath begins. The ruins on the shelf — old foundations, old anchoring-posts, old roads — create a lattice of structure that the Tidebound use as the skeleton of their summer civilization. For six months of the year, the shelf is the center of their world: the place where they farm, fish, build, trade, court, argue, and govern.

But the highlands — the permanently dry land above the winter waterline — are where they survive. The highlands are rocky, forested, steep. The soil is thin. The growing season is adequate but not generous. The highlands are where the Tidebound retreat when the Inbreath comes, carrying with them what they can, leaving behind what they cannot. The highlands are safety, but they are also confinement. The population density in winter is three or four times what it is in summer, and the resources are a fraction. Winter is a time of closeness, of rationing, of social friction, of politics conducted in rooms that are too small and too warm.

This creates the fundamental condition from which all Tidebound stories emerge: **the people who control the highlands control survival, but the people who master the shelf control wealth.** These are not always the same people. The highland families — old, entrenched, their territories marked by ancient boundary stones that the sea never reaches — hold power in winter. They control the granaries, the timber, the freshwater springs. But in summer, the shelf families — navigators, engineers, those who know where the best anchoring-posts are, who can read the sediment and predict where the seasonal lakes will form — these families hold the real wealth of the civilization.

The two groups need each other absolutely and resent each other perpetually. Highland families marry into shelf families and vice versa, but the cultural divide is deep. Highlanders think of shelf people as reckless, impermanent, too in love with the temporary. Shelf people think of highlanders as hoarders, conservatives, people who mistake endurance for virtue. Both are right. The tension between them is the engine of Tidebound politics, art, philosophy, and — when it breaks down — violence.

## IV. The Shelf Itself

A note on the exposed shelf, because it is the most extraordinary feature of this world and the one that most demands the reader's imagination.

When the water pulls back, it reveals a landscape that is neither land nor sea but something else — a liminal terrain that has been shaped by both forces and belongs fully to neither. The rock is layered and carved by centuries of submersion and exposure, creating formations of eerie beauty: arches, pillars, shallow canyons, flat platforms of stone that the Tidebound call *haara* — "tables" — which they use as foundations for their summer markets and workshops.

Among the rock, things grow. Not seaweed — or rather, not only seaweed. There are plants that have evolved to exploit the six-month window of air and light: thick-stemmed, fast-growing, with root systems that grip the stone and survive months of submersion in a state of dormancy. The Tidebound cultivate these plants with great care, and their agriculture on the shelf is a marvel of adapted practice — planting that begins the moment the ground is exposed, harvesting that must be complete before the Inbreath.

And there are animals. Creatures that live in the boundary — amphibious, or seasonally migratory, or adapted to survive in the strange interstitial pools that persist year-round in the deepest crevices of the shelf. Some of these creatures are large. Some are dangerous. The shelf in early Outbreath is not a safe place, and the first weeks of exposure are a time of organized expeditions, not casual wandering.

The ruins are the oldest mystery. Deep on the shelf, farther out than living memory extends, there are structures that do not match any known Tidebound architecture. Stone walls too massive to be seasonal. Foundations sunk into bedrock with a precision the Tidebound cannot replicate. These ruins predate the current civilization by an unknown span, and their existence raises a question that the Tidebound have never satisfactorily answered: *who built on the shelf as though the sea would never return?*

## V. Open Questions for the Worldbuilders

These questions are left deliberately unresolved. Each parallel worldbuilder should answer them independently, and the divergence in their answers will create the richness of the final world.

**1. The Deep Shelf Ruins — Who built them, and what happened to them?**
Were they a civilization that existed before the oscillation began? Did the oscillation itself destroy them? Or did they understand the shelf in a way the current Tidebound do not — and were they destroyed by something else entirely? The ruins are a canvas. Paint on them what you will.

**2. What is the Tidebound relationship to the open ocean?**
The shelf is their domain, but what lies beyond the shelf edge, where the continental slope drops into deep water? Are the Tidebound sailors? Do they fear the deep sea, or exploit it? Is there contact with other civilizations across the water, and if so, what form does it take? The shelf is a threshold — what is on the other side?

**3. How do the Tidebound govern themselves?**
The highland-shelf tension implies competing power structures, but what form do these take? Is there a centralized authority, or a patchwork of local arrangements? How are disputes between highland and shelf families resolved? What role, if any, do the transition seasons play in governance — are the Inbreath and Outbreath political events as well as natural ones?

**4. What do the Tidebound believe about death?**
A civilization built on cyclical submersion and renewal must have a distinctive relationship to mortality. Do they see death as a final Inbreath? Do they practice burial, or do they return their dead to the sea? Is there a concept of afterlife, and if so, does it mirror the rhythm of the world — a great Outbreath in which everything that was submerged is finally, permanently exposed?

**5. What is happening at the margins — the edges of the oscillation zone?**
Not every community lives at the center of the rhythm. Some must live at the extremes — where the sea barely reaches in winter, or where the shelf barely emerges in summer. What are these marginal communities like? What do they trade, what do they fear, and how do they see the "core" Tidebound who live in the full sweep of the cycle?

---

*This document is the seed. It is not the world — it is the conditions under which the world will grow. The five worldbuilders who receive it should treat it as a foundation, not a constraint: a set of truths from which to extrapolate, not a set of rules to obey. The best worldbuilding happens when the builders disagree about implications and converge on feeling.*

*The feeling is this: a world where loss is not a disaster but a season, and where the most profound human question is not "how do I keep what I have?" but "what will I find when I return?"*
