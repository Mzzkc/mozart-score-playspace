# The Shape of Things

*A Geography of the Tidebound Coast*

---

## The Mechanism

The world tilts.

Not metaphorically — though the Tidebound have a hundred metaphors for it — but literally, the way a drunk tilts in a chair, slow enough that you forget it's happening until the floor is somewhere else entirely. The planet's axial tilt, severe by the standards of more temperate worlds, cants the northern hemisphere toward its sun for half the year and away for the other half. This alone would produce seasons. But the Tidebound coast sits at the confluence of two additional accidents of geology: a continental shelf so broad and shallow that it extends nearly forty kilometers from the cliff-base before dropping into true ocean, and a polar ice mass that swells and contracts like a breathing lung, pulling water toward it as it grows and releasing it as it shrinks.

The result is not a tide. The Tidebound know tides — small, daily, ordinary things, the heartbeat of the sea. This is something else. This is the sea deciding, over the course of weeks, that it would rather be somewhere else.

At midsummer, the exposed shelf stretches to the horizon. Standing on the Copper Stacks above Thursa, you can see all the way to the Glint — that razor-line of light where the retreated ocean begins, impossibly far away, shimmering like a second sky laid flat. The air smells of salt-rot and bloom, of the vast gardens already pushing through the mud, of the kelp-forests collapsing into horizontal fans that dry into something the Tidebound call *vesh* — a material halfway between leather and paper that they use for everything from roofing to record-keeping.

At midwinter, the same vantage point shows only water. Grey-green, heavy, lapping at the base of the Stacks themselves, swallowing the lowland forests to their canopy-tips so that the winter sea appears to grow trees. The shelf is gone. The gardens are gone. Thirty meters of water sit where summer children played.

The transition takes six weeks in each direction. The Tidebound call the summer retreat the Outbreath and the winter advance the Inbreath, and these are the most dangerous and most beautiful times.

## The Shelf: Barantha

The exposed shelf has a name — *Barantha*, which means roughly "the floor that was hidden." It is not flat. This is the first thing outsiders misunderstand, imagining a vast mudflat, featureless and grey. Barantha is a landscape as complex as any above permanent sea level, sculpted by currents that have run across it for millennia.

The innermost zone, closest to the permanent coast, is the **Nearmarsh** — a band perhaps three kilometers wide where freshwater streams from the highlands meet the receding salt. This is where the shelf-gardens grow: dense, furious tangles of vegetation that sprint from mud to harvest in the fourteen weeks of full exposure. The soil here is black and impossibly rich, centuries of marine sediment layered with highland runoff. The Nearmarsh squelches underfoot. It smells of iron and green. Every surface is alive with things that have been waiting.

Beyond the Nearmarsh, the shelf rises — *rises* — into a series of long, low ridges called the **Whaleback**, ancient sandstone formations that the retreating sea exposes like the spines of buried animals. The Whalebacks run roughly parallel to the coast, and the channels between them hold standing pools of seawater that evaporate slowly through the summer, growing saltier and stranger by the week. By late summer, the Whaleback pools are crusted with mineral deposits in colours that have no business existing in nature: viridian, rust-orange, a pale violet that the Tidebound call *thursa's blush* after the city that overlooks them. The pools breed brine-flies in such density that the air above them hums, and the brine-flies feed the darting shelf-birds, and the shelf-birds feed everything else. The Whaleback is the spine of the summer economy.

Beyond the Whaleback, the shelf descends into the **Farflat** — and here the name is more honest. The Farflat is genuinely, eerily level, a plain of compacted grey-blue clay that stretches to the Glint. It is too saline for most shelf-gardens, too exposed for permanent structures, and too far from the coast for easy transport. The Farflat is where the ruins are.

## The Ruins of the Farflat

No one built them. This is the Tidebound consensus, held with the particular firmness of people who have considered the alternative and found it unbearable.

The formations — because *formations* is the safe word — are geometric. They are made of a calcium-ite aggregate that does not match any natural deposit on the shelf or the mainland. They are arranged in clusters of five to nine, and the clusters repeat at intervals that are not quite regular but not quite random, like the spacing of trees in an orchard planted by someone whose measuring-rope was fraying.

Each formation is roughly cylindrical, between two and four meters tall, with a concave upper surface that collects rainwater during the summer exposure. Some have channels carved — *eroded*, the Tidebound insist, *eroded* — into their sides, and these channels connect formations within a cluster so that water flows between them. The purpose of this is unclear. The effect is that in late summer, after weeks of sun, each cluster holds a network of shallow pools at different heights, connected by narrow streams, and the water in each pool is a slightly different temperature and mineral composition.

The shelf-creatures use them. That much is certain. The formations are encrusted with life within days of exposure, and the connected pools become nurseries for species that exist nowhere else — transparent crustaceans the length of a finger, slow-moving radial animals that the Tidebound call *stone-stars*, and a particular kind of soft-bodied worm that builds tubes of cemented shell-fragments inside the channels and sings. (The singing is produced by water pressure through the tubes. It sounds like someone humming through a comb. At dusk, when the Farflat is quiet, you can hear the formations singing from half a kilometer away, and it is the loneliest sound in the world.)

The Tidebound do not build on the Farflat. They harvest from it — carefully, ritually, with the kind of caution that suggests a prohibition worn smooth into custom. The *shelfwalkers* who make the three-day journey to the formations and back are respected and slightly feared, the way you might respect someone who swims with sharks and comes back thoughtful rather than triumphant.

## Three Places

### Thursa

The largest permanent settlement, built not *on* the coast but *into* it. The Copper Stacks — sea-cliffs of oxidized sandstone, striated green and amber — rise forty meters above the winter high-water mark, and Thursa occupies the caves, ledges, overhangs, and carved-out chambers within them. The city is vertical. Streets are stairways. Markets are terraced ledges connected by rope-bridges that sway in the onshore wind. The deepest chambers, carved generations ago into the heart of the Stacks, stay cool in summer and warm in winter, and these are where the city's records are kept: shelf-maps inscribed on *vesh*, tide-tables going back centuries, and the *Breathing Ledger* — the continuously updated document that tracks the precise advance and retreat of the sea, measured daily at seventeen marker-stones along the coast.

Thursa smells of copper and cooking-smoke and the particular ozone tang of air channeled through sea-caves. Its people are shelf-family by heritage, and they speak with an accent that clips vowels short, as though language itself should not waste time.

### Kedderath

Inland, up the river Sullow, where the winter floods reach their farthest extent. Kedderath sits on a granite bluff above the floodplain — a highland town that exists because the Tidebound need somewhere to *go*. In winter, the population triples as shelf-families migrate inland with their dried stores, their *vesh*-wrapped tools, their children who have never seen snow. Kedderath's permanent residents — highland-family, stone-workers and timber-cutters and upland shepherds — receive them with a hospitality that is also a transaction. Winter shelter is not free. It is paid for in summer goods: salt, dried shelf-fish, mineral pigments from the Whaleback pools, and the small, luminous pearls that form inside the shells of Farflat stone-stars.

The architecture of Kedderath reflects its dual nature. Every building has a **summer face** and a **winter face**. The summer face is the entrance that serves the smaller, permanent population — modest, functional, oriented toward the river and the upland roads. The winter face opens onto the wider interior spaces designed for three families where one lived before, with shared hearths and long dormitory-halls and the particular, exhausting intimacy of people who did not choose each other but cannot survive apart.

There is a saying in Kedderath: *The house breathes out in summer and in in winter.* It is not a warm saying.

### The Hinge

Not a settlement but a zone — the strip of coast where the Nearmarsh meets the permanent land during the six weeks of transition. During the Outbreath, the Hinge is where the sea is *right now*, retreating but still present, and it is the most productive and most dangerous place on the coast. The water is shallow and warming. Creatures that have spent the winter in the deep are suddenly accessible. The shelf-gardens are germinating beneath ankle-deep water that recedes centimeters per day. And the footing is treacherous — soft mud over harder clay, with sinkholes where trapped water has undermined the surface, and sudden channels where the retreating current still runs fast enough to knock a grown person sideways.

The Hinge-workers are specialists. They wear wide, flat shoes woven from *vesh* that distribute their weight across the mud — *hinge-shoes*, broad as dinner plates, laced to the calf. They carry hooked poles for testing the ground ahead and for hauling themselves out of sinkholes. They work in pairs, always, connected by a braided rope at the waist, so that if one goes down the other can pull. The Hinge during Outbreath is where the Tidebound are at their most visibly adapted — not farmers, not fishers, but something between, reading a landscape that is changing beneath their feet even as they work it.

## The Deep Edge and the Question of Beyond

Past the Glint, where the shelf drops away, the ocean is abruptly, shockingly deep. The continental slope descends at a grade steep enough that the Tidebound, who are not afraid of the sea, are afraid of *that*. Their boats — the keelless skimmers, hulls waxed with tidecreep resin, designed for the shallow shelf-waters — cannot survive the open ocean swells. They have no deep-water tradition. The ocean beyond the Glint is not forbidden; it is simply unreachable, the way the moon is unreachable, a place that exists but does not concern you.

And yet.

Things wash up. After storms, the Farflat's outer edge collects debris that did not come from the shelf: fragments of wood that is not any species the Tidebound cultivate, lengths of cordage braided in unfamiliar patterns, and occasionally — rarely, but persistently — objects of worked stone that are *not* the Farflat formations. These are smaller, portable, clearly shaped by hands, and they are made of the same calcium-ite aggregate.

The Tidebound do not discuss this. They collect the objects — the shelfwalkers bring them back with the same careful, uneasy reverence they show the formations — and they store them in Thursa's deep chambers, and they do not discuss them. There is no prohibition against discussion. There is simply a silence, the way there is silence around a question whose answer might require you to rebuild everything you believe.

## The Breathing Land

What makes the Tidebound coast extraordinary is not any single feature but the *rhythm* — the way the entire landscape participates in the annual cycle. In summer, the coast is a wide, generous, teeming expanse of exposed life. In winter, it is a narrow, vertical, huddled strip of stone above cold water. The same person stands on the same cliff and sees two completely different worlds, separated not by distance but by time.

The geological process is indifferent. The ice grows, the water shifts, the shelf emerges or submerges, and it has done so for longer than anyone can count. The Tidebound did not create this rhythm. They submitted to it, and in submitting, they became something that could not exist anywhere else: a people whose deepest understanding of reality is that the ground beneath your feet is borrowed. That permanence is a fantasy of the inland mind. That the only honest relationship with a place is one that includes the knowledge that the place will, reliably and without malice, ask you to leave.

And then, just as reliably, invite you back.

The Breathing Ledger in Thursa records seventeen centuries of this oscillation. The earliest entries are scratched into stone tablets. The most recent are inked on *vesh* in a precise, economical hand. The measurements have never varied by more than a few days. The sea has never failed to retreat. The sea has never failed to return.

This is not faith. The Tidebound do not have faith in the oscillation. They have *data*.

But in the margins of the Breathing Ledger — in the spaces between measurements, where generations of keepers have occasionally allowed themselves a personal note — there is a recurring entry, appearing every few decades, always in a different hand:

*The Farflat sang later this year.*

No one has ever recorded it singing earlier.
